\section*{\centering Abstract}

Historical musical instruments present a complex set of challenges within the
heritage sector, as they span across many categories of value systems in heritage
conservation. The research in this thesis was carried out as part of the
NEMUS project, which seeks to address these challenges presented by musical
instrument heritage objects through the application of digital technologies, and
discusses how we might confront challenge combining through physical
authenticity, digital innovation, and sustainable conservation practices. Two
challenges of digital musical instrument heritage conservation are explored:
interaction with digitised instruments and analysis for understanding material
properties. The sound of musical instruments can be digitally represented
through multiple means, audio samples, synthesis approximations or physical
modelling synthesis. With respect to the harpsichord, regardless of the approach
taken, standard MIDI-based controllers lack the resolution to capture the fine
temporal detail required for performance. This challenge is explored through the
creation a two-register haptic interface constructed from a custom-built,
historically informed harpsichord keyboard. The interface contains the same
mechanism and kinaesthetic response of an original instrument with the
capability of communicating with digitised instruments through the MIDI protocol.
The interface's design principles, prototyping and fabrication process are
discussed alongside the open methodology which informed them, culminating in an
exhibition at Museo San Colombano, Bologna. Complementing the interface project
is an open-source finite difference simulation framework for plate vibrations
featuring adjustable elastic boundary conditions, \textsc{magpie}, which
addresses the second challenge of material analysis. The design and development
of \textsc{magpie} are examined with attention paid to its cross-language
implementation in M\textsc{atlab}, Python and C\textsc{++}. \textsc{magpie}
aims to democratizes access to acoustic modelling, benefiting researchers,
educators, and instrument builders by visualizing mode shapes and frequencies,
facilitating experimental exploration of the plate systems associated with
string instrument soundboards. Encompassing both projects is an open
methodology, the methods needed to ensure that research adheres to the FAIR
principles. Digital technologies can support the conservation, analysis and
recreation of historical musical instruments. With this technology, comes the
challenges of sustaining digital artefacts, reproducibility, software citation
and sustainable data practices. These challenges are discussed through case
studies of musical acoustics research software over the past 30 years,
illustrating institutional responsibilities for maintaining open, citable, and
reproducible digital research. The future directions for each project are
examined, with prospective developments and technical challenges
contextualised with the broader open methodology.
