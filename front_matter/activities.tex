\section*{PhD Activities}

This PhD thesis forms part of the research output of the NEMUS project, which focuses on the digital restoration of ancient musical instruments through physical modelling techniques. Part of the remit of NEMUS is the creation of digital musical instrument interfaces to numerical simulations of historical instruments. These interfaces aim to incorporate the tactile-kinaesthetic (`haptic') feedback provided by the physical instrument that is being simulated. This work focussed on developing of such an interface, particularly a XVII century two-register harpsichord in the Italian style. NEMUS's dedication to open source its research output likewise directed the creation of the interface using entirely open source hardware and open source software. As part of this, research tools with use beyond the scope of NEMUS were identified for development and distribution under open source licenses. One tool was chosen for development for its potential use within musical instrument conservation and was developed further using an open source methodology

The first year focused on gathering and studying relevant literature, in particular those focussed on the creation of haptic interfaces. Further to literature review, research and prototyping tests were carried out for the creation of the interface. A small scale three-key mechanical model was purchased, on which a selection of sensors were test in order to identify a suitable candidate for the final interface. As part of the process,  two institutes were identified for outside collaboration and support. The first was San Colombano in Bologna for extensive harpsichord collection and expertise of staff within the subject of historical Italian harpsichords. The second institute was Imperial College London for its prolific research output in the field of digital musical instrument interfaces. After visits to both institutes, support was brokered between the curator of San Colombano, Catalina Vicens, and Professor Andrew McPherson Chair in Design Engineering and Music at Imperial College London. A mechanical interface was commissioned with the luthier Roberto Livi, which was designed and created concurrently with the optical sensor system. 

In the second year,  design and fabrication of the optical sensor system for the interface was carried out. This involved the fabrication of printed circuit boards which were ordered with PCBWay. Given lead times, many different designs were ordered simultaneously, each assembled and tested with the most viable designs iterated upon for the next version. Part of this process require the furnishing of the NEMUS laboratory with appropriate equipment for PCB assembly and testing. Digital fabrication facilities for 3D printed brackets and rigs were researched and found within the Alma Labor facility at Unibo, managed by Massimiliano Fraulini. The first full interface was delivered in the June of the second year and all components were assembled and installed within the October. Installation required multiple iterations on firmware as well as the creation of documentation for remote support and further refinement. This first model was installed for exhibition in San Colombano the following year.

Concurrent to the interface project, development was carried out what would become known as the \textsc{magpie} framework, an open source tool for thin plate modelling and simulation using elastic boundary constants. The framework was authored in both M\textsc{atlab} and the Python programming languages. The python version was published to the Python Package Index May 2024 for demonstration at the  \href{https://cism.it/en/activities/courses/C2404/}{Physics Of Musical Instruments Applied To Instrument Making} advanced course at the International Centre for Mechanical Sciences in Udine. A developed version of the framework with a graphical was accepted at the conference Internoise 2024.

From June 2024 a year-long visit abroad to the University of Edinburgh was started as part of the Una Europa Cultural Heritage doctorate. The Una Europa co-supervisor chosen was the curator of Dr. Jenny Nex 
who provided guidance and support within research of digital cultural heritage and musical instrument restoration. Additional guidance was provided by Jonathan Santa Maria Bouquet Senior Conservator of the Heritage Collections of the University of Edinburgh on the subject of material and process for the computer aided manufacture of harpsichord parts. In addition to further literature review, the visit provided the opportunity to consult the Edinburgh Futures Institute's Centre for Data science and Culture and The Software Sustainability Institute to assess the open source practice of the project. 
Part of this research included attendance at the \href{https://www.eventbrite.co.uk/e/the-digital-museum-conference-tickets-995019448957?aff=eemailordconf&utm_campaign=order_confirm&ref=eemailordconf&utm_medium=email&utm_source=eventbrite&utm_term=viewevent}{``Digital Museum Conference''} to learn more about the challenges facing museums and heritage institutes with the incorporation of digital technology.
Research carried out at Edinburgh informed the second iteration of the sensor system presented as part of a paper at Forum Acusticum 2025 in Malaga, Spain. Research at the University of Edinburgh also led to the participation of the San Colombano exhibition as part of the Una Europa workshop on ``Museums and New Challenges: virtual technologies, social responsibility and environmental sustainability.'' The final year saw the finished completion of the second interface and the presentation of the first at the NIME 2025 conference in Canberra, Australia.
