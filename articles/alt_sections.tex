Technological advances have reshaped the ways museums document, present, and interpret their collections. Immersive experiences are now made possible through tools such as 3D printing and virtual reality, but such experiences can place a veil over the user and remove, rather than engage them with the heritage. These technologies are capable of facilitating experiential authenticity, yet achieving it requires museums to carefully negotiate the balance between preservation and meaningful engagement \cite{pine_museums_2007}—a tension that is especially pronounced with historical musical instrument collections \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}.

Musical instruments occupy a distinctive position within cultural heritage: they are not only aesthetic artefacts but also functional tools with an inherently tactile dimension \cite{charalampos_role_2018}. Over time, however, they inevitably deteriorate to the point where they become too fragile for continued handling \cite{paradiso_to-save_2019}. This places museums in a persistent dilemma between accessibility and conservation, often limiting how visitors can interact with collections \cite{templeton_museum_2018,mcalpine_sampling_2014}. The key question, then, is how to preserve not just the physical object, but also the embodied experience of playing it.

This chapter addresses that question by presenting the design and creation of a replica harpsichord keyboard with a typical early Italian layout, electronically augmented for use in an interactive exhibition. Moving beyond the constraints of the traditional “red velvet cord” \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014,masu_o-in-nime_2023}, the replica aims to enhance visitor interaction while protecting fragile originals. Rather than focusing on technical novelty, the project demonstrates how digital interventions can foster meaningful engagement with shared musical heritage, helping to sustain its relevance in contemporary museum practice.

The resulting exhibition, hosted at Museo San Colombano in Bologna, provided visitors with a tactile interface to digitised versions of instruments in the collection. It built on the understanding that human perception of music is strongly shaped by visual cues \cite{fritz_player_2012,fritz_soloist_2014,fritz_listener_2017,tsay_sight_2013}. The installation sought to balance historical authenticity, accessibility, and preservation, while contributing to broader debates on the role of technology in museums. The chapter discusses the keyboard’s design principles, technical implementation, and implications, highlighting its potential for deepening public engagement and supporting the long-term preservation of musical heritage.
