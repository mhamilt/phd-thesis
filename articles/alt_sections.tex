


This thesis addresses a compound problem: the specific challenges of digital conservation as it relates to musical instruments, and the broader, systemic issues facing digital conservation as a whole. Within the context of musical instruments, one of the most significant challenges is preserving the tactile and embodied experience of performance. As discussed in the previous section, this issue is not exclusive to digital conservation—it also arises in traditional conservation approaches. However, it becomes even more complex when interaction is mediated through digital means.

More broadly, the integration of digital technology within the heritage sector is far from resolved. Although digital and immersive technologies are frequently described as “transforming” the museum experience \cite{gedert_technology_2017, li_systematic_2024, dutot_changing_2021}, such claims merit critical examination. Have digital technologies genuinely transformed museums, or are they often deployed with an analogue mindset, replicating traditional models of engagement in digital form?

Importantly, the digital should not be seen as a substitute for the physical object, but rather as a means of extending its reach and interpretive potential \cite{foster_defining_1998, parry_museums_2010}. Moreover, digitisation should not be understood as a one-time act, but rather as an ongoing, iterative process \cite{rubio_work_2020, kelley_digital_2018}. Both digital and physical heritage materials rely on physical storage media \cite{rubio_work_2020}, yet the financial and environmental costs associated with long-term digital preservation can far exceed those required for analogue equivalents \cite{cieply_afterlife_2007}.

Despite these challenges, digital technologies offer distinct advantages. One of the most significant is the ability to provide centralised access to geographically distributed collections—particularly the data associated with those collections. The Musical Instrument Museums Online (MIMO) project exemplifies this strength, aggregating collection data from institutions around the world into a single online catalogue. However, the usability and long-term viability of such platforms depend heavily on the quality and structure of their data \cite{dolan_mimo_2017}, as well as the skills required to manage them.

Effective digital conservation requires multidisciplinary expertise. Museum professionals increasingly need training in digital literacy, data management, and awareness of the risks posed by digital obsolescence and silent data corruption \cite{rubio_work_2020, schwarz_disk_2006, parry_museums_2010}. In the humanities more broadly, a significant gap remains in both digital skills and the adoption of sustainable data practices \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}.

Another critical consideration is the preservation of the digital heritage generated through conservation work—such as software tools, data sets, and digital models. If we aim to preserve musical instruments through digital means, we must also ensure the long-term sustainability of the tools and systems used in that process. Software, in particular, presents a major challenge due to its reproducibility problem: it often becomes difficult to replicate processes or results even after relatively short periods of time \cite{perkel_challenge_2020, robert_reproducibility_2020}.

In response to these challenges, the digital components of this thesis have been developed in accordance with current best practices in openness, sustainability, and reproducibility. The remainder of this section outlines the structure and organisation of these digital elements and details the rationale behind the chosen methodologies.
