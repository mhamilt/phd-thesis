Technological innovation has profoundly reshaped the ways in which museums
document, present, and interpret their collections.
Museums have wrestled with how to present the ``aura of authenticity'' within a digital domain.
what \textcite{trant_when_1999}. describes as an experiential authenticity, affording encounters that
evoke the sensory, emotional, and intellectual dimensions of the past
\cite{trant_when_1999}. Yet, as Pine and Gilmore observe
\cite{pine_museums_2007}, the pursuit of authenticity requires museums to
carefully balance conservation with meaningful visitor engagement—a tension that
emerges with particular clarity in historical musical instrument collections
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}.

Museums consistently navigate the competing demands of accessibility and
preservation, which restrict the ways in which audiences can interact with
collections \cite{templeton_museum_2018, mcalpine_sampling_2014}. For
instrument museums, these challenges are heightened by the difficulty of
maintaining fragile historical instruments in playable condition
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}. Even with the strictest conservation protocols,
deterioration is unavoidable, and instruments eventually reach a stage at which
performance is no longer possible \cite{paradiso_to-save_2019}. In response,
recent decades have witnessed a cultural shift: emphasis has moved away from
playability toward conservation. Karp
\cite{karp_restoration_1979, karp_musical_1985} argues for cultivating a deep
understanding of musical instruments to generate sufficient knowledge to make
them as ``copyable'' as possible.

McAlpine’s discussion of the Benton Fletcher Collection at the National Trust
Fenton House illustrates this dilemma \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}. When the
collection was donated, Benton Fletcher required that the instruments remain
playable for both tuition and performance. To reconcile this mandate with
preservation concerns, a large-scale sampling campaign was undertaken, and a
custom MIDI interface was developed. The controller—two commercially available
keyboards designed to emulate a two-manual harpsichord—enabled visitors to
trigger instrument samples recorded with collection-specific strategies.
However, user testing revealed a limitation: the weighted keys, although
commercially available, failed to convey an authentic sense of interacting with
a historical plucked keyboard instrument \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}.

A related example is the ``Tromba Moderna'' project, introduced by
\textcite{baldwin_tromba_2016} for an exhibition at the Musikmuseet Musical
Instrument Museum in Copenhagen. Designed to address the challenges of
engagement and interactivity, the project involved recreating and augmenting a
replica of a historical tromba marina. A piezo transducer was coupled with a
sound synthesis engine and an internal driver to simulate the expected
vibrations of the instrument. Surveyed visitors reported that the Tromba Moderna
enhanced their overall museum experience.

The interface described in this chapter builds on these precedents, inheriting
elements from the Tromba Moderna while seeking to extend their scope. Its
design is motivated by the desire to enrich the visitor experience of the
Tagliavini collection, while maintaining continuity with historical
instrument-building traditions. In this sense, both the Tromba Moderna
\cite{baldwin_tromba_2016} and McAlpine’s Benton Fletcher project
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014} serve as points of departure. Furthermore, the
development of the harpsichord interface draws on four decades of haptic
keyboard research, embedding this project within a broader lineage of
instrumental interface design.
