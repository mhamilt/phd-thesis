\section{Introduction}
This thesis was undertaken as part of the NEMUS project, which addresses challenges in the conservation of historical musical instruments through the application of digital technologies \cite{nemus_description_2023}. Two key aspects of the conservation process are considered here. When a historical instrument is no longer playable—and restoration to a playable condition would compromise its physical integrity—digitisation offers a non-invasive alternative. This may involve sampling the instrument’s sound \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014,paradiso_to-save_2019,tasten_project_2018}, or reconstructing its acoustic and physical characteristics through numerical simulation \cite{XXXX}.

Despite the emergence of these techniques, the question of how to provide meaningful access to digitised instruments within museum contexts remains unresolved \cite{woodmusick_effects_2015}. Even after digitisation, the issue of interaction persists: how can we engage audiences in the experience of a musical instrument that no longer functions physically?

This thesis explores these questions through two interlinked projects. Chapter~\ref{chapter:keyboard} focuses on interaction design, considering how users can engage meaningfully with digitised instruments. Chapter~\ref{chapter:magpie} takes a material approach, investigating how the analysis of physical components—in this case, those of a harpsichord—can inform decisions about conservation pathways \cite{barclay_care_1997}.

Bridging these two case studies is a broader inquiry into the sustainability and reproducibility of digital conservation. Digital research, particularly in heritage contexts, faces significant reproducibility challenges \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}. Thus, this thesis also documents the steps taken to ensure the longevity, reusability, and accessibility of its digital outputs \cite{soito_citations_2016,smith_software_2016,katz_recognizing_2021,stall_journal_2023}.

This chapter provides context for the research, beginning with an overview of historical musical instrument conservation. It examines the theoretical and practical tensions that shape the field, especially concerning the preservation of function and sound. The second section introduces the term digital conservation as it is used throughout the thesis, exploring both its technical implications and its position within broader debates in digital heritage. Particular emphasis is placed on open methodologies—approaches that aim to produce digital outputs that are accessible, sustainable, and reusable.

\section{Musical Instrument Conservation}
The conservation of musical instruments presents unique challenges within the field of cultural heritage. Unlike many museum artefacts, musical instruments are inherently functional: they are not only objects of visual or historical interest, but also tools designed for performance, with complex tactile and acoustic properties \cite{charalampos-saitis_musical_2018}. Maintaining instruments in playable condition introduces significant risks and costs \cite{lamb_play_2007}, prompting ongoing debate about how best to balance preservation and access.

This debate has largely revolved around the competing priorities of conservation and restoration, as documented in the foundational work of Barclay and others \cite{barclay_care_1997,barclay_preservation_2005}. Barclay articulates a schema of three possible states for historical instruments: Currency, Conservation, and Restoration \cite[p. 19]{barclay_preservation_2005}. Instruments in Currency are maintained in playable condition and regularly used; Conservation involves stabilising the object in its current state without attempting to make it playable; and Restoration seeks to return the instrument to playing condition, often involving speculative reconstruction.

The harpsichord has featured prominently in this discourse, with notable examples from several collections. These include the Benton Fletcher Collection at Fenton House \cite{barclay_preservation_2005,mcalpine_sampling_2014}, the Raymond Russell Collection at St Cecilia’s Hall, and the Tagliavini Collection in San Colombano \cite{wraight_report_2022,meer_collezione_2007}. Seventeenth-century instruments from the Tagliavini Collection served as a motivating case study for this thesis. While some of the methodologies discussed here may be applicable to other instrument types, the focus throughout remains on the harpsichord.

Debates surrounding the restoration of harpsichords are not only technical but philosophical. Barclay argues that attempting to recreate the original sound of a historical instrument is problematic, particularly when restoration involves speculative elements. He suggests that museums should inform players and audiences of the uncertainties involved in such reconstructions, noting that “the speculative nature of the instrument’s ‘original sound’” must be acknowledged \cite[p. 19]{barclay_preservation_2005}.

This perspective stands in contrast to earlier views, such as that of Benton Fletcher, who insisted that instruments should remain playable to avoid becoming relics in “a dead museum of glass cases” \cite[p. 154, quoted in a letter]{barclay_preservation_2005}. A particularly telling example is the 1777 Kirkman harpsichord restored by the firm Adlam Burnett, where opposing views saw the instrument either as a playable cultural artefact or as “a temple of silence” to be conserved as furniture, with its musical function set aside \cite{barclay_critical_1999,adlam_proposal_1972}.

These debates reflect a broader tension within the heritage sector between static conservation and “living heritage”—between preserving artefacts as historical documents and enabling their continued cultural function through use. This thesis positions itself within that conversation, exploring how digital approaches might offer a third path: one that maintains the integrity of the original object while providing meaningful opportunities for interaction and interpretation.
