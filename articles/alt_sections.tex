Open source hardware has great potential benefits for use in academia \cite{oellermann_open_2022} and those same benefits can extent to the heritage sector. \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} rightfully raises concerns of tackling hardware obsolescence and how that is tackled as part of the archiving process. Proprietary systems limit the potential for innovation or reproducibility and, when these systems fail, repairs rely on the original manufacturer who may have ceased support or production of replacement parts \cite{oellermann_open_2022}.
To facilitate reproduction of hardware, it should be self-evident but, any form of archiving and provision of access to source material makes the task much more feasible. Reproduction is made even easier by incorporating open hardware.

This highlights a core problem with digital cultural heritage, which is if we rely on proprietary system for digital conservation, then they will be locked in an ecosystem which is designed to chase change. If heritage institutions are using proprietary digital systems \cite{innocente_framework_2023} they should be aware of their choice of digital infrastructure and the implications that will have on a project's longevity. As \textcite{bonvoisin_source_2017} coined ``openwashing'' likewise with digital conservation we run a risk of ``heritagewashing'' where digital projects claim to serve the conservation of cultural heritage, but are in fact ephemera. That is not to say that ephermeral heritage projects are forbidden, but this should be a conscious choice made at a project's inception and not one applied at a point in the future when the task of archiving a work becomes too great. An open methodology needs to be `baked-in' to the research process and it is not something trivially applied after the fact. It is of course better to do it afterwards and not at all, but the information at stake is constitutes also the development process, not just the end product. The point at which a project is abandoned is not the key point of record, which given their `living` status is likely to have undergone some further modifications.

Both \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} and \cite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} approach hardware archiving in a repository format. \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} proposed a workshop with the intention to create a a central repository (or database) for DMI designs. Part of that workshop's primary eight goals were the FAIR adjacent aims of encouraging reproducibility, open access and encouraging a normalised practice of documentation. Both \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} and \cite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} raise the issue of repository structure with the latter proposing a a prescriptive measure which can be applied to any DMI project.

Research in the form of Open Hardware, open software, research product can and should take advantage of the platforms available for distribution of the work,
\footnote{such as github, gitlab for source code or thingiverse for 3D models \cite{oshwa_best_2013}.}
but the work still needs to be citable \cite{smith_software_2016}, and cross-reference-able \cite{stall_journal_2023} if that work is to be built upon.

The limitation here is assuming that the constituent parts of a DMI are a collective to archive and version controlled. This is true to some degree, there is a complete artifact to be documented, but it is very much made of constituent parts that develop and are swapped out. There is the interface the electronics and the firmware, whose versions current and old can be used in all number of combinations of versions. Keeping these components contained to a single version control repository limits their modularity and potential for reuse.

Taking all that has been discussed so far and using the \citp{ssi_checklist_2018} checklist we can narrow down the core requirements of an open source research project as source code availability, a version control system (VCS), documentation (developer reference and user manual, a compliant open source licence and a unique identifier, typically a DOI. Recommendation for research software extend beyond these categories, but they are dependant on their existence. \subsection{ITEMM OpenLab}

\textsc{magpie} is not the first attempt to make plate analysis accessible, nor is it the only tool marketed as ``free.'' The Institut Technologique Européen des Métiers de la Musique (ITEMM) maintains an online platform, ITEMM Open Lab,%
\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com}} which provides luthiers and makers with resources ranging from datasets of materials to CAD models of instrument parts. Most relevant for present purposes, OpenLab also includes a suite of calculators for acoustical and mechanical properties of wood, offering workshop access to methods otherwise restricted to laboratory environments. 

Among the available tools are calculators for string behaviour under tension, resonance frequencies of soundboards and bridges, and a plate-stiffness tool, \textit{Rigidité de plaques},%
\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/calculateur-mecanique/rigidite-de-plaques-1/}} which implements the methodology of \textcite{mcintyre_measuring_1988} to estimate elastic constants. The initiative is admirable: ITEMM has made genuinely useful processes accessible to practitioners who might otherwise lack the means. But the openness has limits.

Although freely accessible once registered, OpenLab tools follow a free distribution model. In Stallman’s terms, they are closer to ``free as in beer'' than ``free as in freedom'' \cite{stallman_why_2007}. 
The code that underpins calculators such as \textit{Rigidité de plaques} is delivered through the browser and can, in principle, be examined,
\footnote{Calculations are carried out in \href{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/wp-content/plugins/ITEMM-OpenLab-tools-main/js/p_w_script.js}{\texttt{js/p\_w\_script.js}} with support from an \texttt{olt} utility class at \href{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/wp-content/plugins/ITEMM-OpenLab-tools-main/js/olt_class.js}{\texttt{js/olt\_class.js}}, both accessible without authentication.} 
but documentation, licensing, and citation pathways are not yet foregrounded.

ITEMM’s legal framework, for example, explicitly covers 3D models%
\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/mentions-legals/}} 
under a CC-BY-SA
\footnote{Although the version is not explicitly stated, this can be implied as a CC-BY-SA-4.0.}
but does not naturally extend to the software calculators themselves \cite{cc_can_2025}. 

By contrast, the development of \textsc{magpie} follows an ``open first'' methodology guided by the FAIR principles \cite{wilkinson_fair_2016,go_fair_2016}. This means that source code, metadata, and licensing are made explicit from the outset, persistent identifiers are assigned, and scholarly attribution is supported through standard citation practices \cite{soito_citations_2016}. In this way, \textsc{magpie} seeks not only to provide functionality comparable to existing online tools, but also to embed sustainability, interoperability, and citability as core design objectives.

\textcite{stallman_floss_2021} stresses the importance of distinguishing between open-source software (FOSS) and free/libre open-source software (FLOSS). The intent behind \textsc{magpie} is to occupy this latter category: a toolkit for plate analysis that is genuinely free and open in both senses of the word, and designed from the ground up as shared research infrastructure.


\subsection{NeSS}

The Next Generation Sound Synthesis project from the University of Edinburgh is a suite of physically modelled sound synthesis algorithms. NESS was a joint project between the Acoustics and Audio Group at the University of Edinburgh and the Edinburgh Parallel Computing Centre, running for five years between ran between 2012 and 2017. 

During the course of this thesis the source code for NeSS was made publicly available by the Edinburgh Acoustics and Audio Group under an OSI approved MIT Licence via the online VCS service GitHub. The repository has a flattened structure with all NeSS source code files placed in a single directory, with only a shared library for sparse matrix operations separated in a sub-directory. 
The repository documentation covers the compilation of the source code as well as an incomplete overview of structure and usage.
\footnote{https://github.com/Edinburgh-Acoustics-and-Audio-Group/ness/tree/main/doc}
The associated website 
\footnote{\url{https://www.ness.music.ed.ac.uk}}
provides explanatory documentation for each of the core components of the software and working examples for each of the NeSS instruments as well as the central \citetitle{ness_welcome_2016} \cite{ness_welcome_2016}.

\subsection{PFFDTD}

An extension of room acoustics modelling from the NeSS project, simulations are run exclusively using the CUDA framework for GPU parallelisation of computations. With the exception of SketchUp source model, PFFDTD uses the open source Ruby, Python and C languages installation and execution. 
The source code is provided under an OSI approved MIT license

\subsection{VK-Gong}

VK-Gong is software created out of ENSTA Paris for the numerical simulation of nonlinear plates and shells using Föppl–von Kármán theory for nonlinear vibrations. Such non linear systems would be found in percussive instruments such as gongs, cymbals and bells.  The VK-Gong source code, in MATLAB and C\textsc{++}, is hosted from an ENSTA Paris site. The project contains one of the most comprehensive user manuals \cite{vkgong_manual_2017} among the  software in this sample set, which outlines the technical background and usage instructions in both a `how-to' and tutorial format, although this applies entirely to the MATLAB version of the source. The C\textsc{++} is marked as ``preliminary'' with documentation to be ``ready in summer 2017.''

VK-Gong's site states that the work is distributed under the CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 licence,
\footnote{\url{https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/}}
although the license text does not appear within the source as either a document string or a separate license file. The usage of CC licenses for software is discouraged by both the Creative Commons and Open Source Initiative and though the CC-BY-SA-4.0 is ``one way'' compliant with the GPLv3 \cite{cc_can_2025}, the CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 is not.

Though the source is openly available from the VK-Gong site, it does not use any version control system. Without a VCS, the growth of the software is stunted which is a missed opportunity especially given the C\textsc{++} version of VK-Gong is still in its preliminary stages. The CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 licence permits the mirroring of the source code,
\footnote{\url{https://github.com/rodrigodzf/VKGong}}
uploading of this code onto an online archive platform like Zeonodo is not compliant with its rules \cite{zenodo_metadata_2025}. 
The lack of a DOI or provision of a citation standard make tracking usage of the software in research tricky to undertake, though it has seen usage \cite{nguyen_nonlinear_2019}.

The main technical obstacle with VK-Gong source code is the usage of SSE single instruction multiple data (SIMD) instructions, also known as intrinsics,
\footnote{\url{https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/docs/intrinsics-guide/index.html}}
which are only applicable to Intel processors. There are also SSE SIMD instructions used within the NeSS source code, although separate compilation instructions have been provided for specific CPU architecture and operating system. The SSE SIMD instructions are not compatible with Arm architecture processors, therefore the pre-compiled binary files that are distributed with the C\textsc{++} code would not be executable on a incompatible processor. Any users with an arm processor would therefore need to recompile the source code themselves would find them selves unable to do so with the provided \texttt{Makefile}. NeSS and VK-Gong have a common library used for matrix multiplication and therefore there usage of SSE SIMD is identical. To compile the VK-Gong source on an arm processor the user would have to edit or create a new make file with the \texttt{USE_SSE} flag removed as well as removing the \texttt{sse.c} file from the list of compiled files. Alternatively, the user could also employ a translation file to move from Intel's SSE to Arm's NEON SIMD instructions.
\footnote{\url{https://github.com/DLTcollab/sse2neon}}
Since there is no version control repository or public issue tracking, the same modifications would need to be research and carried out by every potential user.

\subsection{YIN}
The YIN pitch detection algorithm was also briefly discussed in Section~\ref{sec:aurora} alongside the case study of Aurora as example open source acoustics software practice.
The theory behind the YIN algorithm is discussed in \textcite{cheveigne_yin_2002} and its MATLAB source code code see direct usage in research.
Like Aurora, YINs source code has no single fixed URI, let alone a DOI with which it can be cited and traced. YIN also also has one of the most restrictive licences with the original MATLAB source limited to ``FOR RESEARCH PURPOSES''
\footnote{\url{https://github.com/mhamilt/yin/blob/main/LICENCE}}
which is not an OSI compliant clause and would disqualify its categorisation as FOSS.
Used in \textcite{bowen_assessing_2019} as part of a study on musical instrument heritage clarinets that were not playable.
\footnote{\textcite{bowen_assessing_2019} cites \textcite{cheveigne_yin_2002} but also remarks that the MATLAB was used as part of the study, making highly likely that the paper used the original YIN source code.}

The documentation for YIN takes the form of the associated publication \cite{cheveigne_yin_2002}, which would constitute the ``Explanation'' form of documentation in \textcite{procida_diataxis_2025}, developer documentation is absent.
Like the problematic SSE intrinsics in VK-Gong, YINs technical stumbling block is the usage of MATLAB's MEX compilation system \cite{matlab_mex_2025}. In YIN's currently form, as it was 2003, the compiled \texttt{.mex} files will not execute correctly in any MATLAB version from R2016a onwards, a not to dissimilar situation as the SSE instruction hurdle in VK-Gong. Instead, the \texttt{.mex} must be recreated due to changes in the compilation system. Recompiling does not provide a great obstacle, and the correct syntax can be quickly found by consulting the documentation, but this becomes a problem that \textit{every} user needs to solve. Not all researcher will have the same technical background and skill-set and without a centralised source, like a version controlled repository, there is no single place to raise or document such technical problems.


\subsection{Summary of Acoustics Software Practice}

From the set of seven acoustics software we can we can see a inclination towards an open source model, but not yet full compliance with current guidance. Table~\ref{tab:foss-compliance} provides an overview of the software that has been discussed and where the comply in terms of source availability, version control, documentation, licensing and unique identification.  

It is apparent that unique identifiers are absent across all examples, though some such as \citetitle{hamilton_pffdtd_2021}, \citetitle{berzborn_ita_2017} have taken step towards citablility by providing an explicit citation, which is the same observation made in \textcite{hornikx_exploring_2024}.

It is also clear that developer documentation is largely missing from these projects. The audience served is  primarily users, but from the level of documentation, one with an assumed base level of technical knowledge. Assumption of technical skill excludes a potential audience, in particular those who are not from the field of acoustics, but would wish to apply this software to other research.
