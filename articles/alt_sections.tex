Open source hardware has great potential benefits for use in academia \cite{oellermann_open_2022} and those same benefits can extent to the heritage sector. \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} rightfully raises concerns of tackling hardware obsolescence and how that is tackled as part of the archiving process. Proprietary systems limit the potential for innovation or reproducibility and, when these systems fail, repairs rely on the original manufacturer who may have ceased support or production of replacement parts \cite{oellermann_open_2022}.
To facilitate reproduction of hardware, it should be self-evident but, any form of archiving and provision of access to source material makes the task much more feasible. Reproduction is made even easier by incorporating open hardware.

This highlights a core problem with digital cultural heritage, which is if we rely on proprietary system for digital conservation, then they will be locked in an ecosystem which is designed to chase change. If heritage institutions are using proprietary digital systems \cite{innocente_framework_2023} they should be aware of their choice of digital infrastructure and the implications that will have on a project's longevity. As \textcite{bonvoisin_source_2017} coined ``openwashing'' likewise with digital conservation we run a risk of ``heritagewashing'' where digital projects claim to serve the conservation of cultural heritage, but are in fact ephemera. That is not to say that ephermeral heritage projects are forbidden, but this should be a conscious choice made at a project's inception and not one applied at a point in the future when the task of archiving a work becomes too great. An open methodology needs to be `baked-in' to the research process and it is not something trivially applied after the fact. It is of course better to do it afterwards and not at all, but the information at stake is constitutes also the development process, not just the end product. The point at which a project is abandoned is not the key point of record, which given their `living` status is likely to have undergone some further modifications.

Both \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} and \cite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} approach hardware archiving in a repository format. \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} proposed a workshop with the intention to create a a central repository (or database) for DMI designs. Part of that workshop's primary eight goals were the FAIR adjacent aims of encouraging reproducibility, open access and encouraging a normalised practice of documentation. Both \textcite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} and \cite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} raise the issue of repository structure with the latter proposing a a prescriptive measure which can be applied to any DMI project.

Research in the form of Open Hardware, open software, research product can and should take advantage of the platforms available for distribution of the work,

but the work still needs to be citable \cite{smith_software_2016}, and cross-reference-able \cite{stall_journal_2023} if that work is to be built upon.

The limitation here is assuming that the constituent parts of a DMI are a collective to archive and version controlled. There is the interface the electronics and the firmware, whose versions current and old can be used in all number of combinations of versions. Keeping these components contained to a single version control repository limits their modularity and potential for reuse.

Since the biharmonic operator matrix is multi-diagonal a diagonal format sparse matrix appeared to be the best target. As opposed to the compressed sparse column format which stores row indices and first element columns indices, the diagonal stores arrays of diagonal values and their diagonal position (Figure~\ref{fig:sparse-dia}). The diagonal format would require explicit zero values, though there are a relative small number and thus have minimal impact on performance. 
It is worth noting that additional configuration such as the SciPy sparse library requires is not a unique phenomenon when it comes to FOSS. With the openness there also comes a cost in the form of a technical skill barrier.
Such is the case here, for the proprietary MATLAB the user does not need to engage with directly with the sparsity format, rather they are given a single format, CSC \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992}, and a small set of functions with which to construct a sparse matrix.
\footnote{\texttt{spdiags} for banded matrices and the \texttt{sparse} function for triplet format or dense matrix conversion.}

These construction matrices will then need explicit compression into formats such as CSC or DIA (Figure~\ref{fig:sparse-dia}), which are steps that would be otherwise invisible to the MATLAB user. This is granularity is not a net negative or positive, but  it is double-edged. The additional debt of technical knowledge is off-set by the freedom to scrutinise the process.
