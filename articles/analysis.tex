\section{Introduction}

This chapter aims to cover the main development steps of \textsc{magpie}, the design choices taken, technical underpinnings, justification and a case study of a hypothetical library application.

Research into plate vibration have a long-standing and ongoing presence in the scientific literature. Plates are ubiquitous in engineering applications, particularly in acoustics, where they play a critical role in musical instruments. Soundboards of string instruments, for example, are accurately described by classical plate theories such as Kirchhoff-Love \cite{love_small_1888} and Mindlin-Reissner \cite{mindlin_influence_1951} models.

Knowledge of material parameters, particularly elastic constants, provides valuable insight into the structural integrity of a musical instrument. In the case of string instrument soundboards, elastic constants offer a quantitative measure of the instrument’s condition \cite{viala_decision_2025,duran_use_2025}. For collections of musical instruments, understanding the elastic limits of materials is essential for effective conservation \cite[p. 19-20]{barclay_care_1997}. Periodic monitoring of these constants allows curators to assess the state of a soundboard and to intervene when necessary.

Determining the material properties of a complete instrument is challenging \cite{viala_model_2019}. Conventional mechanical tests—such as bending, tensile, or compression experiments—cannot be conducted in situ and are typically destructive. In contrast, vibration-based measurements provide a minimally invasive approach, offering low-risk assessment of elastic constants.

Characterization of tonewood elastic properties can be achieved by representing eigenvectors as mode shapes (eigenshapes) \cite{ducceschi_method_2024}, complementing numerical simulations. Despite their proliferation, plate simulations are still largely accessible only through proprietary software, often beyond the budget of students or small research groups. Finite element tools, such as those employed in modelling instrument vibrations \cite{viala_model_2019}, frequently present financial or technical barriers.

This situation motivated the development of a software library, \textsc{magpie}, designed for the non-invasive derivation of material parameters. \textsc{magpie} is intended as an open-source platform for simulating the physics of plates, offering intuitive visualization tools and a range of analysis techniques. \textsc{magpie} represents the second branch the thesis 'heritage conservation through analytical methods.' This chapter covers the the creation of the \textsc{magpie} including the background plate physics, the numerical approach for simulations, and the implementation details. 

Plates are particularly well-suited for studying vibration through eigenshape visualization. They are commonly used as educational tools for demonstrating Chladni patterns \cite{chladni_treatise_2015}, providing both accurate results and visually engaging representations. Presenting eigenshapes as Chladni patterns can also be used a visual verification tool for matching conditions during a physical experiment. A platform such as \textsc{magpie} can serve the dual purposes an analytical tool for researchers and industry practitioners and also a teaching aid for instructors. \textsc{magpie}'s functionalities include applying elastic boundary conditions along plate edges, visualizing and exporting eigenshapes and frequencies, performing time-domain simulations of the plate equation for sound synthesis, and estimating the elastic modulus of experimental plates.

\section{Context}

The Kirchhoff-Love theory of plates describes the dynamics of thin sheet materials under bending. Assuming constant thickness and density, and with the further hypothesis of isotropy, a linear partial differential equation describing wave propagation is, thus \cite{graff_wave_1991,bilbao_numerical_2009}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:KLCnt}
\rho \zeta \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = - D \Delta\Delta u - 2\rho\zeta\sigma\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \delta(x-x_p)\delta(y-y_p)f(t).
\end{equation}

% The boundary terms for the edges at $y = 0,L_y$ are obtained using analogous expressions for the force and moment ${\mathfrak F}_y$, ${\mathfrak M}_y$: one only needs to swap $x$ and $y$ in the expressions for ${\mathfrak F}_x$, ${\mathfrak M}_x$. With these in mind, one can easily recover the expressions for free, clamped and simply supported edge conditions: in the free case, the force and the moment vanish; in the clamped case, the displacement and the slope vanish; in the simply supported case, the displacement and the moment vanish. For all such cases, the boundary terms vanish, so the total plate energy follows the energy balance above. One further condition arises at the corner of two free edges, namely:

% \begin{equation}\label{eq:CornerCnt}
% \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x \partial y} = 0,
% \end{equation}

see, for example, the book by Szilard for an explanation \cite{szilard_theories_2004}.

The origins of \textsc{magpie} extend from a set of \textsc{matlab} scripts used internally at the NEMUS projects. The \textsc{matlab} scripts were created for computing the eigenmodes for an isotropic rectangular Kirschhoff-Love plate model. Additionally, the  plate modelled is under general elastic boundary conditions, which is to say that the boundary coefficients for each edge of the plate are computed parametrically using two boundary constants representing flexural and rotational stiffness.
The script was therefore multi-modal with use cases of calculating the discrete biharmonic matrix for a finite difference scheme. This generated biharmonic can be used directly with an eigensolver for calculation of the modes and modal shapes, either for analysis or visualisation.  An impulse response of the plate can be generated through a finite difference simulation. Finally, the script was capable of being used as a tool for estimation of the Young's modulus for a real plate. For this process, an experimental setup is created where a plate is put under boundary conditions. An impulse response of the plate can be measured using an impact hammer from which the modal frequencies of the plate can be retrieved. Starting from a best guess Young's estimation, the script could then be used to simulate the physical experiment under multiple iterations with least-squares regression and provide an estimation.

Process is to get non-dimensional frequencies, then least-square (LS) optimisation

\begin{verbatim}
% Run magpie first to get non-dimensional frequencies
Om        = magpie(rho,E0,nu,ldim,h,BCs,Ntrain,"none") ;
OmNDim    = Om ./ sqrt(D0) * sqrt(rho * A^2 * Lz) ;
OmNDimsq  = OmNDim.^2 ;

% Least-square (LS) optimisation
psi       = (2*pi*Freq).^2 * rho * Lz * A^2 ;
DLS       = (OmNDimsq.' * psi') / (OmNDimsq.' * OmNDimsq) ;
ELS       = DLS / (Lz^3/12/(1-nu^2));
    
\end{verbatim}


$$
D_{0}          = \frac{EL_z^3}{12(1-nu^2)},\quad A = L_xL_y ; \\
\vec{\omega_n} = \left(\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{D_0 \rho A^2 L_z}}\right)^2\\
\vec{\psi}     = (2 \pi \vec{F_{e}})^2 \rho L_z A^2 ;\\
D_{LS}        = (\vec{\omega_n} psi) / (\vec{\omega_n} * \vec{\omega_n}) ;\\
E_{LS}        = \frac{D_{LS}}{12 L_z^3 (1-nu^2)}
$$

% \begin{equation}\label{eq:EigenBounds}
% 0  \lessapprox \lambda_{\bf B} \leq 64 h^{-4},
% \end{equation}

% where the approximate left inequality is due to the rigid-body eigenvalues becoming negative in a very small neighbourhood around zero. For elastically restrained plates, the eigenvalues are bounded according to:

% \begin{equation}

% \end{equation}

% where the ``big-Oh'' notation was used. Thus, when the plate is restrained using very stiff springs, the spectral radius of the matrix becomes large. Whilst most of the eigenvalues will still be found within the bounds in \eqref{eq:EigenBounds}, a handful of ``spurious'' eigenvalues and associated modal shapes may result from applying the elastic boundary conditions using very large stiffness constants. In any case, the matrix $\bf B$ is factorised according to:

% \begin{equation}\label{eq:factorisation}
% {\bf B} = {\bf Q}{\boldsymbol \Lambda}{\bf Q}^{-1},
% \end{equation}

% where ${\boldsymbol \Lambda}$ is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues, and $\bf Q$ is a matrix of associated column eigenvectors. The plate eigenfrequencies, in radians per second, are obtained as:

% \begin{equation}
% {\boldsymbol \Omega} = \sqrt{\frac{D}{\rho\zeta}} \sqrt{{\boldsymbol \Lambda}},
% \end{equation}

% \end{equation}

% \begin{equation}\label{eq:ModalSyst}
% \ddot {\bf w}(t) = -{\boldsymbol \Omega}^2 {\bf w}(t) -2 {\bf C}\dot {\bf w}(t) + \rho^{-1}\zeta^{-1}h^{-2}{\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf J}f(t),
% \end{equation}

The proposal was the creation of a tool \textsc{magpie} which would collect together the functionality for computing a discrete biharmonic operator with parametric boundary conditions, an eigensolver, impulse response generation through modal time integration, and Young's modulus estimation. The tool could also function as a teaching aid for lecturers and as an analysis tool for researchers and industry stakeholders. \textsc{magpie} was originally planned exclusively as a library for the Python programming language. Early in development, it became apparent that \textsc{magpie} would have greater reach as a teaching aid and analysis tool if implemented across multiple programming languages. It was devised that  \textsc{magpie} would instead be a core set of functions replicated across languages, beginning with \textsc{matlab} given its prevalence within academia and industry. The original script on which \textsc{magpie} is based was written in \textsc{matlab} and could function as a know working example. This would avoid an platform specific problem that may arise during the refactoring  process. After the initial refactor in \textsc{matlab}, the set of functions could be replicated in other programming languages, ultimately Python and \textsc{c++}. The structure of \textsc{magpie} would allow the ability to design interfaces separately, which would lead to a user-interface using the Jupyter notebooks platform.

\subsection{Design}\label{design}

Source code was structured using the model-view-controller (MVC) paradigm \cite{krasner_description_1988}, splitting the software into three major components: data (Model), interface (view), and core logic (controller). The MVC approach was taken after the case study of Aurora (see Section \ref{sec:aurora}) where the interweaving of interface and logic made the source code much more difficult to maintain. For MAGPIE, the logic and interface have been separated. The logic is organised into a library, allowing for easy translations across platforms. Source code for the interface to the logic is contained in a separate repository. By separating logic and interface, further developments can be made to one component without impacting the other. 
The interface repository contains a git submodule reference to the logic repository, such as used in the meta-repository for the harpsichord interface.
The model (or data) is supplied by the user and would comprise of the measured modal frequencies of a thin plate \cite{duran_use_2025}.

In addition, this format of interacting with the library means that only affected coefficients need to be recalculated and is, therefore, more intuitive than a script structure. Users can alter the number of grid points within the biharmonic to trade-off between responsiveness and accuracy. First, users can explore the parameter space with fewer grid points for quicker computation. When an area of interest is found, the effective resolution can be increased for higher-quality visualisations. Lastly, Jupyter Notebooks allows for the potential to provide \textsc{magpie} as a service on a public server. This would remove the need for installation of additional libraries and dependencies, lowering the barrier of entry to MAGPIE.

The version control software \texttt{git} was used to track changes and permit distribution using the GitHub platform \cite{magpie_matlab_2025}.

\subsubsection{Computing the biharmonic operator}

The thin plate \cites[][p. 331]{bilbao_numerical_2009}[][p. 233]{graff_wave_1991} with loss \cites[][p. 341]{bilbao_numerical_2009} and force.

\begin{equation}
\rho\zeta\ddot{u} = -\kappa^{2}\Delta\Delta u - 2 \rho\zeta\sigma\dot{u}
\end{equation}


% transformation of a 2D grid to a linear indexed vector. The grid co- ordinates are aligned with their corresponding linear index value. Ny is the number of grid points in the y-axis.

The plate edges can be elastically restrained \cite{li_exact_2009} by terms which control the rotation and displacement of the boundary. The biharmonic operator as in \textcite[][p. 233]{graff_wave_1991} and its discrete finite difference form in \textcite[][p. 289]{bilbao_numerical_2009} can be approximated as 

\[
B_{\Delta\Delta} \approx \delta\Delta\delta\Delta = (\delta_{xx} + \delta_{yy})^{2} = \delta_{xxxx} + 2\delta_{xx}\delta_{yy} + \delta_{yyyy}\\
\Delta\Delta = \frac{\delta^4}{\delta x^4} + 2\frac{\delta^4}{\delta x^2 \delta y2} + \frac{\delta^4}{\delta y^4}
\]

The 13-point biharmonic stencil, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:ghost-points}, only has 4 unique coefficients. In addition, two extra coefficients are required for the case of clamped and simply supported boundary conditions, implying that the biharmonic operator in those classical cases is composed of 6 unique coefficients. By contrast, the biharmonic operator with generalised elastic boundary conditions can contain up to 229 unique coefficients. As a result, each block or diagonal sector cannot be constructed through the repetition of a stencil pattern but must be declared explicitly. This lack of pattern repetition also impacts computational performance when constructing the operator. Since the biharmonic operator with generalised boundary condition provides very useful functionality in and of itself, the functionality to compute it is provided with an open interface as part of the library through the \texttt{bhmat} function \cite{magpie_matlab_2025}. The operator can then be used in further user-defined Finite Difference Time Domain schemes or if the users wish to take a more bespoke approach to eigenvalue/eigenvector calculation.


The finite difference system of the plate grid, with $N_x$ points in the x-axis and $N_y$ point in the y-axis, can be transformed in to a linear indexed vector $N_x$ by $N_y$ in length. (Figure~\ref{fig:fdtd-linear}). The corresponding biharmonic spatial operator will then be a $N_x$ by $N_y$ square matrix the central coefficients forming the stencil and matrix shown in Figure~\ref{fig:biharmonic-stencil}. Once the boundary conditions are taken into consideration, the result is a multi-diagonal matrix with coefficient distribution as given in Figure~\ref{fig:biharm_zoom}. An more in-depth formulation of the discrete finite difference biharmonic operator can be found in \textcite[Chapters 10-12]{bilbao_numerical_2009}.


The biharmonic coefficients would be calculated multiple times for some functionality within \textsc{magpie} and it was clear that the process of assembling the biharmonic would be key for computational performance. Over 200 matrix coefficients first need to be computed before they are assembled. The original source had been designed for a block sparse format (Figure~\ref{fig:biharm-subblocks}). The block format resulted in more manageable source code, but at the cost of performance. The intention as to optimise the assembly of the biharmonic, which takes place in the \texttt{bhmat} function. Optimisation would be made easier if the coefficient calculation were separated from the biharmonic assembly stage. 
Given the complexity of the coefficient calculations, by being interwoven with another function, the potential for error would be much higher. Avoiding typographic or input errors is a far easier task when moving a single-line function compared to 20 lines of code.
Consolidating the biharmonic construction stage into the \texttt{bhmat} function would make it much more portable and ensure optimisation would cascade to any other source code which used it such as the central \texttt{magpie} function.

Optimal construction of the operator was a key factor during \textsc{magpie}'s development. 
It was necessary to declare the biharmonic operator as in matrix as this is the input format used by the interfaces to ARPACK \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998} available in both \textsc{matlab} and Python's SciPy package. Conveniently, the biharmonic operator is multi-diagonal and lends itself to the sparse matrix formats of \textsc{matlab} and SciPy.
The biharmonic operator sparsity pattern (Figure \ref{fig:biharm_zoom}) can be decomposed into several methods, of which two will be described. 
It is worth noting that explicitly declaring the set of coefficients in a loop formulation, though a valid method of using the biharmonic operator, was not considered an option for the current implementations as it does not output a matrix. 

Given its multi-diagonal structure the biharmonic is largely empty and is well suited for sparse matrix storage formats \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992}. 
Sparse matrices provide optimisation in both storage and computation, since zero multiplication results in zero any zero value in a matrix can be discarded. This quality is only beneficial, however, for matrices with a small enough non-zero content. \footnote{``any matrix
with enough zeros that it pays to take advantage of them'' \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992} quoting  \cite{wilkinson_algebraic_1965}}
In the first approach, the operator can be composed of sparse sub-blocks assembled separately and then concatenated later. Figure \ref{fig:biharm-subblocks} shows the pattern of repetition within the biharmonic and how each sub-block can be indexed. The colour of each sub-block illustrates the coefficients of the biharmonic affected by the plate boundaries. The central repeating section, highlighted in blue, represents the central part of the plate extending away from boundaries with ghosts points like in Figure~\ref{fig:ghost-points} and expands upon the stencil in Figure~\ref{fig:biharmonic-stencil}. This approach simplifies code structure by avoiding the need for zero padding of the computed matrices but has implications for performance. 
\textsc{matlab} stores its sparse matrices in a compressed sparse column array format \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992} where the values and indices of the matrix are stored in order or column. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{img/plateBCs1.pdf}
    \caption{Ghost points resulting from applying the biharmonic difference operator to the upper-left corner.}
    \label{fig:ghost-points}
\end{figure} 

Iteration over columns results in sequential memory access, which provides better performance compared to non-sequential row iteration \cite{dongarra_linpack_1979}. \textsc{matlab}'s specific sparse implementation is covered in \textcite[p. 335]{gilbert_sparse_1992} which states:

\begin{quotation}
   A sparse matrix is stored as the concatenation of the
sparse vectors representing its columns. Each sparse vector consists of a floating point
array of non-zero entries (or two such arrays for complex matrices), together with an
integer array of row indices. A second integer array gives the locations in the other
arrays of the first element in each column.
\end{quotation}

For example, taking matrix:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:csc-sparse}
    A = \begin{bmatrix}
            1 & 0 & 0 \\
            0 & 2 & 0 \\
            4 & 0 & 5
        \end{bmatrix}; v_{A} =\begin{bmatrix}1\\4\\2\\5\end{bmatrix} ; r_{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\3\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}; c_{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\3\\4\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $v$ is the concatenated array of sparse matrix values, $r_{A}$ is the array of row indices and $c_{A}$ is the array of first element column indices.\footnote{For a coloumn compressed spasrse format, column indices may also end with a value one greater than the total number of values stored. For eq~\ref{eq:csc-sparse} this would result in a column index array of $c_{A} = [1 3 4 5]$ \cite{nvidia_sparse_2025}}
% A sparse vector for a single value would consist of a value from $v_A$ and $r_A$, for instance for an index i = 2, sparse vector $v_1 = [v_{A2},r_{A2}] = [4,1]$.

This would mean that concatenation of blocks would necessitate the reordering and concatenation of the new columns. Such an operation would require the copying and recopying of memory blocks which has a severe impact on computing performance. Before the biharmonic construction was optimised, the function were ported to Python to confirm that the same methodology could be used and confirm the portability of the existing source code.

\subsection{Python}

Unlike \textsc{matlab}, Python is an entirely open source programming language with growing use within scietific research \cite{}\footnote{\url{https://cacm.acm.org/blogcacm/python-is-now-the-most-popular-introductory-teaching-language-at-top-u-s-universities/}}. These qualities made Python a obvious first choice for porting the \textsc{magpie} as it conformed to the accessible and reusable principles of the projects open methodology.

% Changes to biharmonic formulation
For the python variation the biharmonic formulation was update. The new approach was to decompose the operator across the 13 diagonals and to separate them into non-repeating sectors. Each diagonal array is split into 3-5 sectors; each diagonal sector contains between 3-5 unique coefficients. Care must be taken to zero pad diagonals where necessary. The two boundary conditions (flexural and rotational spring stiffness) per edge of the plate mean 8 boundary stiffness coefficients (BSP), which impacts the repetition of diagonal patterns in the distribution of coefficients. Figure \ref{fig:biharm_zoom} illustrates the distribution of unique coefficients within the operator. As in Figure \ref{fig:bh-block}, Figure \ref{fig:biharm_zoom} shows a repeating central section but an overall lack of repeating diagonal patterns.

The diagonal format would require explicit zero values, though there are a relative small number and thus have minmal impact on performance.

\subsubsection{Eigensolver accuracy}

Both \textsc{matlab} and Python provide eigensolver functionality through an interface with the ARPACK library \cite{scipy_arpack_2025,gilbert_sparse_1992}. For the standard eigen problem $\textbf{A}\vec{x} = \lambda \vec{x} $ the ARPACK solver routines find eigenvalues iteratively, ranked by magnitude, with the largest magnitude eigenvalues requiring the least computation \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998}.

% For the \textsc{matlab} Implementation, the eigensolver computes the smallest absolute values which is a Krylov method using the inverse of the biharmonic \url{https://uk.mathworks.com/help/matlab/ref/eigs.html} \cite{stewart_krylov_2002}.

The biharmonic is multi-diagonal, sparse, real, and has a possibility to be symmetrical in which case it is positive definite \cite{}. The eigenvalues of the biharmonic are the modes of the system and, for musical acoustics, the lower and fundamental modes are those of greatest interest. 
For small magnitude eigenvalues, the eigensolvers will either converge slowly or fail entirely leading to much longer compute times \url{https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy-1.0.0/reference/tutorial/arpack.html}. 
Given these conditions, the subset of relevant matrix operations for eigensolvers is reduced.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
\begin{verbatim}
    for k = 1 to n
        // Step 1: Pivot selection
        pivot_row = index of max(abs(A[k:n, k])) + k - 1
        if pivot_row != k
            swap row k of A and pivot_row of A
            swap row k of A and pivot_row of P
            if k > 1:
                swap row k of L and pivot_row of L (columns 1..k-1)

        // Step 2: Compute multipliers and elimination
        for i = k+1 to n

            for j = k to n
                A[i,j] = A[i,j] - L[i,k] * A[k,j]

        // Step 3: Set U row
        for j = k to n
            U[k,j] = A[k,j]
\end{verbatim}

    \label{fig:lu-decomp}
\end{figure}

In order to find these smaller magnitude eigenvalues, the solver must be operated in a shift-inverse mode. This consists of transforming the eigenvalue problem from the traditional $Ax = \lambda x$ to $(A- \sigma I)^{-1} = (\frac{1}{\lambda - \sigma})x$ shifting with a value $\sigma$ and inverting the matrix $(A - \sigma I)$. For $sigma = 0$, $(A)^{-1}x = (\frac{1}{\lambda})x$, large $\lambda$ therefore becomes small to the solver and smaller magnitude eigenvalues will be found first. When the biharmonic is large inverting it can be a computationally expensive operation. 
The biharmonic can be inverted algebraically as part of LU decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:lu-decomp}).

For a shift-invert solve, the matrix is never explicitly inverted. Instead the backwards substitution of LU decomposition inverts the matrix algebraically which is computationally more efficient as part of the eigensolver. LU decomposition is defined as $A = LU$, where $L$ is the lower triangular matrix (with $1$s on the diagonal), $U$ is upper triangular matrix. This can be applied to the standard eigen problem such that \(Ly = \lambda \vec{x}, U\vec{x} = y\).

Through forward substitution of $y$ with the upper triangular matrix $L$ we determine: 

\(y = L^{-1} \lambda x\)

and through backwards substitution of $x$ with the upper triangular matrix $U$: 

\((x = U^{-1}y\)

from which we can derive

\(x = U^{-1}L^{-1}\lambda x, \frac{1}{\lambda}x = U^{-1}L^{-1}x, \frac{1}{\lambda}x = (A)^{-1}x\)

For a shift-invert using $\sigma = 0$ with LU decomposition the $A$ and $\lambda$ will be re-inverted.

For this reasons, the \textsc{matlab} and SciPy interfaces with ARPACK can be passed a sigma value of 0 to find the smallest magnitude eigenvalues. Counter-intuitively, ARPACK requires that the option to find largest magnitude is kept, but when a $\sigma$ values is provided the values will be implicitly passed through a second shift-invert process.\footnote{This can cause some confusion between \textsc{matlab} and SciPys interfaces. If \textsc{matlab}'s \texttt{eigs} is passed a \texttt{'smallestabs'} option, this is equivalent to a $\sigma 0$ and `largestabs' configuration. SciPy on the otherhand requires the explicit largest magnitude option \texttt{which='LM'} on top of a $\sigma$ value. For SciPy \texttt{which='SM'} will always result in a slower or potentially non-converging solve.}

\subsection{\textsc{c++}}

Like python, \textsc{c++} is entirely open source which continued to satisfy the FAIR principles of the open methodology. As both a strongly typed and compiled language, \textsc{c++} presented a greater challenge for development. 
The primary bottleneck for development in \textsc{magpie} is the eigensolver stage.  As stated previously, \textsc{matlab} and Python's SciPy interface with the ARPACK library \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998}. \textsc{matlab} incorporates some analysis of the target matrix to which the user is not privvy. 
On top of that, \textsc{matlab} uses its own sparse library, \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992} which obfuscates the process further.
\textsc{matlab}'s \texttt{eigs} function is not one that is supported by the software's code generation systems and the function still requires the \textsc{matlab} runtime if it is to be interfaced with externally. This has implications on the portability of any tool using \textsc{matlab} and eigs, with development being constrained to those with paid access to the \textsc{matlab} environment.
ARPACK itself is open source \url{https://github.com/opencollab/arpack-ng} and requires the Basic Linear Algebra Subroutines (BLAS) \url{http://www.netlib.org/blas/} and Linear Algebra PACKage (LAPACK)  and \url{http://www.netlib.org/lapack/} in order to function.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{img/plot-biharmonic-comparison.pdf}
    \caption{Construction of the diagonalised biharmonic in \textsc{matlab}, Python and \textsc{c++}.}
    \label{fig:biharm-diag-comp}
\end{figure}

For \textsc{c++}, interfacing with ARPACK is available via the open source Eigen library \cite{eigen_git_2025}. ARPACK is written in Fortran and a compiled binary is required for whatever operating system architecture is present.
Therefore, any tool using ARPACK is limited to the compiler support for Fortran.
Developing in \textsc{c++} presented an opportunity to bypass this restriction using the open source Spectra library \cite{spectra_git_2025}. Spectra is a header-only library for solving eigenvalue problems and consists of a code base that has re-implemented ARPACK solvers in \textsc{c++}. The header-only nature of the library allows for direct, native compilation. Both Spectra and Eigen are heavily templated languages which, though versatile, results in a higher barrier to entry for development. The same structure and development process as taken with the Python and \textsc{matlab} version of \textsc{magpie} was applied to the \textsc{c++} implementation \cite{magpie_cpp_2025}. 

Although the \textsc{c++} project is still in its infancy, early benchmarking of performance and accuracy shows promise.
Figure~\ref{fig:biharm-diag-comp} shows the compute time for the biharmonic of increasing grid resolution between all three implementations, with \textsc{c++} showing similar performance to \textsc{matlab} and it is likely that further performance improvement could be achieved with further refinement. The Eigen library's sparse implementation is similar to \textsc{matlab}'s in that it uses CSC format sparse matrices. Where they differ is in the assembly phase, where Eigen allows for matrices to be assembled using "triplet"\footnote{in the format \texttt{value, x-coordinate, and y-coordinate}} after which the matrix is "finalised", thus avoiding any memory reallocation.

This section covers the  creation of a graphical user interface to MAGPIE, forming a case study on how intrefaces with the library can be built. The interface was designed around the scenario where a user wishes to explore the transition from a simply supported condition case to a clamped case by gradually increasing rotational spring stiffness across one edge of a plate and observing the effects on modal patterns and frequency response. The user first defines the plate material parameters, which are Young's modulus, density, Poisson number, and dimensions. These parameters compute the discrete biharmonic operator in a sparse matrix format. The biharmonic operator is then used as input for an eigensolver, which returns the eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

The intention was to design an interface to the \textsc{matlab} version of \textsc{magpie}, given the performance,m accuracy and ease of use of the development environment. The interface was to be design using \textsc{matlab}'s GUIDE tool, but it was deprecated in 2021a and finally  removed in 2025a \url{https://uk.mathworks.com/help/matlab/ref/guide.html#mw_10928f5b-5143-48ed-a134-6cecf2b1bb6c}. Instead the Jupyter notebooks system was chosen for portability given its web browser interface, as well as existing UI element support (Figure~\ref{fig:magpie-ui}).

The user wishes to compare with Chladni patterns recorded during the course of experiments on a real plate. As such, the user chooses the 'Chladni' format option for plotting mode shapes. The `Chladni' option renders a top-down 2D visualisation of mode patterns where points at or near zero are highlighted. This allows the user to render Figure \ref{fig:bc_transitions}, which shows the effect of transition in the rotational stiffness.  From these plots obtained by MAGPIE, the user can illustrate how the mode shapes develop between simply supported and clamped cases. As expected, an increase in rotational stiffness increases modal frequency.
