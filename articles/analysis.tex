\section{Introduction}

Research into plate vibration have a long-standing and ongoing presence in the scientific literature. Plates are ubiquitous in engineering applications, particularly in acoustics, where they play a critical role in musical instruments. Soundboards of string instruments, for example, are accurately described by classical plate theories such as Kirchhoff-Love \cite{love_small_1888} and Mindlin-Reissner \cite{mindlin_influence_1951} models.

Knowledge of material parameters, particularly elastic constants, provides valuable insight into the structural integrity of a musical instrument. In the case of string instrument soundboards, elastic constants offer a quantitative measure of the instrument’s condition \cite{viala_decision_2025,duran_use_2025}. For collections of musical instruments, understanding the elastic limits of materials is essential for effective conservation \cite[p. 19-20]{barclay_care_1997}. Periodic monitoring of these constants allows curators to assess the state of a soundboard and to intervene when necessary.

Determining the material properties of a complete instrument is challenging \cite{viala_model_2019}. Conventional mechanical tests—such as bending, tensile, or compression experiments—cannot be conducted in situ and are typically destructive. In contrast, vibration-based measurements provide a minimally invasive approach, offering low-risk assessment of elastic constants.

Characterization of tonewood elastic properties can be achieved by representing eigenvectors as mode shapes (eigenshapes) \cite{ducceschi_method_2024}, complementing numerical simulations. Despite their proliferation, plate simulations are still largely accessible only through proprietary software, often beyond the budget of students or small research groups. Finite element tools, such as those employed in modelling instrument vibrations \cite{viala_model_2019}, frequently present financial or technical barriers.

This situation motivated the development of a software library, \textsc{MAGPIE}, designed for the non-invasive derivation of material parameters. \textsc{MAGPIE} is intended as an open-source platform for simulating the physics of plates, offering intuitive visualization tools and a range of analysis techniques. \textsc{MAGPIE} represents the second branch the thesis 'heritage conservation through analytical methods.' This chapter covers the the creation of the \textsc{MAGPIE} including the background plate physics, the numerical approach for simulations, and the implementation details. 

Plates are particularly well-suited for studying vibration through eigenshape visualization. They are commonly used as educational tools for demonstrating Chladni patterns \cite{chladni_treatise_2015}, providing both accurate results and visually engaging representations. Presenting eigenshapes as Chladni patterns can also be used a visual verification tool for matching conditions during a physical experiment. A platform such as \textsc{MAGPIE} can serve the dual purposes an analytical tool for researchers and industry practitioners and also a teaching aid for instructors. \textsc{MAGPIE}'s functionalities include applying elastic boundary conditions along plate edges, visualizing and exporting eigenshapes and frequencies, performing time-domain simulations of the plate equation for sound synthesis, and estimating the elastic modulus of experimental plates.

\section{Context}

The origins of \textsc{MAGPIE} extend from a set of MATLAB scripts used internally at the NEMUS projects. The MATLAB scripts were created for computing the eigenmodes for an isotropic rectangular Kirschhoff-Love plate model. Additionally, the  plate modelled is under general elastic boundary conditions, which is to say that the boundary coefficients for each edge of the plate are computed parametrically using two boundary constants representing flexural and rotational stiffness.
The script was therefore multi-modal with use cases of calculating the discrete biharmonic matrix for a finite difference scheme. This generated biharmonic can be used directly with an eigensolver for calculation of the modes and modal shapes, either for analysis or visualisation.  An impulse response of the plate can be generated through a finite difference simulation. Finally, the script was capable of being used as a tool for estimation of the Young's modulus for a real plate. For this process, an experimental setup is created where a plate is put under boundary conditions. An impulse response of the plate can be measured using an impact hammer from which the modal frequencies of the plate can be retrieved. Starting from a best guess Young's estimation, the script could then be used to simulate the physical experiment under multiple iterations with least-squares regression and provide an estimation.

Process is to get non-dimensional frequencies, least-square (LS) optimisation

\begin{verbatim}
    psi       = (Freq*2*pi).^2 * rho * Lz * A^2 ;
    DLS       = (OmNDimsq.'*psi) / (OmNDimsq.' * OmNDimsq) ;
    ELS       = DLS / (Lz^3/12/(1-nu^2)) ;
\end{verbatim}

The script consolidated calculation of the biharmonic coefficients. These coefficients were generated through MATLAB symbolic tool box. Given there generated nature, the coefficient calculations are understandably complex and can consist of over 400 arithmetic operations for a single coefficient.

The coefficient calculations are thus abstracted into bands for constructing the biharmonic in sub-blocks of pentagonal, tridiagonal, and diagonal matrices Figure~\ref{fig:biharm-subblocks}.

\section{Proposal}

The proposal was the creation of a tool \textsc{MAGPIE} which would collect together the functionality for computing a discrete biharmonic operator with parametric boundary conditions, an eigensolver, impulse response generation through modal time integration, and Young's modulus estimation. The tool could also function as a teaching aid for lecturers and as an analysis tool for researchers and industry stakeholders. \textsc{MAGPIE} was originally planned exclusively as a library for the Python programming language. Early in development, it became apparent that \textsc{MAGPIE} would have greater reach as a teaching aid and analysis tool if implemented across multiple programming languages. It was devised that  \textsc{MAGPIE} would instead be a core set of functions replicated across languages, beginning with MATLAB given its prevalence within academia and industry. The original script on which \textsc{MAGPIE} is based was written in MATLAB and could function as a know working example. This would avoid an platform specific problem that may arise during the refactoring  process. After the initial refactor in MATLAB, the set of functions could be replicated in other programming languages, ultimately Python and \textsc{C++}. The structure of \textsc{MAGPIE} would allow the ability to design interfaces separately, which would lead to a user-interface using the Jupyter notebooks platform.

\begin{equation}

% transformation of a 2D grid to a linear indexed vector. The grid co- ordinates are aligned with their corresponding linear index value. Ny is the number of grid points in the y-axis.

The plate edges can be elastically restrained \cite{li_exact_2009} by terms which control the rotation and displacement of the boundary. The biharmonic operator as in \textcite[][p. 233]{graff_wave_1991} and its discrete finite difference form in \textcite[][p. 289]{bilbao_numerical_2009} can be approximated as 

\[
\delta\Delta\Delta \approx \delta\Delta\delta\Delta = (\delta_{xx} + \delta_{yy})^{2} = \delta_{xxxx} + 2\delta_{xx}\delta_{yy} + \delta_{yyyy} 
\Delta\Delta = \frac{\delta^4}{\delta x^4} + 2\frac{\delta^4}{\delta x^2 \delta y2} + \frac{\delta^4}{\delta y^4}
\]

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{img/biharm-grid.pdf}
    \caption{Arrangement of the finite difference plate as a linear vector.}
    \label{fig:fdtd-linear}
\end{figure}

The finite difference system of the plate grid, with $N_x$ points in the x-axis and $N_y$ point in the y-axis, can be transformed in to a linear indexed vector $N_x$ by $N_y$ in length. (Figure~\ref{fdtd-linear}). The corresponding biharmonic spatial operator will then be a $N_x$ by $N_y$ square matrix the central coefficients forming the stencil and matrix shown in Figure~\ref{fig:biharmonic-stencil}. Once the boundary conditions are taken into consideration, the result is a multi-diagonal matrix with coefficient distribution as given in Figure~\ref{fig:biharm_zoom}. An in-depth formulation of the discrete biharmonic can be found in \textcite[Chapters 10-12]{bilbao_numerical_2009}.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{img/biharm_zoom.pdf}
\caption{Cross-section of the biharmonic illustrating the coefficient content for a single grid line.}\label{fig:biharm_zoom}
\end{figure} 

Given its multi-diagonal structure the biharmonic is largely empty and is well suited for sparse matrix storage formats \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992}. The coefficients would be calculated multiple times for some functionality within \textsc{MAGPIE} and it was clear that the process of assembling the bihamronic would be key for computational performance. Over 200 matrix coefficients first need to be computed before they are assembled. The original source had been designed for a block sparse format (Figure~\ref{fig:biharm-subblocks}). The block format resulted in more manageable source code, but at the cost of performance. The intention as to optimise the assembly of the biharmonic, which takes place in the \texttt{bhmat} function. Optimisation would be made easier if the coefficient calculation were separated from the biharmonic assembly stage. 
Given the complexity of the coefficient calculations, by being interwoven with another function, the potential for error would be much higher. Moving a single function without tyyping error is an easier task than moving 20 lines of code.
Separating this stage into the \texttt{bhmat} function would also ensure optimisation would cascade to any other source code which used it such as the central \texttt{magpie} function. 

Optimal construction of the operator was a key factor during \textsc{MAGPIE}'s development. 
It was necessary to declare the biharmonic operator as in matrix as this is the input format used by the interfaces to ARPACK \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998} available in both MATLAB and Python's SciPy package. Conveniently, the biharmonic operator is multi-diagonal and lends itself to the sparse matrix formats of MATLAB and SciPy.
The biharmonic operator sparsity pattern (Figure \ref{fig:biharm_zoom}) can be decomposed into several methods, of which two will be described. 
It is worth noting that explicitly declaring the set of coefficients in a loop formulation, though a valid method of using the biharmonic operator, was not considered an option for the current implementations as it does not output a matrix. 

In the first approach, the operator can be composed of sparse sub-blocks assembled separately and then concatenated later. Figure \ref{fig:bh-block} shows the internal pattern repetition of blocks and how each block can be indexed. The colour of each block illustrates the coefficients of the biharmonic affected by the plate boundaries. The central repeating section, highlighted in blue, contains the stencil. This approach simplifies code structure by avoiding the need for zero padding of the computed matrices but has implications for performance.

Using the factorisation \eqref{eq:factorisation}, diving both sides of \eqref{eq:SemiDisc1} by $\rho \zeta$ and multiplying both sides by ${\bf Q}^{-1}$, the following system results:

% \begin{equation}\label{eq:ModalSyst}
% \ddot {\bf w}(t) = -{\boldsymbol \Omega}^2 {\bf w}(t) -2 {\bf C}\dot {\bf w}(t) + \rho^{-1}\zeta^{-1}h^{-2}{\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf J}f(t),
% \end{equation}

% where ${\bf w}(t) := {\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf u}(t)$. This is a completely parallel (i.e. diagonal) system of $M$ lossy, forced oscillators, with natural frequency $\Omega_m$, $1\leq m\leq M$. Note that the loss matrix $\bf C$ can now contain different loss factors $\sigma_m$ for different modes, such as from experimental measurements.  


% One can truncate the system to an integer $N < M$, and work with the modes belonging to a given frequency band. One may, for instance, solve the eigenvalue problem \eqref{eq:SemiDisc1} using a very fine grid for increased precision but only retain the modes for which the natural frequency falls within the audible range. System \ref{eq:ModalSyst} may then be updated in time using the ``exact'' integrator for the lossy oscillator, see e.g. \cite{cieslinski_on-exact_2011} thus, yielding numerical simulations with very low numerical dispersion.

% One of the first steps for \textsc{MAGPIE} is creating a discrete biharmonic operator. The biharmonic operator sparsity pattern, described in Figure \ref{fig:PlateGhostFD},  can be decomposed into several methods, of which two will be described. It is worth noting that explicitly declaring the set of coefficients in a loop formulation, though a valid method of using the biharmonic operator, was not considered an option for the current implementations as it does not output a matrix. It is advantageous to declare the biharmonic operator as a matrix as this is the input format used by the eigensolvers in both MATLAB and Python's SciPy package utilised in other parts of MAGPIE. Conveniently, the biharmonic operator is multi-diagonal and lends itself to the sparse matrix formats of MATLAB and SciPy.\\

% The 13-point biharmonic stencil, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:PlateGhostFD}, only has 4 unique coefficients. In addition, two extra coefficients are required for the case of clamped and simply supported boundary conditions, implying that the biharmonic operator in those classical cases is composed of 6 unique coefficients. By contrast, the biharmonic operator with generalised elastic boundary conditions can contain up to 229 unique coefficients. As a result, each block or diagonal sector cannot be constructed through the repetition of a stencil pattern but must be declared explicitly. This lack of pattern repetition also impacts computational performance when constructing the operator. Since the biharmonic operator with generalised boundary condition provides very useful functionality in and of itself, the functionality to compute it is provided with an open interface as part of the library through the \texttt{bhmat} function \cite{nemus_magpie-matlab_2025}. The operator can then be used in further user-defined Finite Difference Time Domain schemes or if the users wish to take a more bespoke approach to eigenvalue/eigenvector calculation.

The ongoing work concerns the Föppl–von Kármán model for nonlinear thin plates \cite{karman_festigkeitsprobleme_1910}, which calls for the implementation of other operators in sparse matrix form. These operators are used in a variety of other models, such as the nonlinear bar and nonlinear string, which are other models that will be developed in MAGPIE. The orthotropic Kirchoff-Love plate is another direction to explore. The orthotropic model is the most interesting from a musical instrument manufacturing perspective as it would be a better approximation of wooden soundboard vibration.
