\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

The work presented in this thesis was carried out as part of the NEMUS project
\cite{nemus_description_2023}, which seeks to address the challenges of
historical musical instrument restoration through the application of digital
technologies. Two aspects of the conservation process are considered here. 
The first aspect is when a historical musical instrument has fallen out of playing condition---and
restoring it would compromise its structural or historical integrity---can
interaction be reintroduced through digitisation? Digitisation may involve
sampling an instrument
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014,paradiso_to-save_2019,tasten_project_2018} or
reconstructing it through numerical simulation
\cite{bevilacqua_sound_2023,sun_3d_2020}. Despite the emergence of these
techniques, the question of how to provide meaningful access to digitised
instruments within museum contexts remains unresolved
\cite{woodmusick_effects_2015,holzer_imperfect_2025}. The second aspect of 
conservation concerns the analysis of an instrument, in particular string 
instruments with wooden soundboards. How can we provide access to modal 
analysis and an understanding of material properties in a manner that 
Is it accessible and minimising the risk of damaging the instrument?

The renowned Tagliavini Collection exemplifies these challenges in
Bologna \cite{meer_collezione_2007}, housed at Museo San Colombano
(Figure~\ref{fig:san-colombano-interior}), comprised primarily of early Italian
plucked string keyboards. Fernando Tagliavini envisioned that the instruments in
his collection would be maintained in their original playable condition. While
the instruments are indeed playable, their use necessitates strict controls and
is permitted only under special supervision. For example, instruments such
as the Museum’s 1547 Alessandro Trasuntino harpsichord are in a state where 
restoration to playable condition poses significant risks due to their fragility.

This issue is not unique to the Tagliavini Collection but also pertains to other notable historical instrument collections, such as those of Richard Burnett,
Benton Fletcher, and Bate \cite{barclay_preservation_2005}, which all house
playable harpsichords dating from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. 
The two aspects of the conservation process relevant to these collections are
reflected in chapters titled `Analysis' and `Interaction'.

This thesis addresses these questions through two interconnected projects.
Chapter~\ref{ch:interaction} examines what occurs after digitisation has taken
place, asking: how can interaction with a digitised musical instrument be
meaningfully facilitated? Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis} focuses on the development
of an open-source software tool for `plate simulation,` designed to be used
alongside measurements to support decision-making in conservation strategies
\cite{barclay_care_1997}. 
In this context, plate simulation is the digital simulation of thin plates, 
based on physics-based models,
from which information, such as modal frequencies, can be derived.
The `plates` are a representation of the systems that can be found on the soundboards
of string instruments.
Linking these two case studies is a broader
investigation into the sustainability and reproducibility of digital
conservation and the tools that underpin it. Reproducibility poses a significant
challenge for digital research \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}. Accordingly, this
thesis outlines the steps required to ensure the longevity and accessibility of
digital research outputs
\cite{soito_citations_2016,smith_software_2016,katz_recognizing_2021,stall_journal_2023}
within the context of musical cultural heritage.

This chapter situates the work within its broader context and is divided into
two main areas. The first concerns the field of musical instrument conservation,
addressing the challenges that arise and the limitations that persist, whether
or not the wider community acknowledges them. The second examines the
term `digital conservation' as it is used throughout this thesis, focusing on
the challenges posed by digital technologies and their integration into cultural
heritage and conservation practice. In particular, this section discusses an
open methodology---in which digital research outputs are accessible, reusable,
and sustainable---as a framework for the practice of digital conservation.

\section{Musical Instrument Conservation}

Historical musical instruments present a unique challenge within the field of
cultural heritage. Instruments are both an aesthetic physical object, but also a
tool with an inherent tactile experience \cite{charalampos_role_2018}.
Maintaining instruments in playable condition introduces significant risks and
costs \cite{lamb_play_2007}, prompting ongoing debate about how best to balance
preservation and access. The discussion within the field of musical instrument
conservation has typically been split between approaches to balance competing
values of aesthetics and function \cite{barclay_care_1997,
barclay_preservation_2005}. 

The harpsichord has found itself at the centre of the `playability' debate in
more than one collection. Of note are the Benton Fletcher Collection in Fenton
House \cite{barclay_preservation_2005, mcalpine_sampling_2014}, Musical
Instruments Museums Edinburgh, and the Tagliavini Collection in San Colombano
\cite{wraight_report_2022}. Instruments from the Tagliavini collection
\cite{meer_collezione_2007}---in particular sixteenth-century instruments---were a
motivating factor and test case for part of thesis. When discussing musical
instrument conservation, it is the case of the harpsichord that is being
considered. Though some of the methodology can be translated to other
instruments, much of what is discussed is specific to the harpsichord.

\subsection{Living Heritage, Dead Heritage}

\textcite[p.~19]{barclay_preservation_2005} defines a schema of three states
into which a historical instrument may fall: Currency, Conservation, and
Restoration. The state of Currency---where the instrument is in working
condition and actively maintained---is not relevant to the concerns of this
thesis. Arguments in favour of restoration often appeal to aesthetics and sound
quality, which have historically motivated restoration practices. However, the
degree of accuracy with which instruments can be assessed varies considerably
across the spectrum of musical instruments. Focusing specifically on the
harpsichord, it will be argued that pursuing the original sound of a historical
instrument is a questionable rationale for restoration. As Barclay suggests,
museums should inform both players and audiences of the uncertainties inherent
in such reconstructions, noting that ``the speculative nature of the
instrument’s original sound'' \cite[p.~83]{barclay_care_1997} must be acknowledged.

This perspective stands in contrast to earlier views, such as those of Benton
Fletcher, who insisted that instruments should remain playable in order to avoid
becoming relics in a ``dead museum of glass cases'' \cite[p.~154, quoted in a
letter]{barclay_preservation_2005}. Fletcher’s sentiments are echoed in a later
report on the restoration of instruments within his donated collection. As relayed by
\textcite[]{barclay_critical_1999}, the report warns against instruments being
relegated to ``a temple of silence where it may be conserved as a piece of
furniture, its musical function forgotten'' \cite{adlam_proposal_1972}. The
tension between Barclay’s three states reflects a wider discourse in the
heritage sector over the past decade concerning `living heritage' versus
static, preservable `dead heritage' \cite{poulios_moving_2010,
smith_uses_2006}.

\textcite{poulios_moving_2010} critiques values-based conservation for treating
the past as dead, which creates a discontinuity between past and present
experiences. In contrast, the living heritage paradigm emphasises the ongoing,
dynamic use and relevance of heritage, advocating for the preservation not only
of physical artefacts but also of the practices, skills, and meanings that
sustain them in contemporary life.

While Poulios’ arguments are primarily directed at heritage sites and ceremonial
objects, the underlying philosophy extends to the broader category of heritage
artefacts, including musical instruments. The framing of conservation as a
strict choice between static dead heritage and perpetually living heritage
constitutes a false dichotomy.%
\footnote{``a handful dismissed the idea of
heritage as a negative idea, noting for instance that heritage was keeping that
which ought to be alive dead.'' \cite{smith_uses_2006}}
The reality is more complex: to treat heritage as endlessly living without 
acknowledging its material finitude risks producing an undead heritage, neither 
truly alive nor genuinely preserved.

In addition to those above, this work proposes a new category for musical
instruments and potentially all heritage objects that embody craft and practice:
`instructional value.' Instructional value refers to the capacity of a heritage
object to convey knowledge of practice, technique, and skill intrinsic to its
use and creation. A painting or sculpture may reveal insights into artistic
methods through `witness marks,' the traces of the artist’s hand or materials.
Similarly, a musical instrument can serve as a pedagogical tool for performers,
luthiers, and scholars, instructing through its construction, wear patterns, and
interaction how music was historically produced and experienced.

Instructional value may superficially appear to be a subset of educational or
academic value. This conflation obscures a critical distinction between
`descriptive knowledge' (knowing that) and `procedural knowledge' (knowing how).
This division is essential to resolving tensions in musical instrument
conservation, where preserving the physical object alone cannot fully capture
its performative essence without maintaining or reviving the skills and
practices it embodies. The information derived from playing an instrument is
difficult to convey to performer and who in turn find it difficult to express
\cite{lamb_play_2007}.

The discourse around conservation frequently centres on the notion of
`authenticity' \cite{pine_museums_2007, laurenson_authenticity_2006},
particularly regarding historically informed performance
\cite{davies_authenticity_2001}. Setting aside the complexities and debates
surrounding the meaning of `authenticity,' it is important to recognise that the
audience's experience often dominates discussions of musical heritage. Yet,
consider a scenario where a performer rehearses a piece 50 times before
performing to an audience of 50 who hears it once. Which value was represented
and by what magnitude in the course of the performance? This thesis argues for the
performer’s experience---informed by the instrument’s unique qualities --
and the significant instructional value derived from it, which is equal or greater 
in magnitude to those values relevant to audience. 

The performer, through hours of embodied interaction, acquires a richer, more
nuanced understanding, gained through tactile feedback and `gestural
repertoires' \cite{levinson_music_1990} intrinsic to playing the instrument.
The instrument shapes the performance as much as the performer shapes the music.
This interplay is especially salient when comparing, for instance, a modern
Steinway piano with a historical harpsichord, where differences in touch, sound
production, and response create distinct musical experiences. Put simply, this is
yet another example of McLuhan’s maxim that ``the medium is the message''
\cite{mcluhan_understanding_1964}.

Conventional museum practice rarely permits visitors to handle or play
historical instruments. Though instruments may be in `playing condition' they
are not typically playable by the average museum visitor. Instead, instruments
cordoned off behind a---real or imagined---``red velvet cord,''
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014} displayed as objects to be seen but not touched.
This approach robs visitors of the experience of vibrotactile and kinaesthetic
cues encoded into the interface of the instrument. The challenge, then, is how
to reintroduce embodied interaction with historical instruments in ways that
respect conservation imperatives while restoring some degree of their living
function.

On the z`priorities for future collecting'' for the harpsichord collection at
Musical Instrument Museums Edinburgh from \citetitle{uoe_collections_2020} \cite{uoe_collections_2020}:

\begin{quotation}
    The collection is generally considered to have the widest scope of any in
    the world. Each item is important for reasons relevant to research and
    teaching, and in some cases, performance potential. 
\end{quotation}

For instruments that originated from a fixed point in time (e.g. sixteenth-century
harpsichords), `performance potential' is becoming less likely. A holistic
conservation framework for musical instruments must integrate both values-based
and living heritage perspectives. Such a synthesis acknowledges the physical
realities of ageing instruments while honouring their performative and
instructional roles. This integration opens pathways to novel conservation
strategies that extend beyond current paradigms.

\subsection{Reproduction or Restoration}\label{sec:reproduction-or-restoration}

Musical instrument states of Currency and Restoration must contend with a Ship
of Theseus problem: how much alteration can the original instrument undergo
before it ceases to be considered the same object? The drawback of restoration
is twofold: the process can be irreversibly destructive both to the instrument’s
longevity \cite{barclay_care_1997} and to the surviving evidence of original
craftsmanship \cite{karp_restoration_1979}. If the authenticity of performance
requires the use of an original instrument \cite{karp_musical_1985}, and if
``every restoration [...] wipes away evidence and makes the original [...]
condition more remote'' (from \cite{waitzman_music_1988} quoted in
\cite{barclay_critical_1999}), then there exists only a finite reservoir of
authentic music to be played. This is not a sustainable long-term approach for
enabling performance on historical instruments.

Barclay has been critical of restoration \cite{barclay_care_1997}, while at the
same time expressing caution regarding the notion of an `authentic copy'
\cite{barclay_preservation_2005}. For a replica to be of instructional value,
Barclay appears to assume---perhaps mistakenly---that we still have to make this
assemblage of materials into a working musical instrument, comfortable and
useful to players of the twenty-first century \cite{barclay_preservation_2005}. This
assumption risks overlooking the possibility that it is from the ``gestural
repertoires'' \cite{levinson_music_1990} of the original instrument that the
most meaningful insights may be gained.

Approaches to historical instruments that prioritise either strict conservation
or reproduction preserve the informational integrity of the original and thus
avoid the Ship of Theseus problem altogether. Even so, reproduction is not
without contention. Barclay cites two cases of copying---a recorder and a
natural trumpet---to address the broader issue of fidelity to measurements, a
concern also raised by \textcite{koster_exact_1994}. In raising the ``thorny
issue'' \cite{barclay_preservation_2005} of reproduction, Barclay draws on
\textcite{koster_restoration_1996}, who argues not from aesthetics but from
tonal quality. In evaluating a 1785 Jacques Germain harpsichord copy, Koster
concludes: ``... a direct comparison of the Germain harpsichord and its copy
shows that the tone of the copy does not quite match the surpassing elegance of
the original'' \cite{koster_restoration_1996}. The conclusion that the older
instrument sounded superior to its copy is echoed by contemporaries,%
\footnote{A Stradivari has the ability to deliver the whole range of tonal colors [...]''
[...] Simon Morris, the director of Beare’s... New violins don’t have the range
of tonal colour. The depth of personality isn’t there [...]''
\cite{mead_musical_2014}} 
and even a team of museum experts may hesitate before creating a copy.

However, given recent studies by \textcite{fritz_player_2012,
fritz_soloist_2014, fritz_listener_2017}---which demonstrate that the acoustic
quality of older violins, including Guarneri and Stradivari, deteriorates over
time---Koster’s conclusion may be unsound. His objections to copies also extend
beyond acoustics: they might deceive by being misrepresented as originals, or
deviate from the original simply through the act of replication. Yet an argument
against reproduction based on acoustics must be tested empirically. To
demonstrate that a copy sounds inferior, an ideal study would require a
double-blind design. Great care is also necessary to clarify exactly what is
being assessed.%
\footnote{``Of course, the community didn't like these results.
And so, the study was heavily criticised. We basically got killed on the
Internet, on phones, like What have you done?', How can you disrespect
Stradivarius so much?' People were really not happy, and then they found all
different excuses to justify that the study was necessarily badly conducted,
starting with the fact that we definitely chose a bad Stradivarius, which was
already a good move because, I mean, now people were happy to acknowledge that
there could be a bad Stradivarius. That was always something going forward.''
\cite{fritz_musica_2017}}

``That's where the 'accurate copy' inevitably falls from grace. And this is, of
course, grist for the mills of those who argue for restoring as opposed to
copying.'' In light of recent scientific studies, it would appear that neither
approach can be accurate. The limitations in copying is no longer ``grist for the
mills'' \cite[p.~81]{barclay_preservation_2005} for restorers as that approach cannot be deemed `authentic' for all
instruments either, and is detrimental to other heritage values an instrument
can hold. An ``authentic ruin'' providing more valuable information than a
``falsely stored original'' \cite[Citing Martin Skowroneck][p
78]{barclay_preservation_2005}. This was already apparent in 1985 when Karp
wrote: ``it is becoming obvious that restored older instruments do not
necessarily behave or sound as they did when they were new''
\cite{karp_musical_1985}.

There is also an implication in this rhetoric that the original maker of these
historical instruments could perfectly: ``a true acoustical copy''
\cite{koster_exact_1994}. In the work the \textcite{viala_simultaneous_2020},
material variability and the affects of geometry means that, if the goal is
indeed an ``acoustical copy'' the construction will likely deviate from the
original. The environmental impact of musical instrument fabrication should not
be discounted \cite{allen_fatto_2012}, and this suggests that replicating or
restoring an instrument is a false dichotomy and that environmental concerns
mean neither is feasible at scale.

\subsection{Conservation and Sustainability}

Musical instrument restoration and reproduction do not exist in isolation.
Instruments require raw materials for their manufacture, and many of the
materials historically employed are no longer readily available, or may soon
become scarce. As part of the broader discussion on the preservation of musical
instrument heritage, consideration should also be given to environmental
concerns.

The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and
Flora (CITES) is a multilateral treaty established to protect animal and plant
species threatened by international trade. CITES maintains three Appendices,
which classify species according to the degree of threat they face, with
Appendix I representing the highest level of protection and Appendix III the
lowest. Among the species listed under these Appendices are several materials
traditionally used in the manufacture of musical instruments.

The use of these woods, and the implications of their inclusion in the CITES
Appendices for the musical instrument industry, are far from trivial. For
example, a large seizure of illegally harvested Pernambuco, used in the
manufacture of violin bows, was reported in Brazil \cite{toledo_operation_2022}.
Similarly, in 2008, the Gibson Guitar Company was implicated in the import of
illegally harvested wood from Madagascar \cite{justice_gibson_2012}. Such cases
highlight the environmental impact that the musical instrument industry can
exert on endangered ecosystems.

As more tree species become at risk and are brought under the protection of
CITES, many of the materials historically employed in instrument making are no
longer legally obtainable. Beyond legality, it is also financially and
ecologically unsustainable to maintain historical instruments in their original
form on a large scale. With the environmental consequences of musical instrument
conservation in view, the binary choice between replication and restoration
appears increasingly untenable. Neither option, pursued at scale, is financially
or ethically viable. Moreover, the number of historical instruments that could
realistically undergo such processes is limited, raising a further question: who
decides which instruments are deemed worthy of replication or restoration?

\subsection{A Third Path}\label{sec:third-path}

\textcite{desilvey_curated_2017} puts forward the question ``what could be
gained if we were to care for the past without pickling it[?]'' If heritage can
be in a state of living---and a conservation approach is akin to `dead'
\cite{poulios_moving_2010}---then there must also be a transition phase where
heritage dies and moves from one state to the other. Rather than resisting and
fearing this transition, \textcite{desilvey_curated_2017} suggests that we might
embrace this natural event instead. \textcite[p 75]{barclay_preservation_2005}
touches on this same idea as he discusses `benign neglect' where the
preservation of playing state---or Currency---is passive but the decision to do
so is deliberate. We might then turn to other approaches to maintaining the
idea of an instrument. Rather than striving for the ``exact copy'' we can
instead consciously and deliberately seek an `imperfect' that elicits the
``exploring the social, political, historical, conceptual, contextual'' aspects
of musical instrument heritage \cite{holzer_imperfect_2025}.

What will be presented is an approach to historical harpsichords conservation
that is equal parts reconstruction, emulation and re-enactment of the original
interface. By creating this new augmented interface, there is still a question
of how the new artefact is conserved as it ages. If the same process is applied
where the copy is preserved and reconstructed, then it is `harpsichords all the
way down.' Instead, this new interface should be considered finite, and the
focus of conservation should not be on the interface but rather the instructions
for re-creating it.

For this reason, the term `conservation,' rather than `preservation,' is used
deliberately throughout this thesis in relation to musical instrument heritage.
As the discussion above has shown, the working lifespan of an instrument---the
period during which it can provide instructional value---is finite. To attempt
to `preserve' a specific instrument with instructional value in mind is a futile
endeavour. What can be achieved, at best, is conservation: sustaining the
instrument for as long as possible through careful treatment.


\section{Digital Conservation}

Having outlined the broader challenges of musical instrument conservation, this section considers the role of digital technology within that context under the term `digital conservation.' Although the phrase more commonly appears in biodiversity and environmental work \cite{vanderwal_digital_2015}, here it is recast with reference to ``digital heritage'' \cite{unesco_heritage_2003}, ``digital preservation'' \cite{garrett_preserving_1996}, and ``digital cultural heritage'' \cite{fresa_digital_2014}.

To clarify the terminology used throughout the thesis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital preservation} is the process of creating and maintaining digital surrogates of artefacts for the purpose of a persistent record.
    \item \textbf{Digital heritage} comprises all ``digital materials of enduring value'' \cite{unesco_heritage_2003}, including documents, datasets, and software.
    \item \textbf{Digital cultural heritage} refers to heritage artefacts that have undergone digitisation, with specific reference to their digital instances.
\end{itemize}

Digital conservation is a compound problem. It inherits the systemic issues of digital preservation while introducing domain-specific challenges for musical instruments, notably the difficulty of preserving the tactile, embodied experience of performance. As discussed previously, this is not a uniquely digital problem, but it becomes more acute when interaction is mediated through screens, sensors, and code.

The heritage sector’s use of digital technology is far from settled. Immersive media and interactive systems are frequently heralded as transformative \cite{gedert_technology_2017, li_systematic_2024, dutot_changing_2021}, yet the question remains: have museums been transformed by digital technologies, or deployed them with an analogue mindset? The digital should not replace the physical artefact; it should extend the artefact’s reach and interpretive context \cite{foster_defining_1998, parry_museums_2010}. Digitisation is not a single act but a continuing process \cite{rubio_work_2020, kelley_digital_2018}. Both digital and analogue collections depend on physical media \cite{rubio_work_2020}, and the financial and energy costs of keeping bits alive can exceed those of maintaining an analogue equivalent \cite{cieply_afterlife_2007}. By example, \citetitle{science_digital_2007} \cite{science_digital_2007} reported in 2007 that the archiving of a physical film was \$1059 while the equivalent digital archiving cost was an order of magnitude higher at \$12514.
\footnote{``Based on an annual cost of \$500 per terabyte of fully managed storage of 3 copies of an 8.3 terabyte 4K digital master.'' \cite{science_digital_2007}}
% Alongside the digital storage comes the risks of bit rot and obsolescence.
% Museum professionals need adequate training to have an understanding of digital data and its inherent problems of obsolescence and silent corruption \cite{rubio_work_2020,schwarz_disk_2006, parry_museums_2010}.

One clear advantage of the digital domain is the aggregation of data from separate sources. Projects such as Musical Instrument Museums Online (MIMO) collate catalogue data from distributed collections, improving access and enabling comparison at scale. The usefulness of such systems hinges on data quality and stewardship \cite{dolan_mimo_2017}, which in turn demand multidisciplinary skills. Museum professionals require training in data management, obsolescence, and silent corruption \cite{rubio_work_2020,schwarz_disk_2006,parry_museums_2010}; across the humanities, gaps remain in sustainable data practices \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}.

A further consideration concerns the `digital heritage we create:' software, datasets, and workflows produced during conservation. If instruments are preserved digitally, the tools of preservation must themselves be preservable. Software reproducibility is a persistent problem \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}, with processes becoming irreproducible after surprisingly short periods \cite{robert_reproducibility_2020}.

The digital components of this thesis therefore follow current best-practice guidelines. The remainder of the section outlines the organisational approach and motivations behind the chosen methodology.

\subsection{Open Methods}\label{sec:open-methods}

Addressing digital musical instrument conservation requires frameworks that speak to preservation, access, and reuse. Open Science \cite{unesco_open_2021} provides a foundation by encouraging the open sharing not only of results%
\footnote{Examples include original research outputs, research data, software, source code, workflows and protocols, and digital representations of pictorial and graphical materials \cite{unesco_open_2021}.}
but also of the processes by which those results are obtained. Digital technologies make such openness feasible, but they also require structure. The FAIR principles---Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable---offer a practical framework for digital assets \cite{wilkinson_fair_2016}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Findability:} digital assets carry rich metadata and persistent identifiers.
\item \textbf{Accessibility:} access protocols are open, free, and universally implementable.
\item \textbf{Interoperability:} data and tools integrate across heterogeneous systems with minimal effort.
\item \textbf{Reuse:} assets are released with clear, accessible licensing and provenance.
\end{itemize}

For software, FAIR also implies citability \cite{soito_citations_2016}. This requires action by authors \cite{smith_software_2016}, by those citing software \cite{katz_recognizing_2021}, and by journals that must cross-reference software artefacts \cite{stall_journal_2023}. Open software is ideally ``living,'' yet research demands that specific versions be discoverable and stable. Version control (e.g., \texttt{git}) allows releases to be tagged, while public repositories (e.g., GitHub) aid discoverability. For long-term reference, archival deposition (e.g., Zenodo) provides persistent identifiers (DOIs) \cite{smith_software_2016,stall_journal_2023}.

Persistence is one side of sustainability; reproducibility is the other \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}. Software should run now and in the future, despite evolving languages, libraries, and hardware, and despite more mundane obstacles such as incomplete documentation \cite{robert_reproducibility_2020}.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{hardware-reproducibility}

Digitally designed `hardware' introduces additional challenges. In digital musical instrument (DMI) design, long-term reuse remains difficult \cite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}. Hardware projects typically combine electronics schematics, CAD/CAM assets, bills of materials, and assembly/operation documentation; these components must be archived together in a well-structured repository \cite{calegario_documentation_2021}. CAD formats and toolchains themselves are preservation risks. During this thesis, Autodesk announced the discontinuation of the EAGLE EDA tool \cite{autodesk_eagle_2024} for July 2026. A deliberate decision was made to continue using EAGLE’s XML format: much as Python~2’s end-of-life ``calcified'' that environment for archival purposes \cite{rougier_loupe_2020}, EAGLE’s stable format remains processable by other tools such as KiCad \cite{kicad_eagle_2024}.

\textcite{perkel_challenge_2020} aggregates guidance from the ReScience~C ten-year challenge on reviving decade-old research software. Key recommendations---version control, archival deposits, and explicit documentation---align with wider best practice \cite{smith_software_2016, stall_journal_2023}.

All digital assets in this thesis are released under the GPLv3 licence, following recommendations to provide permissions as openly as possible \cite{stall_journal_2023}. GPLv3 permits copying, distribution, and modification, with the requirement that derivatives remain under the same licence \cite{gplv3}. Repositories were structured in line with Software Sustainability Institute guidance and underwent assessment by the Institute at the University of Edinburgh \cite{ssi_online_2025}.

\subsection{Case Study: Aurora}\label{sec:aurora}

During the course of this PhD, Professor Angelo Farina---a prolific acoustics researcher and author of several free analysis tools---passed away. Among his contributions, the Aurora suite provides a pertinent case study in software sustainability: it has enduring value and has supported digital cultural heritage projects for decades \cite{fausti_acoustic_2000,frey_acoustical_2013,almagropastor_assessing_2021}.

Aurora originated in 1995 \cite{farina_auralization_1995} as an extension to Cool Edit \cite{tronchin_acoustics_1997} (later Adobe Audition \cite{farina_listening_2004}) and was initially distributed with Farina’s Ramsete room-acoustics simulator \cite{farina_aurora_1995} via shareware sites. Its broad adoption followed the addition of sine-sweep impulse response measurement \cite{farina_aurora_2000}. In 2009, Simone Campanini ported Aurora to the open-source editor Audacity \cite{farina_new_2009}, ending the ``Russian roulette'' shareware model \cite{farina_registration_2007} and making Aurora freely usable.

Aurora remains widely cited \cite{fontana_highly_2024,popp_speech_2024,denti_pan_2024}, and development continued as late as 2023 \cite{bevilacqua_new_2023}. However, the absence of consistent software citation complicates provenance: inferring versions from publication dates is imprecise, and substantial code changes may affect comparability of results. In response, a small conservation effort---in collaboration with Campanini and Farina---uploaded the Audacity variant’s source to a public version-controlled repository, under GPLv3 with an assigned DOI \cite{farina_aurora_2025}, in line with best practice \cite{stall_journal_2023, hong_how_2019}.

Technically, Audacity supports extension through an experimental module system.\footnote{\url{https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/modules.html}} Modules must be co-compiled with the host source and version-checked at load time. This strategy enabled distribution of a single working editor with Aurora integrated, but it also intertwined module and host code, making Aurora vulnerable to breaking changes as Audacity evolved, particularly after its 2021 partnership with Muse Group \cite{prokoudine_ultimate_2021}. Consequently, Aurora’s long-term availability depends on maintaining build compatibility.

Farina’s websites hosted Aurora binaries,\footnote{\url{https://www.angelofarina.it/Aurora/download}} but such hosting is fragile.\footnote{At the time of writing, Farina’s University of Parma site (\url{http://pcfarina.eng.unipr.it}) is down, though it has been mirrored at \url{https://www.angelofarina.it}.} The Audition-era source is not public; the current repository is therefore a partial historical snapshot. This is not unique. The YIN pitch-detection code by de~Cheveign\'e \cite{cheveigne_yin_2002} faces similar issues: widely used, historically hosted at unstable URLs,\footnote{\url{http://www.ircam.fr/pcm/cheveign/sw/yin.zip} (defunct); see also \url{http://audition.ens.fr/adc/sw/yin.zip}.} and often cited via papers rather than software artefacts. When the `software'---not merely the algorithm---is integral to research, the record should include an accessible, citable source package.

These examples point to an institutional role: research organisations could host software repositories, assign DOIs, and provide light-touch stewardship for outputs arising from funded work. Documentation is also central to sustainability \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}; even basic steps such as adding a licence can improve community engagement \cite{colazo_licence_2009}. Lessons from Aurora and YIN inform the project structure, documentation, and digital outputs presented in this thesis.

\section{Free Open Source Software for Acoustics Research}

% Before introducing the \textsc{magpie} framework, this section surveys free and open-source software (FOSS) in acoustics with demonstrable relevance to heritage work, whether through measurement of historical spaces \cite{tronchin_acoustics_1997,fagerlande_adequaccao_2020} or digital reconstruction \cite{katz_exploring_2020}. The aim is not exhaustiveness but to indicate current practice and where the community might develop further.

The preceding discussion highlighted the fragility of digital heritage and the responsibilities that follow from creating new digital tools. Having shown how issues of access, licensing, documentation, and long-term stewardship affect the preservation of software itself, it is necessary to consider the broader ecosystem into which such tools are released. This section, therefore, surveys free and open-source software (FOSS) relevant to acoustics research, with particular attention to projects that intersect with heritage practice—whether through analysis, simulation, reconstruction, or measurement of instruments and spaces. The aim is not to catalogue the field exhaustively but to understand the current landscape into which new work is introduced, identify patterns of good practice and recurring shortcomings, and clarify where further development is needed.


\subsection{Software Documentation}

Following \textcite{procida_diataxis_2025}, we can distinguish four complementary forms of documentation: Tutorials, How-to guides, Explanation, and Reference.
These forms of documentation can be naturally split to address two audiences: user-facing and developer-facing.
Dividing \textcite{procida_diataxis_2025}, developer-facing documentation lies in How-To and Reference forms and the user-facing in tutorials and explanation. 
This division of documentation is not formed by hard boundaries, as the definition of a binary audience does not hold true in practice. 
Instead the audience could more precisely be described as on a user-developer spectrum. 
Reference documentation can be generated from source annotations to avoid duplication and to support automation.
Across M\textsc{atlab}, Python, and C\textsc{++} there are multiple conventions \cite{vanrossum_pep8_2001,goodger_pep257_2001}, and several tooling ecosystems coexist (e.g. Doxygen, Sphinx, HeaderDoc).
\footnote{See the Stack Overflow data explorer for indicative tag counts: \url{https://data.stackexchange.com/stackoverflow/query/1069131/get-all-tags}.}
Even so, generated API references should not be a substitute for user manuals \cite[Section~6.2]{stallman_gnu_1992}, but this is often the case \cite{procida_diataxis_2025}.
\footnote{\url{https://diataxis.fr/reference/}}
% Nevertheless, reference documentation is a valuable resource for a project and can still be used to inform the remaining forms of documentation.
% \footnote{``The only good way to use documentation strings in writing a good manual is to use them as a source of information for writing good text.'' \cite{stallman_gnu_1992}}

Best practice emphasises the importance of documentation \cite{smith_software_2016}, though it is less prescriptive about form and depth. 
\textcite{writethedocs_sowftware_2025} offers comprehensive guidance. A pragmatic rule is that documentation should enable you to still ``understand your code in six months'' \cite{writethedocs_sowftware_2025}, a theme underscored by reproducibility studies \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}. 
Using the \citep{ssi_checklist_2018} checklist, the core requirements for an open research software project are: publicly available source, a version control system (VCS), user and developer documentation, an OSI-compliant licence, and persistent identifiers (typically DOIs). 
A public VCS implies source availability; the reverse is not guaranteed. 
Likewise, a DOI implies some level of digital archiving, but a release archive does not necessitate a DOI. DOIs should be assigned both to the software as a whole and to individual releases \cite{smith_software_2016}; the licence should be clearly stated and OSI-compliant \cite{hong_choosing_2025}.
To better serve the complexity of software citation, \textcite{druskat_citation_2021} created the citation file format (CFF). CFF files aim to standardise the meta-data structure for software citations and are supported by both the GitHub and Zenodo platforms. Recommendation in \textcite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} for hardware projects, with tools for citation \cite{spaaks_cffconvert_2021}. Recommendations beyond these basics (contribution policies, issue tracking, codes of conduct) presuppose an active repository. The following subsections consider seven projects in this light.

\subsubsection{Aurora}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:aurora}, Aurora began in 1995 and became fully open only with the 2009 Audacity port \cite{farina_new_2009}. The project now has an OSI-compliant licence and a citable release with DOI \cite{farina_aurora_2025}. Developer documentation remains sparse; a user manual exists but is not currently set up for community contribution.
\footnote{\url{https://www.aurora-plugins.com/Package.htm}}

\subsubsection{ITA Toolbox}

The ITA Toolbox emerged around 2010 \cite{dietrich_matlab_2010}, moved to RWTH Aachen’s GitLab in 2016, and adopted an explicit open-source model in 2017 \cite{berzborn_ita_2017}. The toolbox can be viewed as a successor to Aurora for measurement and analysis \cite{dietrich_influence_2013}. Developer documentation within M\textsc{atlab} is extensive, but---as warned by \textcite{stallman_gnu_1992}---``doc strings'' are not a full user manual. Public-facing materials correspond mostly to "Tutorials" in \citetitle{procida_diataxis_2025} \cite{procida_diataxis_2025}; other forms are more limited. Recommended citations are tied to publications; releases are available via the institutional GitLab. The toolbox depends on proprietary M\textsc{atlab} toolboxes, narrowing its audience. Its BSD-4-Clause variant is not currently OSI-approved.\footnote{\url{https://spdx.org/licenses/BSD-4-Clause.html}} Despite these caveats, it remains a well-maintained institutional open-source effort and has been used in heritage case studies \cite{fagerlande_adequaccao_2020}. A Zenodo DOI has been suggested \cite{berzborn_ita_2017} but not, at the time of writing, implemented which is possibly as a result of the BSD-4-Clause's non-compliance with Zenodo's license requirements \cite{zenodo_metadata_2025}.

\subsubsection{ITEMM OpenLab}

The ITEMM OpenLab platform\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com}} provides resources for makers, including datasets, CAD models, and workshop-oriented calculators. Of particular relevance, the \textit{Rigidité de plaques} tool implements \textcite{mcintyre_measuring_1988} for plate elastic constants.\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/calculateur-mecanique/rigidite-de-plaques-1/}} While accessible after registration, the model is distribution-free rather than open-source; code delivered to the browser can be inspected but is not clearly licensed for reuse.
\footnote{See \texttt{js/p\_w\_script.js} and \texttt{js/olt\_class.js}.} 
Legal terms cover 3D models under CC-BY-SA,\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/mentions-legals/}} but do not explicitly extend to calculators \cite{cc_can_2025}. By contrast, \textsc{magpie} follows an ``open first'' approach aligned with FAIR \cite{wilkinson_fair_2016,go_fair_2016}, aiming for sustainability, interoperability, and citability (as defined by \textcite{soito_citations_2016}). The intent is FLOSS rather than merely ``open source'' \cite{stallman_floss_2021}---the inclusion of an explicit `L' for libre to encompass the two meanings of `free.'

\subsubsection{NeSS}

The Next Generation Sound Synthesis (NeSS) project at the University of Edinburgh employed GPU acceleration for physically based synthesis of instruments and acoustics, running from 2012--2017. During this thesis, the source was released under MIT on GitHub, with documentation covering build steps and partial structure,
\footnote{\url{https://github.com/Edinburgh-Acoustics-and-Audio-Group/ness/tree/main/doc}} 
and an associated website of explanations and examples \cite{ness_welcome_2016}. Given the recent opening, citations outside the original project are currently limited.

\subsubsection{PFFDTD}

PFFDTD \cite{hamilton_pffdtd_2021} provides open-source room-acoustics simulation driven from SketchUp models,\footnote{\url{https://sketchup.trimble.com}} using CUDA for GPU acceleration and open languages (Ruby, Python, C) elsewhere. Documentation includes references and installation guidance; a concise how-to is provided. The project suggests a standard citation---a positive step for traceability---though a DOI would further improve citability. Tools of this kind support virtual exploration of cultural heritage spaces \cite{katz_exploring_2020, tronchin_acoustics_1997, cook_hearing_2023}.

\subsubsection{VK-Gong}

VK-Gong (ENSTA Paris) simulates nonlinear plates and shells relevant to gongs, cymbals, and bells. M\textsc{atlab} and C\textsc{++} sources are hosted via an institutional site, with an unusually comprehensive M\textsc{atlab} user manual \cite{vkgong_manual_2017}. The site lists CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0,\footnote{\url{https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/}} though the licence text is absent from source files; CC licences are discouraged for software by both Creative Commons and OSI \cite{cc_can_2025}. No public VCS is used, limiting collaboration and issue tracking. Platform-specific optimisations (Intel SSE intrinsics) hinder portability, particularly to Arm processors; without a VCS, workarounds must be rediscovered by each user. Despite these hurdles, VK-Gong has been used in research \cite{nguyen_nonlinear_2019}, though it is more difficult to track in comparison to the other examples.

\subsubsection{YIN}

The YIN pitch detector \cite{cheveigne_yin_2002} is widely adopted, with M\textsc{atlab} code commonly used in studies (e.g. \cite{bowen_assessing_2019}). The software lacks a stable URI or DOI and carries a ``FOR RESEARCH PURPOSES'' restriction,\footnote{\url{https://github.com/mhamilt/yin/blob/main/LICENCE}} which is not OSI-compliant. Documentation is primarily the original paper (\emph{Explanation} in \cite{procida_diataxis_2025}); developer reference is absent. Technical friction arises from M\textsc{atlab} MEX changes post-R2016a \cite{matlab_mex_2025}, requiring users to rebuild binaries individually.

\subsection{Summary of Acoustics Software Practice}

Across these seven projects there is a clear movement towards openness, but uneven adherence to best practice. Table~\ref{tab:foss-compliance} summarises the status with respect to source availability, version control, documentation, licensing, and persistent identifiers. Two trends stand out. First, persistent identifiers are largely absent; where present, suggested citations help but do not replace DOIs \cite{hornikx_exploring_2024}. Second, developer documentation is thin; most projects assume technically experienced users, which narrows adoption outside core acoustics communities. Licensing is generally present, though some projects apply non-OSI licences (or CC variants) in ways that limit reuse \cite{cc_can_2025,hong_choosing_2025}. Finally, software evolves: dual DOIs (project and release) clarify which version underpinned a result, and make bugs or behavioural changes tractable in retrospect.

The GNU Software Freedoms \cite{stallman_free_1996} can be summarised as the
freedom to run, copy, study, and modify FOSS. 
From Table\ref{tab:foss-compliance} we can see that the freedoms to run, copy,
and study are well represented in the research software discussed, but the last (to modify)
is the most underserved. Any source code can be modified if it is publicly accessible, but to do so one needs to first
understand it and bare code does not typically provide adequate information.
From the lack of universal version control and reference documentation,
we can surmise that there is little to no intention of external development for the software discussed above.
This conclusion is also backed by the absence of DOIs as when the software is not `alive,' there is no reason to cite it directly. 
Static software, on the other hand, can be served by suggested paper citations as it will function the same as it did at the point of publication.
% At least, in theory, but as has been shown in the case of Aurora, and to a lesser extent YIN, technology moves and with that movement source code breaks.

The projects in this thesis, the keyboard interfaces and \textsc{magpie}, aim to model FLOSS-aligned research practice: public VCS, explicit licensing, structured documentation for users and developers, and archival deposition with DOIs. The thesis and associated papers \cite{hamilton_augmentation_2025,hamilton_magpie_2024} comprise the explanatory documentation and the remaining documentation as part of the repositories \cite{hamilton_nemus_2025,hamilton_cad_2025,hamilton_models_2025,hamilton_firmware_2025,magpie_matlab_2025,magpie_python_2025,magpie_cpp_2025} and online reference manuals \cite{hamilton_documentation_2025,hamilton_matlab_2025,hamilton_python_2025}.

\section{Thesis Outline}

This thesis describes to two digital projects, each of which addresses an aspect
of the conservation pipeline. Chapter~\ref{ch:interaction} considers musical heritage 
once digital conservation has taken place and asks the question: how do
you facilitate interaction with a digitised musical instrument?
Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis} represents the beginning where analysis of materials
take place, in the case of the harpsichord, which can be used alongside
measurements to make further decisions about conservation routes
\cite{barclay_care_1997}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis} discusses the steps taken for sustainability with a
purely software project where a distinct functional software library and
interface library were created for the MAGPIE software.  For electronic
hardware, the problem of sustainability is compounded by the
addition of physical components, their schematics and assembly on top of the
firmware and software required to make the hardware function. Process for
addressing this is covered further in Chapter~\ref{ch:interaction}.

Binding these projects together is the open methodology, the system of methods,
tools and processes that should be considered as part of any digital cultural
heritage project. This thesis aims to demonstrate the considerations necessary to
adhere to these four FAIR principles, particularly within a cross-disciplinary
project. All digital assets—whether source code, measurement data, or CAD
files—are managed accordingly.

The digital output of this thesis has conformed to the following requirements.
All digital assets have been put under version control using the git version
control system. Version controlled repositories are stored on the GitHub
platform to allow for public access. When a release of the repository is
created, it is integrated with the Zenodo archive from which a DOI is generated.

With respect to the project repositories, a `release' is defined as any
self-contained, working version of an asset that was used in the course of
research. Digital assets are living and as such are constantly changing as they
transition from one release to the next.

\subsection{Chapter 2: Interaction}

Chapter~\ref{ch:interaction} discusses the creation of a model harpsichord
interface that was augmented with an electronic optical sensor system to allow
for interaction with digital software instruments. Discussed within the chapter
are the design, prototyping and fabrication stages as well as the final
deployment as part of an exhibition at Museo San Colombano, Bologna
\cite{san_colombano_2025}.

Technological advances have reshaped the ways museums document, present, and
interpret their collections. Immersive experiences are now made possible through
tools such as 3D printing and virtual reality, but such experiences can place a
veil over the user and remove, rather than engage them with the heritage. These
technologies are capable of facilitating experiential authenticity, yet
achieving it requires museums to carefully negotiate the balance between
preservation and meaningful engagement \cite{pine_museums_2007}—a tension that
is especially pronounced with historical musical instrument collections
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}.

Musical instruments occupy a distinctive position within cultural heritage: they
are not only aesthetic artefacts but also functional tools with an inherently
tactile dimension \cite{charalampos_role_2018}. Over time, however, they
inevitably deteriorate to the point where they become too fragile for continued
handling \cite{paradiso_to-save_2019}. This places museums in a persistent
dilemma between accessibility and conservation, often limiting how visitors can
interact with collections \cite{templeton_museum_2018,mcalpine_sampling_2014}.
The key question, then, is how to preserve not just the physical object, but
also the embodied experience of playing it.

This chapter addresses that question by presenting the design and creation of a
replica harpsichord keyboard with a typical early Italian layout, electronically
augmented for use in an interactive exhibition. Moving beyond the constraints of
the traditional “red velvet cord”
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014,masu_o-in-nime_2023}, the replica aims to enhance
visitor interaction while protecting fragile originals. Rather than focusing on
technical novelty, the project demonstrates how digital interventions can foster
meaningful engagement with shared musical heritage, helping to sustain its
relevance in contemporary museum practice.

The resulting exhibition, hosted at Museo San Colombano in Bologna, provided
visitors with a tactile interface to digitised versions of instruments in the
collection. It built on the understanding that human perception of music is
strongly shaped by visual cues
\cite{fritz_player_2012,fritz_soloist_2014,fritz_listener_2017,tsay_sight_2013}.
The installation sought to balance historical authenticity, accessibility, and
preservation, while contributing to broader debates on the role of technology in
museums. The chapter discusses the keyboard’s design principles, technical
implementation, and implications, highlighting its potential for deepening
public engagement and supporting the long-term preservation of musical heritage.

Chapter~\ref{ch:interaction} begins by looking at related work over the last 40
years in the field of haptic keyboard musical interfaces and musical heritage
interaction in museums. This specific context is then related to the wider
discussion of digital cultural heritage and musical instrument conservation (as
discussed above), its influence on the interface design process, and the
constraints that arise from this wider context. The project is
separated into its discrete stages from design process, through the prototyping
process and finally the fabrication of two final interfaces: one interface for
exhibition at Museo San Colombano and the second for further research as part of
the NEMUS project.   

\subsection{Chapter 3: Analysis}

Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis} looks at the  the creation of an open source software
tool for plate vibration analysis, material property estimation and musical
acoustics education. Although applications of plate simulation continue to
expand, access remains largely limited to specialised, proprietary
software—often prohibitively expensive for students or even smaller research
groups. This work presents the theoretical background and initial implementation
of \textsc{magpie}, an open-source platform for simulating plate physics.
\textsc{magpie} provides intuitive visualisation tools and a range of analysis
methods, making it useful both as a teaching aid for instructors and as a
research tool for academics and industry professionals. Its features include
defining elastic boundary conditions along plate edges, visualising and
exporting eigenshapes and corresponding frequencies, running time-domain
simulations of the plate equation for sound synthesis, and estimating the
elastic constants of experimental plates. This final function of the elastic
constants forms the basis of a tool for soundboard analysis to aid luthiers and
conservators of historical string and keyboard instruments. 

\textsc{magpie} would provide a valuable data point, giving insight into the
internal condition of wood as through its elastic constants in a non-destructive
and inexpensive manner without a requirement for costly finite-element software.
In tandem, \textsc{magpie} strives to make open and accessible the scientific
tools developed in the NEMUS laboratory which have been adapted to simplify use
and lower the technical barrier of entry.

Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis} begins by discussing the existing software for plate
analysis and the core mathematical problems that need to be solved. The
development of \textsc{magpie} is then discussed from its initial
proof-of-concept to a formally structured software library. The development of
\textsc{magpie} across multiple programming languages is discussed alongside the
application of the open methodology.


\subsection{Chapter 4: Future}

Chapter~\ref{ch:future} looks at the paths for development for the projects in
Chapters~\ref{ch:interaction} and \ref{ch:analysis}. Developments are discussed
alongside the repercussions to each project's design principles and the wider
open methodology. Neither project is at an end point and there are a multitude
of developments for expansion, optimisation and refinement. A selection of
possible developments are discussed for the interface and \textsc{magpie} in
turn, outlining the technical challenges of development and the impact they
would have on the open methodology and the adherence to the FAIR principles.

Developments for the interface are split between the fabrication process in the
mechanical interface, in particular the jacks that are used and incorporation of
a sensor surface within them. This is followed by discussion in the
electronics system, the refinement of the sensor circuitry as well as the
expansion in its functionality and the consideration that would have on the
accessibility of the project. This concludes with a discussion that looks
towards the future for digital musical interfaces as a tool for research and musical expression,
and the possibilities of the interface that have not yet been explored.

The future of \textsc{magpie} is then discussed in terms of expanded
functionality and the changes that have already taken place at time of writing,
but have yet to be consolidated into a release format. Finally, the  development
of \textsc{magpie}'s graphical interfaces are outlined, with description of the
emerging technologies (in particular, web technologies) which would make the
software even more accessible.
