\subsection{Living Heritage, Dead Heritage}

Over recent decades, there has been a greater discourse around `living heritage' versus the static, preservable `dead' heritage \cite{poulios_moving_2010,smith_uses_2006}. 

Poulios critiques values-based conservation for treating the past as something `dead,' resulting in a discontinuity between past and present experiences \cite{poulios_moving_2010}. In contrast, the `living heritage' paradigm emphasises the ongoing, dynamic use and relevance of heritage, advocating for the preservation of not just physical artifacts but the practices, skills, and meanings that sustain them in contemporary life.

While Poulios' arguments are primarily directed at heritage sites and ceremonial objects, the underlying philosophy is applicable to the wider umbrella heritage objects, particularly musical instruments. There is false dichotomy that presents conservation as a choice between heritage as static and `dead' or `living.'\footnote{``a handful dismissed the idea of heritage as a negative idea, noting for instance that heritage was `keeping that which aught to be alive dead.' '' \cite{smith_uses_2006}}
The reality is more complex and to treat heritage as perpetually living without acknowledging its material finitude risks creating an `undead' heritage, neither truly alive nor preserved.

In addition to those above, I propose as a new category for musical instruments and potentially all heritage objects that embody craft and practice: `instructional value.' Instructional value refers to the capacity of a heritage object to convey knowledge of practice, technique, and skill intrinsic to its use and creation. A painting or sculpture may reveal insights into artistic methods through `witness marks,' the traces of the artist’s hand or materials. Similarly, a musical instrument can serve as a pedagogical tool for performers, luthiers, and scholars, instructing through its construction, wear patterns, and interaction how music was historically produced and experienced.

Instructional value may superficially appear to be a subset of educational or academic value. I argue that this conflation obscures a critical distinction between `descriptive knowledge' (knowing that) and `procedural knowledge' (knowing how). This division is essential to resolving tensions in musical instrument conservation, where preserving the physical object alone cannot fully capture its performative essence without maintaining or reviving the skills and practices it embodies.

The discourse around conservation frequently centres on the notion of `authenticity' \cite{pine_museums_2007, laurenson_authenticity_2006}, particularly regarding historically informed performance \cite{davies_authenticity_2001}. Setting aside the complexities and debates surrounding the meaning of `authenticity,' it is important to recognise that the audience's experience often dominates discussions of musical heritage. Yet, consider a scenario where a performer rehearses a piece 50 times before performing to an audience of 50 who hears it once. Which value was represented and by what magnitude in the course performance? I would argue the performer’s experience -- informed by the instrument’s unique qualities -- carries significant instructional value which is equal or greater in magnitude to those values relevant to audience. 

The performer, through hours of embodied interaction, acquires a richer, more nuanced understanding, gained through tactile feedback and `gestural repertoires' \cite[][]{levinson_music_1990} intrinsic to playing the instrument. The instrument shapes the performance as much as the performer shapes the music. This interplay is especially salient when comparing, for instance, a modern Steinway piano with a historical harpsichord, where differences in touch, sound production, and response create distinct musical experiences. Put simply, this yet another example of McLuhan’s maxim that ``the medium is the message'' \cite[][]{mcluhan_understanding_1964}.

Conventional museum practice rarely permits visitors to handle or play historical instruments. Though instruments may be in `playing condition' they are not typically playable by the average museum visitor. Instead, instruments cordoned off behind a -- real or imagined -- `red velvet cord,' \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014} displayed as objects to be seen but not touched. This approach robs visitors of the experience of vibro-tactile and kinaesthetic cues encoded into the interface of the instrument. The challenge, then, is how to reintroduce embodied interaction with historical instruments in ways that respect conservation imperatives while restoring some degree of their living function.

On the `priorities for future collecting' for the harpsichord collection at Musical Instrument Museums Edinburgh from \cite{uoe_collections_2020}

\begin{quotation}
    The collection is generally considered to have the widest scope of any in the world. Each item is important for reasons relevant to research and teaching, and in some cases performance potential. 
\end{quotation}

With performance potential only becoming less likely as time moves on.

I argue here that a holistic conservation framework for musical instruments must integrate both values-based and living heritage perspectives. Such a synthesis acknowledges the physical realities of ageing instruments while honouring their performative and instructional roles. This integration opens pathways to novel conservation strategies that extend beyond current paradigms.

\subsection{Harpsichords All The Way Down}

What will be presented is an approach to historical harpsichords conservation that is equal parts reconstruction, emulation and re-enactment of the original interface. By creating this new augmented interface there is still a question of how the new artefact is conserved as it ages. If the same process is applied where the copy is preserved and reconstructed, then it is `harpsichords all the way down.' Instead, this new interface should be considered finite and the focus of conservation should not be on the interface but rather the instructions for re-creating it.

For this reason the word `conservation' is used deliberately throughout this as opposed to `preservation' with respect to musical instrument heritage. Given the facts presented above, the working lifespan of an instrument -- where it can provide value instructional value -- is finite. To attempt to `preserve' a specific instrument with instructional value in mind is a futile endeavour, conserving, keeping as long as possible through careful treatment, is the best we for which can hope.

Defining some terms: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital preservation} is the process of digitising an artefact for the purpose of a permanent record.
    \item \textbf{Digital heritage} constitutes all ``digital materials of enduring value'' \cite{unesco_heritage_2003} including documents, datasets and software.
    \item \textbf{Digital cultural heritage} are heritage artefacts with have undergone the process of digitisation. This specifically references to the digital instance of heritage objects
\end{itemize}

We need to ask ourselves: have museums been transformed by digital technologies? Or have digital technologies simply been wielded with an analogue mentality?

Reproducibility checklist
Although it’s impossible to guarantee computational reproducibility over time, these strategies can maximize your chances.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Code} Workflows based on point-and-click interfaces, such as Excel, are not reproducible. Enshrine your computations and data manipulation in code.

\item \textbf{Automate} Use continuous-integration services (for example, Travis CI) to automatically test your code over time, and in various computational environments.
\item \textbf{Simplify} Avoid niche or hard-to-install third-party code libraries that can complicate reuse.
\item \textbf{Verify} Check your code’s portability by running it in a range of computing environments.

One example of Farina's software is Aurora, a free software suite for acoustical analysis. Auroa provides an apposite case for importance of software sustainability and preservation, not just as a software of enduring value but also in its own use in digital cultural heritage projects for decades \cite{fausti_acoustic_2000,frey_acoustical_2013,almagropastor_assessing_2021}.

Created in 1995 \cite{farina_auralization_1995} as an extension to the then Cool Edit software \cite{tronchin_acoustics_1997} -- which would eventually become Adobe Audition \cite{farina_listening_2004} -- Aurora was initially distributed alongside Farina's Ramsete software for 3D simulation of building acoustics \cite{farina_aurora_1995} from \url{http://aurora.ramsete.com/Aurora} and \url{http://aurora-plugins.com/} as shareware \cite{farina_registration_2007}. 

From 1995, Aurora was in active development over the next decade where it was used simplify the process of sine sweep room impulse response measurement \cite{farina_aurora_2000}. The source code for Aurora would later be re-written by Simone Campanini for the free open source audio editor Audacity \cite{farina_new_2009} marking the first time the suite could be run entirely for free and ended of the `Russian Roulette' approach to shareware that had been taken in the past \cite{farina_registration_2007}.

Aurora has been used widely in research papers since its release and has continued to be used even as recent as 2024 \cite{fontana_highly_2024,popp_speech_2024,denti_pan_2024}. Although Aurora is free software, its source code has not historically been open; however it was still in active development by Farina as late as 2023 \cite{bevilacqua_new_2023}.

Without a formal software citation system, Aurora's usage in historical research is difficult to track. Determining the versions of Aurora used can be inferred from the date of a paper but this neither ideal nor accurate. If we consider the development Aurora has undergone, it is difficult to determine what impact these changes might have had in the results of previous research. This was in part the motivation for undertaking a small software conservation project in collaboration with Campanini and Farina. The Audacity variant of Aurora's source code was uploaded to a public version control repository, under a GPLv3 licence and DOI created \cite{farina_aurora_2025} in line with the guidelines of Software Sustainability institute \cite{hong_how_2019}. 

Since Audacity is an open source project, additional software can be embedded directly into it utilising Audacity's experimental `module' system, a little-used and under-documented feature \footnote{\url{https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/modules.html}}. One requirement for modules are that they be co-compiled with the Audacity source code in order to function \footnote{``Modules should be built on the same date as Audacity, otherwise the version string check will prevent the module loading'' \url{https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/modules.html}}.

The module approach was the one taken for Aurora port to Audacity \cite{farina_new_2009}. A benefit is the ability to distribute a single working audio editor environment alongside the Aurora suite. A potential, though not inevitable, pitfall of this approach is that the module source code becomes entangled within that of Audacity's and sadly this was the case for Aurora.

Where this all has implications for software sustainability is when the host software, Audacity, undergoes major development which -- after its announced partnership in with Muse Group in 2021 \cite{prokoudine_ultimate_2021} -- is likely to take place. In this scenario breaking changes -- updates to the source code that cause other dependant code or software to fail -- are inevitable. This leaves Aurora in a state of precarity where not only might it not available for use, but also a clear reference of functionality will not be possible.

The repository for the Aurora binaries -- hosted from \url{https://www.angelofarina.it/Aurora/download} -- will inevitably become unusable\footnote{At time of writing Farina's University of Parma site, \url{http://pcfarina.eng.unipr.it}, is down though it has been mirrored at \url{https://www.angelofarina.it}}
. Source code therefore presents one of the few points of reference for functionality. Although the source code for Aurora's Audacity port is available, the original Adobe Audition version is still not public. The current repository \cite{farina_aurora_2025} represents only a snapshot of the source code's complete history.

Aurora is not alone in this predicament as the parallels between it and the YIN source code by Alain de Cheveigne \cite{cheveigne_yin_2002} are strikingly similar. Likely YIN is widely used and cited piece of software in academia. YIN's source code was originally available from a repository at IRCAM at \url{http://www.ircam.fr/pcm/cheveign/sw/yin.zip}. YIN has recently been cited as available from de Cheveigne's ``informal'' page at Laboratoire des Systèmes Perceptifs (LSR)  \url{http://audition.ens.fr/adc/sw/yin.zip} \cite{alain_cancellation_2023}\footnote{YIN as it is available from the the LSR page \textit{still} references the IRCAM url as the repository}.

Aurora and YIN represents only two examples to problem of sustainability and preservation of research software; however, they illustrate that software though prolific and free like Aurora or free and open source like YIN does not make it invulnerable to decay and the risk of being lost.
Institutes could potentially be more proactive in hosting repositories and providing DOIs of the software that is born from their research output.

Documentation is vital for understanding how the source code is structured and functions \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}. Something as simple as adding a software licence can improve the developer engagement with software \cite{colazo_licence_2009}

The work outlined in the is thesis build from the lessons learned from software like Aurora and YIN and have informed the methodology of project structure documentation for the digital output of the research. 

Chapter~\ref{chapter:magpie} discusses the steps taken for sustainability with a purely software project where a distinct functional software library and interface library were created for the MAGPIE software.  For electronic hardware, the problem of sustainability is compounded compounded with the addition of physical components, their schematics and assembly on top of the firmware and software required to make the hardware function. Process for addressing this is covered further in Chapter~\ref{chapter:interaction}.

    \item PCB: Printed circuit board. The circuit required for connecting electronics together. Printed on plate of silicon with a copper coating.
    \item FAIR: Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reuse of digital assets    
    \item DOI: Digital object Identifier
    \item Digital Asset: Any type of digital data, encapsulating source code, compiled binaries, CAD files, images. Anything created then stored digital and used in the course of research.
    \item FOSS: Free open source software where free encapsulates GNU Project Four Essential Freedoms \cite{gnu_philosophy_2025} the user's ability to run, study, distribute and modify without restriction and that the source code be publicly available.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Chapter 2: Interface}

This chapter covers the creation of a model harpsichord interface that was augmented with an optical electronic sensor system to allow for interaction with digital software instruments. Incorporates the design, prototyping and fabrication stages as well as the final deployment as part of an exhibition at Museo San Colombano, Bologna.

Technological advances have transformed how museums document, present and interpret their collections. Immersive experiences are realised through tools such as 3D printing and virtual reality \cite{allard_use-of-hand-held_2005,wachowiak_3d-scanning_2009,music_3d-printed_2024,kuzminsky_three-dimensional_2012,schaich_from_2007}.
These technologies form a kind of experiential authenticity, enabling encounters that evoke the past's sensory, emotional, and intellectual essence \cite{trant_when_1999}. 
Achieving authenticity requires museums to navigate the delicate balance between preservation and meaningful engagement \cite{pine_museums_2007}—a challenge that is particularly evident in the case of historical musical instrument collections \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}. 
Musical instruments present an interesting problem for cultural heritage as they are both an aesthetic physical object, but also a tool with an inherent tactile experience \cite{charalampos-saitis_musical_2018}.
However, musical instruments inevitably decay until they reach a point where they are too fragile for continued interaction \cite{paradiso_to-save_2019}.
Museums face a constant tension between accessibility and preservation, restricting how visitors can interact with collections \cite{templeton_museum_2018, mcalpine_sampling_2014}.
What can be done to preserve not just the object, but the tactile experiences of playing a musical instrument? 
The chapter discusses the motivations, design and creation of a replica harpsichord keyboard with a typical early Italian layout, electronically augmented for an interactive exhibition.
Thinking beyond the ``red velvet cord''\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014, masu_o-in-nime_2023}, the replica keyboard aims to provide an opportunity to enhance visitor interaction with cultural heritage while safeguarding delicate artefacts. 
Rather than prioritising technical novelty, the project demonstrates how digital interventions can support meaningful interaction with a shared musical heritage, ensuring its continued relevance in contemporary museum contexts.
An exhibition was held at Museo San Colombano, Bologna, which aimed to enhance the visitor experience by providing an interface to digitised versions of instruments within the collection by leveraging the propensity humans have to being influenced by visual components when making musical judgements \cite{fritz_player_2012, fritz_soloist_2014, fritz_listener_2017, tsay_sight_2013}.
The exhibition attempted to balance the competing demands of historical authenticity, public accessibility, and preservation while situating itself within broader discussions on the role of technology in museums. Discussed are the keyboard's design principles, technical implementation, and implications, emphasising its contribution to public engagement and the long-term preservation of musical heritage.
