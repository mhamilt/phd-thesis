\section{Musical Instrument Conservation}

Begin by looking at musical instrument conservation.  Musical instruments as functional objects present a challenge for cultural heritage preservation. Instruments are both an aesthetic physical object, but also a tool with an inherent tactile experience \cite{charalampos-saitis_musical_2018}. The discussion within the field musical instrument conservation has typically between split by what approach to take in order to retain both these qualities \cite{barclay_care_1997, barclay_preservation_2005}. 

The harpsichord -- in particular those 17th century instruments within the Tagliavini collection -- is central focus of this thesis. When discussing musical instrument conservation, it is the case of the harpsichord that is being considered. Some of the methodology can be translated to other instruments but much of what is discussed is specific to the harpsichord.

The harpsichord has found itself at the centre of the `playability' debate in more than one collection, of not the Benton Fletcher Collection in Fenton House, Raymond Russel Collection in St Cecilia's, and the Tagliavini Collection in San Colombano.

There is argument also toward aesthetics and quality of sound which has been a driving force for the renovation route. The accuracy with which instruments can be assessed varies across the spectrum of musical instruments. Considering, however, just harpsichord, it will be argued that the pursuing the sound of an original historical instrument is a dubious ground for motivation in restoring an instrument. In fact,l Barclay writes of restored instruments that museums should ``make players and audiences aware of the speculative nature of the instrument's ``original sound.'' ''

Benton Fletcher wrote that instruments in a museum should be in playing condition lest they become part of `a dead museum of glass cases' \cite[Benton Fletcher quoted in a letter][p. 154]{barclay_preservation_2005}.

\subsection{Living Heritage, Dead Heritage}

Over recent decades, there has been a greater discourse around `living heritage' versus the static, preservable `dead' heritage \cite{poulios_moving_2010,smith_uses_2006}. 

Poulios critiques values-based conservation for treating the past as something `dead,' resulting in a discontinuity between past and present experiences \cite{poulios_moving_2010}. In contrast, the `living heritage' paradigm emphasises the ongoing, dynamic use and relevance of heritage, advocating for the preservation of not just physical artifacts but the practices, skills, and meanings that sustain them in contemporary life.

While Poulios' arguments are primarily directed at heritage sites and ceremonial objects, the underlying philosophy is applicable to the wider umbrella heritage objects, particularly musical instruments. There is false dichotomy that presents conservation as a choice between heritage as static and `dead' or `living.'\footnote{``a handful dismissed the idea of heritage as a negative idea, noting for instance that heritage was `keeping that which aught to be alive dead.' '' \cite{smith_uses_2006}}
The reality is more complex and to treat heritage as perpetually living without acknowledging its material finitude risks creating an `undead' heritage, neither truly alive nor preserved.

In addition to those above, I propose as a new category for musical instruments and potentially all heritage objects that embody craft and practice: `instructional value.' Instructional value refers to the capacity of a heritage object to convey knowledge of practice, technique, and skill intrinsic to its use and creation. A painting or sculpture may reveal insights into artistic methods through `witness marks,' the traces of the artist’s hand or materials. Similarly, a musical instrument can serve as a pedagogical tool for performers, luthiers, and scholars, instructing through its construction, wear patterns, and interaction how music was historically produced and experienced.

Instructional value may superficially appear to be a subset of educational or academic value. I argue that this conflation obscures a critical distinction between `descriptive knowledge' (knowing that) and `procedural knowledge' (knowing how). This division is essential to resolving tensions in musical instrument conservation, where preserving the physical object alone cannot fully capture its performative essence without maintaining or reviving the skills and practices it embodies. The information derived from playing an instrument is difficult to convey to performer and who in turn find it difficult to express \cite{lamb_play_2007}.

The discourse around conservation frequently centres on the notion of `authenticity' \cite{pine_museums_2007, laurenson_authenticity_2006}, particularly regarding historically informed performance \cite{davies_authenticity_2001}. Setting aside the complexities and debates surrounding the meaning of `authenticity,' it is important to recognise that the audience's experience often dominates discussions of musical heritage. Yet, consider a scenario where a performer rehearses a piece 50 times before performing to an audience of 50 who hears it once. Which value was represented and by what magnitude in the course performance? I would argue the performer’s experience -- informed by the instrument’s unique qualities -- carries significant instructional value which is equal or greater in magnitude to those values relevant to audience. 

The performer, through hours of embodied interaction, acquires a richer, more nuanced understanding, gained through tactile feedback and `gestural repertoires' \cite[][]{levinson_music_1990} intrinsic to playing the instrument. The instrument shapes the performance as much as the performer shapes the music. This interplay is especially salient when comparing, for instance, a modern Steinway piano with a historical harpsichord, where differences in touch, sound production, and response create distinct musical experiences. Put simply, this yet another example of McLuhan’s maxim that ``the medium is the message'' \cite[][]{mcluhan_understanding_1964}.

Conventional museum practice rarely permits visitors to handle or play historical instruments. Though instruments may be in `playing condition' they are not typically playable by the average museum visitor. Instead, instruments cordoned off behind a -- real or imagined -- `red velvet cord,' \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014} displayed as objects to be seen but not touched. This approach robs visitors of the experience of vibro-tactile and kinaesthetic cues encoded into the interface of the instrument. The challenge, then, is how to reintroduce embodied interaction with historical instruments in ways that respect conservation imperatives while restoring some degree of their living function.

On the `priorities for future collecting' for the harpsichord collection at Musical Instrument Museums Edinburgh from \cite{uoe_collections_2020}

\begin{quotation}
    The collection is generally considered to have the widest scope of any in the world. Each item is important for reasons relevant to research and teaching, and in some cases performance potential. 
\end{quotation}

With performance potential only becoming less likely as time moves on.

I argue here that a holistic conservation framework for musical instruments must integrate both values-based and living heritage perspectives. Such a synthesis acknowledges the physical realities of ageing instruments while honouring their performative and instructional roles. This integration opens pathways to novel conservation strategies that extend beyond current paradigms.

\subsection{Replicate or Renovate}

The drawback to restoration is that the process can be irreversibly destructive to the longevity of the instrument \cite{barclay_care_1997} but also to evidence of original craftsmanship \cite{karp_restoration_1979}. 

If authenticity of performance requires an original instrument \cite{karp_musical_1985}, and if ``every restoration [...] wipes away evidence and makes the original [...] condition more remote'' (from \cite{waitzman_music_1988} quoted in \cite{barclay_critical_1999}) then there is a finite supply of music to be played. This is not a sustainable long term plan for enabling access to performing on historic instruments. 

Barclay has been critical of restoration \cite{barclay_care_1997} but is rightly cautious of the concept of an `authentic copy' \cite{barclay_preservation_2005}. For a replica to provide instructions value, Barclay perhaps makes the mistaken assumption that ``we still have to make this assemblage of materials into a working musical instrument, comfortable and useful to players of the 21st century'' \cite{barclay_preservation_2005}. This is a potential misstep, as it is from the ``gestural repertoires'' \cite{levinson_music_1990} of the original instrument that we can learn more about it.

Like \textcite{davies_authenticity_2001}, Barclay relates two cases of instruments being copy, a recorder and a natural trumpet, to apply to the grander idea of copying, in particular the fidelity to measurements. However, Barclay choses to cite \cite{koster_restoration_1996} who argues not for aesthetics but rather tonal quality.

``That's where the 'accurate copy' inevitably falls from grace. And this is, of course, grist for the mills of those who argue for restoring as opposed to copying.'' In light of recent scientific studies, it would appear that neither approach can be accurate. The limitations in copying is no longer `grist in the mills` for restorers as that approach cannot be deemed `authentic' for all instruments either, and is in fact detrimental to other heritage values an instrument can hold. An ``authetic ruin'' providing more valuable information than a ``falsely stored original'' \cite[Citing Martin Skowroneck][p. 78]{barclay_preservation_2005}. This was already apparent in 1985 when Karp wrote: ``it is becoming obvious that restored older instruments do not necessarily behave or sound as they did when they were new''  \cite{karp_musical_1985}.

\textcite{koster_restoration_1996} argues for the `futility of restoration', but also the act of copying an instrument \cite{koster_exact_1994}. Koster's reasons against copies outside of the museum context for their potential to decieve, ``copies falsely advertised as original instruments'' or that the maker copying an instrument will inevitably deviate from the original. Koster concludes about a 1785 Jacques Germain harpsichord copy ``Although a direct comparison of the Germain harpsichord and its copy shows that the tone of the copy does not quite match the surpassing elegance of the original'' \cite{koster_restoration_1996}. The conclusion arrived at by Koster of the older instrument sounding superior to its newer copy is echoed by his contemporaries \footnote{`` A ``Stradivari has the ability to deliver the whole range of tonal colors [...]'' [...] Simon Morris, the director of Beare’s... ``New violins don’t have the range of tonal color. the depth of personality isn’t there[...]'' '' \cite{mead_musical_2014}} and as such even a team of museum experts may think twice about creating a copy. Given the recent studies by \textcite{fritz_player_2012, fritz_soloist_2014, fritz_listener_2017} -- which illustrate the deterioration in acoustic quality of older violins, Guarneri and Stradivari included -- this conclusion by Koster may be unsound. In order to demonstrate a copy sounded inferior it would ideally need to undergo a double blind. Great care also needs to be taken in such studies so that it is clear what is being assessed.\footnote{``of  course, the  community  didn't  like  these  results. And  so,  the  study  was  heavily  criticised. We  basically  got  killed  on the  Internet,  on  phones, like  "what  have  you  done?", "How  can  you  disrespect  Stradivarius  so  much?" People  were really not  happy  and  then  they  found all  different  excuses  to  justify that  the  study  was necessarily  badly  conducted, starting  with  the  fact  that  we definitely  chose a bad Stradivarius, which  was  was  already  a  good  move because,  I  mean,  now  people were  happy  to  acknowledge that  there  could  be  a bad Stradivarius. That was  always  something  going  forward.'' \cite{fritz_musica_2017}}

There is also an implication in this rhetoric that the original maker of these historical instrument  could perfectly: ``a true acoustical copy'' \cite{koster_exact_1994}. In the work the \textcite{viala_simultaneous_2020}, material variability and the affects of geometry means that, if the goal is indeed and ``acoustical copy'' the construction will likely deviate from the original. The environmental impact of musical instrument fabrication should not be discounted \cite{allen_fatto_2012} and it is through this we can argue that to replicate or renovate an instrument is in fact a false dichotomy and that environmental concerns mean that neither is feasible at scale.

\subsection{Harpsichords All The Way Down}

What will be presented is an approach to historical harpsichords conservation that is equal parts reconstruction, emulation and re-enactment of the original interface. By creating this new augmented interface there is still a question of how the new artefact is conserved as it ages. If the same process is applied where the copy is preserved and reconstructed, then it is `harpsichords all the way down.' Instead, this new interface should be considered finite and the focus of conservation should not be on the interface but rather the instructions for re-creating it.

For this reason the word `conservation' is used deliberately throughout this as opposed to `preservation' with respect to musical instrument heritage. Given the facts presented above, the working lifespan of an instrument -- where it can provide value instructional value -- is finite. To attempt to `preserve' a specific instrument with instructional value in mind is a futile endeavour, conserving, keeping as long as possible through careful treatment, is the best we for which can hope.

Musical instrument conservation has to contend with a Ship of Theseus problem. How much can the original instrument be changed before it is considered the same instrument. One benefit of a conservation approach that does not prioritise the playing of instruments is that this problem does to typically need to be addressed.

\section{Open Methods for Digital Conservation}

So far the challenges inherent to musical instrument conservation have been discussed.

In this section we will look at the problem of Digital Conservation as it applies to musical instrument.

For this `digital conservation' is defined as conservation where digital tools play a vital role within the process.

`Digital conservation' has traditionally bee applied to nature conservation \cite{vanderwal_digital_2015}, but here it is used to encapsulate the concepts of ``digital heritage'' \cite{unesco_heritage_2003}, ``digital preservation'' \cite{garrett_preserving_1996}, and ``digital cultural heritage'' \cite{fresa_digital_2014}. 


Defining some terms: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital preservation} is the process of digitising an artefact for the purpose of a permanent record.
    \item \textbf{Digital heritage} constitutes all ``digital materials of enduring value'' \cite{unesco_heritage_2003} including documents, datasets and software.
    \item \textbf{Digital cultural heritage} are heritage artefacts with have undergone the process of digitisation. This specifically references to the digital instance of heritage objects
\end{itemize}

Digital preservation is the process of digitising an artefact for the purpose of a permanent record.

We have to tackle a compound problem of digital conservation as it relates to musical instruments but also broader digital conservation itself. There are the problems that arise ins a digital conservation process particular to musical instruments such as the retention of the tactile performer experience. As has has been discussed in the previous section, retaining performer experience has problem outside of the digital domain.

Digital preservation and the integration of digital technology within the heritage sector is also not a solved problem.
Digital technology and immersive experiences technology are frequently declared to be `transforming' the idea of the museum \cite{gedert_technology_2017, li_systematic_2024, dutot_changing_2021}.
We need to ask ourselves: have museums been transformed by digital technologies? Or have digital technologies simply been wielded with an analogue mentality?
The `digital' in a museum not should not be a substitute of the physical artefact, but rather an extension of its reach \cite{foster_defining_1998, parry_museums_2010}.
It is worth keeping in mind that `digitisation' of an object is also not a single act, but a perpetual process \cite{rubio_work_2020, kelley_digital_2018}.
Both digital and analogue objects rely on physical storage mediums \cite{rubio_work_2020}, but the financial and energy cost of preserving something digitally can often be orders of magnitude greater than an `analogue' equivalent \cite{cieply_afterlife_2007}.

One advantage of digital technologies is the ability to provide a central point of access to disparate and distributed collections, specifically the data of those collections.

The key to the usability \cite{dolan_mimo_2017} of these systems lies within their data, the collection and managing of which requires multidisciplinary skills.

With respect to the humanities, there is still a gap in  digital skills and best practices in data sustainability that needs to be closed \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}.

\subsection{Aurora}

During the course of this PhD was the passing of professor Angelo Farina who, as well being prolific academic within the field of acoustics, was also the author of a number of free software packages for acoustics analysis. One example of Farina's software is Aurora, a free software suite for acoustical analysis. Auroa provides an apposite case for the importance of software sustainability and preservation, not only for its enduring value but also because of its long-standing role in digital cultural heritage projects spanning decades \cite{fausti_acoustic_2000,frey_acoustical_2013,almagropastor_assessing_2021}.

Aurora was created in 1995 \cite{farina_auralization_1995} as an extension to the then Cool Edit software \cite{tronchin_acoustics_1997} -- which would eventually become Adobe Audition \cite{farina_listening_2004} -- and was initially distributed alongside Farina's Ramsete software for 3D simulation of building acoustics \cite{farina_aurora_1995} from \url{http://aurora.ramsete.com/Aurora} and \url{http://aurora-plugins.com/} as shareware \cite{farina_registration_2007}. 

Aurora became prominently used in research after functionality was added that simplified the process of sine sweep room impulse response measurement \cite{farina_aurora_2000}. In 2009 the source code for Aurora would later be ported by Simone Campanini to the free open source audio editor Audacity \cite{farina_new_2009}. This port marked the first time Aurora was fully free to use and ended the ``russian roulette'' approach to shareware that had been taken with the Adobe Audition version \cite{farina_registration_2007}.

Aurora has been widely cited in research papers since its remains a part of toolkit for the acoustics research community \cite{fontana_highly_2024,popp_speech_2024,denti_pan_2024}. Although Aurora is free software, its source code has not historically been open; however it was still in active development by Farina as late as 2023 \cite{bevilacqua_new_2023}. Tracking Aurora’s use in historical research is complicated by the lack of a software citation standard. Inferring the version of Aurora used from publication dates is imprecise, and given the significant development it has undergone, it is difficult to assess how changes may have influenced previous research results. These factors motivated a small software conservation project, undertaken in collaboration with Campanini and Farina, which involved uploading the Audacity variant of Aurora’s source code to a public version control repository, released under a GPLv3 license with an assigned DOI \cite{farina_aurora_2025} in accordance with current best practice guidlines \cite{stall_journal_2023, hong_how_2019}

Since Audacity is an open source project, additional software can be embedded directly into it utilising Audacity's experimental `module' system, a little-used and under-documented feature \footnote{\url{https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/modules.html}}. One requirement for modules are that they be co-compiled with the Audacity source code in order to function \footnote{``Modules should be built on the same date as Audacity, otherwise the version string check will prevent the module loading'' \url{https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/modules.html}}.

The module approach was the one taken for Aurora port to Audacity \cite{farina_new_2009}. A key advantage is the ability to distribute a single working audio editor package that intergrates the Aurora suite. However, this also risks the module’s source code becoming entangled with Audacity’s own codebase — a situation that unfortunately occurred with Aurora.

Where this all has implications for software sustainability is when the host software, Audacity, undergoes major development which -- after its announced partnership in with Muse Group in 2021 \cite{prokoudine_ultimate_2021} -- is likely to take place. In this scenario breaking changes -- updates to source code that cause other dependant code or software to fail -- are inevitable. Consequently, Aurora’s availability and functionality has cbecome precarious.

The repository for the Aurora binaries, hosted from \url{https://www.angelofarina.it/Aurora/download}, will inevitably become obsolete\footnote{At time of writing Farina's University of Parma site, \url{http://pcfarina.eng.unipr.it}, is down though it has been mirrored at \url{https://www.angelofarina.it}}. 
Thus, source code remains one of the few reliable records to Aurora’s functionality. Although the source code for Aurora's Audacity port is available, the original Adobe Audition version source is not public. The current repository represents only a snapshot of the source code's complete history.

Aurora is not unique in this predicament, the YIN source code by Alain de Cheveigne \cite{cheveigne_yin_2002} and it faces strikingly similar challenges to Aurora. YIN is also a widely used and cited software in acoustics. YIN's source code was originally available from a commonly cited link at IRCAM website \url{http://www.ircam.fr/pcm/cheveign/sw/yin.zip}, but this is no longer a valid. YIN has recently been cited as available from de Cheveigne's ``informal'' page at Laboratoire des Systèmes Perceptifs (LSR) \url{http://audition.ens.fr/adc/sw/yin.zip} \cite{alain_cancellation_2023}.\footnote{YIN as it is available from the the LSR page \textit{still} references the IRCAM url as the repository}. Though the original article describes the algorithm, citation to these zips are not uncommon in research \cite{banhalmi_fundamental_2005,staudacher_fast_2016} and when the software, not the algorithm in the paper, is a constituent part part of that research then it is important that we keep a record of it.

Aurora and YIN illustrate broader issues in the sustainability and preservation of research software. Regardless of being free, open source, or widely used, software remains vulnerable to decay. Research institutions could play a more proactive role by hosting repositories and assigning DOIs to software produced from their research outputs.

Documentation is vital for understanding source code structure and functionality \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}.  Even basic steps such as adding a software licence can improve the developer community engagement with software \cite{colazo_licence_2009}.

The work presented in this thesis builds on lessons learned from software like Aurora and YIN. These experiences have informed the methodology for project structure, documentation and the broader digital output of this research.

\subsection{Chapter 2: Interface}

This chapter covers the creation of a model harpsichord interface that was augmented with an optical electronic sensor system to allow for interaction with digital software instruments. Incorporates the design, prototyping and fabrication stages as well as the final deployment as part of an exhibition at Museo San Colombano, Bologna.

Technological advances have transformed how museums document, present and interpret their collections. Immersive experiences are realised through tools such as 3D printing and virtual reality \cite{allard_use-of-hand-held_2005,wachowiak_3d-scanning_2009,music_3d-printed_2024,kuzminsky_three-dimensional_2012,schaich_from_2007}.
These technologies form a kind of experiential authenticity, enabling encounters that evoke the past's sensory, emotional, and intellectual essence \cite{trant_when_1999}. 
Achieving authenticity requires museums to navigate the delicate balance between preservation and meaningful engagement \cite{pine_museums_2007}—a challenge that is particularly evident in the case of historical musical instrument collections \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}. 
Musical instruments present an interesting problem for cultural heritage as they are both an aesthetic physical object, but also a tool with an inherent tactile experience \cite{charalampos-saitis_musical_2018}.
However, musical instruments inevitably decay until they reach a point where they are too fragile for continued interaction \cite{paradiso_to-save_2019}.
Museums face a constant tension between accessibility and preservation, restricting how visitors can interact with collections \cite{templeton_museum_2018, mcalpine_sampling_2014}.
What can be done to preserve not just the object, but the tactile experiences of playing a musical instrument? 
The chapter discusses the motivations, design and creation of a replica harpsichord keyboard with a typical early Italian layout, electronically augmented for an interactive exhibition.
Thinking beyond the ``red velvet cord''\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014, masu_o-in-nime_2023}, the replica keyboard aims to provide an opportunity to enhance visitor interaction with cultural heritage while safeguarding delicate artefacts. 
Rather than prioritising technical novelty, the project demonstrates how digital interventions can support meaningful interaction with a shared musical heritage, ensuring its continued relevance in contemporary museum contexts.
An exhibition was held at Museo San Colombano, Bologna, which aimed to enhance the visitor experience by providing an interface to digitised versions of instruments within the collection by leveraging the propensity humans have to being influenced by visual components when making musical judgements \cite{fritz_player_2012, fritz_soloist_2014, fritz_listener_2017, tsay_sight_2013}.
The exhibition attempted to balance the competing demands of historical authenticity, public accessibility, and preservation while situating itself within broader discussions on the role of technology in museums. Discussed are the keyboard's design principles, technical implementation, and implications, emphasising its contribution to public engagement and the long-term preservation of musical heritage.

\section{Definitions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item CAD: Computer aided design,  Relating to CAD files used in the creation of PCBs or 3D prints
    \item PCB: Printed circuit board. The circuit required for connecting electronics together. Printed on plate of silicon with a copper coating.
    \item FAIR: Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reuse of digital assets    
    \item DOI: Digital object Identifier
    \item Digital Asset: Any type of digital data, encapsulating source code, compiled binaries, CAD files, images. Anything created then stored digital and used in the course of research.
    \item FOSS: Free open source software where free encapsulates GNU Project Four Essential Freedoms \cite{gnu_philosophy_2025} the user's ability to run, study, distribute and modify without restriction and that the source code be publicly available.
\end{itemize}
