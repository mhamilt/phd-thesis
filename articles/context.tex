The work presented in this thesis was carried out as part of the NEMUS project
\cite{nemus_description_2023}, which seeks to address the challenges of
historical musical instrument restoration through the application of digital
technologies. Two aspects of the conservation process are considered here. When
a historical musical instrument has fallen out of playing condition---and
restoring it would compromise its structural or historical integrity---can
interaction be reintroduced through digitisation? Digitisation may involve
sampling an instrument
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014,paradiso_to-save_2019,tasten_project_2018} or
reconstructing it through numerical simulation \cite{XXXX}. Despite the
emergence of these techniques, the question of how to provide meaningful access
to digitised instruments within museum contexts remains unresolved
\cite{woodmusick_effects_2015,holzer_imperfect_2025}.

These challenges are exemplified by the renowned Tagliavini Collection in
Bologna \cite{meer_collezione_2007}, housed at Museo San Colombano, comprised of
primarily early Italian plucked string keyboards. Fernando Tagliavini envisioned
that the instruments in his collection would be maintained in their original
playable condition. While the instruments are indeed playable, their use
necessitates strict controls and is permitted only under special supervision.
Moreover, certain instruments—such as the Museum’s 1547 Alessandro Trasuntino
harpsichord—where restoration to playable condition poses significant risks due
to their fragility.

This issue is not unique to the Tagliavini Collection but also pertains to other
notable historical instrument collections, such as those of Richard Burnett,
Benton Fletcher, and Bate \cite{barclay_preservation_2005}, which all house
playable harpsichords dating from the 17th and 18th centuries. The work
addresses two aspects of the conservation process relevant to these collections
termed `Analysis' and `Interaction'.

This thesis addresses these questions through two interconnected projects.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:keyboard} examines what occurs after digitisation has taken
place, asking: how can interaction with a digitised musical instrument be
meaningfully facilitated? Chapter~\ref{chapter:magpie} focuses on the
development of an open-source software tool for plate simulation, designed to be
used alongside measurements to support decision-making in conservation
strategies \cite{barclay_care_1997}. Linking these two case studies is a broader
investigation into the sustainability and reproducibility of digital
conservation and the tools that underpin it. Reproducibility poses a significant
challenge for digital research \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}. Accordingly, this
thesis outlines the steps required to ensure the longevity and accessibility of
digital research outputs
\cite{soito_citations_2016,smith_software_2016,katz_recognizing_2021,stall_journal_2023}
within the context of musical cultural heritage.

This chapter situates the work within its broader context and is divided into
two main areas. The first concerns the field of musical instrument conservation,
addressing the challenges that arise and the limitations that persist, whether
or not they are acknowledged by the wider community. The second examines the
term `digital conservation' as it is used throughout this thesis, focusing on
the challenges posed by digital technologies and their integration into cultural
heritage and conservation practice. In particular, this section discusses an
open methodology---in which digital research outputs are accessible, reusable,
and sustainable---as a framework for the practice of digital conservation.

\section{Musical Instrument Conservation}

Historical musical instruments present a unique challenge within the field of
cultural heritage. Instruments are both an aesthetic physical object, but also a
tool with an inherent tactile experience \cite{charalampos-saitis_musical_2018}.
Maintaining instruments in playable condition introduces significant risks and
costs \cite{lamb_play_2007}, prompting ongoing debate about how best to balance
preservation and access. The discussion within the field of musical instrument
conservation has typically been split between approaches to balance competing
values of aesthetics and function \cite{barclay_care_1997,
barclay_preservation_2005}. 

The harpsichord has found itself at the centre of the `playability' debate in
more than one collection. Of note are the Benton Fletcher Collection in Fenton
House \cite{barclay_preservation_2005,mcalpine_sampling_2014}, Musical
Instruments Museums Edinburgh \cite{}, and the Tagliavini Collection in San
Colombano \cite{wraight_report_2022}. Instruments from the Tagliavini collection
\cite{meer_collezione_2007} -- in particular 17th century instruments -- were a
motivating factor and test case for part of thesis. When discussing musical
instrument conservation, it is the case of the harpsichord that is being
considered. Though some of the methodology can be translated to other
instruments, much of what is discussed is specific to the harpsichord.


\subsection{Living Heritage, Dead Heritage}

\textcite[p. 19]{barclay_preservation_2005} defines a schema of three states
into which a historical instrument may fall: Currency, Conservation, and
Restoration. The state of Currency---where the instrument is in working
condition and actively maintained---is not relevant to the concerns of this
thesis. Arguments in favour of restoration often appeal to aesthetics and sound
quality, which have historically motivated renovation practices. However, the
degree of accuracy with which instruments can be assessed varies considerably
across the spectrum of musical instruments. Focusing specifically on the
harpsichord, it will be argued that pursuing the original sound of a historical
instrument is a questionable rationale for restoration. As Barclay suggests,
museums should inform both players and audiences of the uncertainties inherent
in such reconstructions, noting that ``the speculative nature of the
instrument’s original sound'' must be acknowledged \cite[p.
83]{barclay_care_1997}.

This perspective stands in contrast to earlier views, such as those of Benton
Fletcher, who insisted that instruments should remain playable in order to avoid
becoming relics in a ``dead museum of glass cases'' \cite[p. 154, quoted in a
letter]{barclay_preservation_2005}. Fletcher’s sentiments are echoed in a later
report on the restoration of instruments in the collection. As relayed by
\textcite[]{barclay_critical_1999}, the report warns against instruments being
relegated to ``a temple of silence where it may be conserved as a piece of
furniture, its musical function forgotten'' \cite{adlam_proposal_1972}. The
tension between Barclay’s three states reflects a wider discourse in the
heritage sector over the past decade concerning \emph{living heritage} versus
static, preservable \emph{dead heritage} \cite{poulios_moving_2010,
smith_uses_2006}.

\textcite{poulios_moving_2010} critiques values-based conservation for treating
the past as dead, which creates a discontinuity between past and present
experiences. In contrast, the living heritage paradigm emphasises the ongoing,
dynamic use and relevance of heritage, advocating for the preservation not only
of physical artefacts but also of the practices, skills, and meanings that
sustain them in contemporary life.

While Poulios’ arguments are primarily directed at heritage sites and ceremonial
objects, the underlying philosophy extends to the broader category of heritage
artefacts, including musical instruments. The framing of conservation as a
strict choice between static dead heritage and perpetually living heritage
constitutes a false dichotomy.\footnote{``a handful dismissed the idea of
heritage as a negative idea, noting for instance that heritage was keeping that
which ought to be alive dead. '' \cite{smith_uses_2006}} The reality is more
complex: to treat heritage as endlessly living without acknowledging its
material finitude risks producing an undead heritage, neither truly alive nor
genuinely preserved.

In addition to those above, this work proposes a new category for musical
instruments and potentially all heritage objects that embody craft and practice:
`instructional value.' Instructional value refers to the capacity of a heritage
object to convey knowledge of practice, technique, and skill intrinsic to its
use and creation. A painting or sculpture may reveal insights into artistic
methods through `witness marks,' the traces of the artist’s hand or materials.
Similarly, a musical instrument can serve as a pedagogical tool for performers,
luthiers, and scholars, instructing through its construction, wear patterns, and
interaction how music was historically produced and experienced.

Instructional value may superficially appear to be a subset of educational or
academic value. This conflation obscures a critical distinction between
`descriptive knowledge' (knowing that) and `procedural knowledge' (knowing how).
This division is essential to resolving tensions in musical instrument
conservation, where preserving the physical object alone cannot fully capture
its performative essence without maintaining or reviving the skills and
practices it embodies. The information derived from playing an instrument is
difficult to convey to performer and who in turn find it difficult to express
\cite{lamb_play_2007}.

The discourse around conservation frequently centres on the notion of
`authenticity' \cite{pine_museums_2007, laurenson_authenticity_2006},
particularly regarding historically informed performance
\cite{davies_authenticity_2001}. Setting aside the complexities and debates
surrounding the meaning of `authenticity,' it is important to recognise that the
audience's experience often dominates discussions of musical heritage. Yet,
consider a scenario where a performer rehearses a piece 50 times before
performing to an audience of 50 who hears it once. Which value was represented
and by what magnitude in the course performance? This thesis argue for the
performer’s experience -- informed by the instrument’s unique qualities --
carries significant instructional value which is equal or greater in magnitude
to those values relevant to audience. 

The performer, through hours of embodied interaction, acquires a richer, more
nuanced understanding, gained through tactile feedback and `gestural
repertoires' \cite[][]{levinson_music_1990} intrinsic to playing the instrument.
The instrument shapes the performance as much as the performer shapes the music.
This interplay is especially salient when comparing, for instance, a modern
Steinway piano with a historical harpsichord, where differences in touch, sound
production, and response create distinct musical experiences. Put simply, this
yet another example of McLuhan’s maxim that ``the medium is the message''
\cite[][]{mcluhan_understanding_1964}.

Conventional museum practice rarely permits visitors to handle or play
historical instruments. Though instruments may be in `playing condition' they
are not typically playable by the average museum visitor. Instead, instruments
cordoned off behind a -- real or imagined -- `red velvet cord,'
\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014} displayed as objects to be seen but not touched.
This approach robs visitors of the experience of vibro-tactile and kinaesthetic
cues encoded into the interface of the instrument. The challenge, then, is how
to reintroduce embodied interaction with historical instruments in ways that
respect conservation imperatives while restoring some degree of their living
function.

On the `priorities for future collecting' for the harpsichord collection at
Musical Instrument Museums Edinburgh from \cite{uoe_collections_2020}

\begin{quotation}
    The collection is generally considered to have the widest scope of any in
    the world. Each item is important for reasons relevant to research and
    teaching, and in some cases, performance potential. 
\end{quotation}

For instruments that originated from a fixed point in time (e.g. 17th-century
harpsichords), `performance potential' is becoming less likely. A holistic
conservation framework for musical instruments must integrate both values-based
and living heritage perspectives. Such a synthesis acknowledges the physical
realities of ageing instruments while honouring their performative and
instructional roles. This integration opens pathways to novel conservation
strategies that extend beyond current paradigms.

\subsection{Reproduction or Renovation}\label{sec:reproduction-or-renovation}

Musical instrument states of Currency and Restoration must contend with a Ship
of Theseus problem: how much alteration can the original instrument undergo
before it ceases to be considered the same object? The drawback of restoration
is twofold: the process can be irreversibly destructive both to the instrument’s
longevity \cite{barclay_care_1997} and to the surviving evidence of original
craftsmanship \cite{karp_restoration_1979}. If the authenticity of performance
requires the use of an original instrument \cite{karp_musical_1985}, and if
``every restoration [...] wipes away evidence and makes the original [...]
condition more remote'' (from \cite{waitzman_music_1988} quoted in
\cite{barclay_critical_1999}), then there exists only a finite reservoir of
authentic music to be played. This is not a sustainable long-term approach for
enabling performance on historical instruments.

Barclay has been critical of restoration \cite{barclay_care_1997}, while at the
same time expressing caution regarding the notion of an `authentic copy'
\cite{barclay_preservation_2005}. For a replica to be of instructional value,
Barclay appears to assume---perhaps mistakenly---that we still have to make this
assemblage of materials into a working musical instrument, comfortable and
useful to players of the 21st century \cite{barclay_preservation_2005}. This
assumption risks overlooking the possibility that it is from the ``gestural
repertoires'' \cite{levinson_music_1990} of the original instrument that the
most meaningful insights may be gained.

Approaches to historical instruments that prioritise either strict conservation
or reproduction preserve the informational integrity of the original and thus
avoid the Ship of Theseus problem altogether. Even so, reproduction is not
without contention. Barclay cites two cases of copying---a recorder and a
natural trumpet---to address the broader issue of fidelity to measurements, a
concern also raised by \textcite{koster_exact_1994}. In raising the ``thorny
issue'' \cite{barclay_preservation_2005} of reproduction, Barclay draws on
\textcite{koster_restoration_1996}, who argues not from aesthetics but from
tonal quality. In evaluating a 1785 Jacques Germain harpsichord copy, Koster
concludes: ``... a direct comparison of the Germain harpsichord and its copy
shows that the tone of the copy does not quite match the surpassing elegance of
the original'' \cite{koster_restoration_1996}. The conclusion that the older
instrument sounded superior to its copy is echoed by contemporaries,\footnote{A
Stradivari has the ability to deliver the whole range of tonal colors [...]''
[...] Simon Morris, the director of Beare’s... New violins don’t have the range
of tonal color. The depth of personality isn’t there [...]''
\cite{mead_musical_2014}} and even a team of museum experts may hesitate before
creating a copy.

However, given recent studies by \textcite{fritz_player_2012,
fritz_soloist_2014, fritz_listener_2017}---which demonstrate that the acoustic
quality of older violins, including Guarneri and Stradivari, deteriorates over
time---Koster’s conclusion may be unsound. His objections to copies also extend
beyond acoustics: they might deceive by being misrepresented as originals, or
deviate from the original simply through the act of replication. Yet an argument
against reproduction based on acoustics must be tested empirically. To
demonstrate that a copy sounds inferior, an ideal study would require a
double-blind design. Great care is also necessary to clarify exactly what is
being assessed.\footnote{``Of course, the community didn't like these results.
And so, the study was heavily criticised. We basically got killed on the
Internet, on phones, like What have you done?', How can you disrespect
Stradivarius so much?' People were really not happy, and then they found all
different excuses to justify that the study was necessarily badly conducted,
starting with the fact that we definitely chose a bad Stradivarius, which was
already a good move because, I mean, now people were happy to acknowledge that
there could be a bad Stradivarius. That was always something going forward.''
\cite{fritz_musica_2017}}

``That's where the 'accurate copy' inevitably falls from grace. And this is, of
course, grist for the mills of those who argue for restoring as opposed to
copying.'' In light of recent scientific studies, it would appear that neither
approach can be accurate. The limitations in copying is no longer `grist in the
mills` for restorers as that approach cannot be deemed `authentic' for all
instruments either, and is detrimental to other heritage values an instrument
can hold. An ``authentic ruin'' providing more valuable information than a
``falsely stored original'' \cite[Citing Martin Skowroneck][p
78]{barclay_preservation_2005}. This was already apparent in 1985 when Karp
wrote: ``it is becoming obvious that restored older instruments do not
necessarily behave or sound as they did when they were new''
\cite{karp_musical_1985}.

There is also an implication in this rhetoric that the original maker of these
historical instruments could perfectly: ``a true acoustical copy''
\cite{koster_exact_1994}. In the work the \textcite{viala_simultaneous_2020},
material variability and the affects of geometry means that, if the goal is
indeed and ``acoustical copy'' the construction will likely deviate from the
original. The environmental impact of musical instrument fabrication should not
be discounted \cite{allen_fatto_2012}, and this suggests that replicating or
renovating an instrument is a false dichotomy and that environmental concerns
mean neither is feasible at scale.

\subsection{Conservation and Sustainability}

Musical instrument restoration and reproduction do not exist in isolation.
Instruments require raw materials for their manufacture, and many of the
materials historically employed are no longer readily available, or may soon
become scarce. As part of the broader discussion on the preservation of musical
instrument heritage, consideration should also be given to environmental
concerns.

The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and
Flora (CITES) is a multilateral treaty established to protect animal and plant
species threatened by international trade. CITES maintains three Appendices,
which classify species according to the degree of threat they face, with
Appendix I representing the highest level of protection and Appendix III the
lowest. Among the species listed under these Appendices are several materials
traditionally used in the manufacture of musical instruments.

The use of these woods, and the implications of their inclusion in the CITES
Appendices for the musical instrument industry, are far from trivial. For
example, a large seizure of illegally harvested Pernambuco, used in the
manufacture of violin bows, was reported in Brazil \cite{toledo_operation_2022}.
Similarly, in 2008, the Gibson Guitar Company was implicated in the import of
illegally harvested wood from Madagascar \cite{justice_gibson_2012}. Such cases
highlight the environmental impact that the musical instrument industry can
exert on endangered ecosystems.

As more tree species become at risk and are brought under the protection of
CITES, many of the materials historically employed in instrument making are no
longer legally obtainable. Beyond legality, it is also financially and
ecologically unsustainable to maintain historical instruments in their original
form on a large scale. With the environmental consequences of musical instrument
conservation in view, the binary choice between replication and restoration
appears increasingly untenable. Neither option, pursued at scale, is financially
or ethically viable. Moreover, the number of historical instruments that could
realistically undergo such processes is limited, raising a further question: who
decides which instruments are deemed worthy of replication or restoration?

\subsection{A Third Path}

\textcite{desilvey_curated_2017} puts forward the question ``what could be
gained if we were to care for the past without pickling it[?]'' If heritage can
be in a state of living -- and a conservation approach is akin to `dead`
\cite{poulios_moving_2010} -- then there must also be a transition phase where
heritage dies and moves from one state to the other. Rather than resisting and
fearing this transition, \textcite{desilvey_curated_2017} suggests that we might
instead embrace this natural event. \textcite[p 75]{barclay_preservation_2005}
touches on this same idea as he discusses `benign neglect' where the
preservation of playing state-- or Currency-- is passive but the decision to do
so is very deliberate. We might then turn to other approaches to maintaining the
idea of an instrument. Rather than striving for the ``exact copy'' we can
instead consciously and deliberately seek an `imperfect' that elicits the
``exploring the social, political, historical, conceptual, contextual'' aspects
of musical instrument heritage \cite{holzer_imperfect_2025}.

What will be presented is an approach to historical harpsichords conservation
that is equal parts reconstruction, emulation and re-enactment of the original
interface. By creating this new augmented interface, there is still a question
of how the new artefact is conserved as it ages. If the same process is applied
where the copy is preserved and reconstructed, then it is `harpsichords all the
way down.' Instead, this new interface should be considered finite, and the
focus of conservation should not be on the interface but rather the instructions
for re-creating it.

For this reason, the term `conservation,' rather than `preservation', is used
deliberately throughout this thesis in relation to musical instrument heritage.
As the discussion above has shown, the working lifespan of an instrument---the
period during which it can provide instructional value---is finite. To attempt
to `preserve' a specific instrument with instructional value in mind is a futile
endeavour. What can be achieved, at best, is conservation: sustaining the
instrument for as long as possible through careful treatment.


\section{Digital Conservation}

Having discussed the broader challenges of musical instrument conservation, this
section turns to the role of digital technology within that context, framed here
under the term \emph{digital conservation}. Although the phrase \emph{digital
conservation} has more commonly been associated with biodiversity and
environmental preservation \cite{vanderwal_digital_2015}, in this thesis it is
redefined to draw on concepts from the fields of ``digital heritage''
\cite{unesco_heritage_2003}, ``digital preservation''
\cite{garrett_preserving_1996}, and ``digital cultural heritage''
\cite{fresa_digital_2014}.


To clarify the terminology used throughout the remainder of this thesis, the
following definitions are provided:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital preservation} is the process of digitising an artefact for the purpose of a permanent record.
    \item \textbf{Digital heritage} constitutes all ``digital materials of enduring value'' \cite{unesco_heritage_2003} including documents, datasets and software.
    \item \textbf{Digital cultural heritage} are heritage artefacts with have undergone the process of digitisation. This specifically references to the digital instance of heritage objects
\end{itemize}

Digital conservation is a compound problem, encompassing both the specific
challenges of digital conservation for musical instruments and the broader,
systemic issues facing digital conservation as a whole. One of the significant
challenges of digital musical instruments is preserving the tactile and embodied
experience of performance. As discussed in the previous section, this issue is
not exclusive to digital conservation; however, it becomes even more complex
when interaction is mediated through digital means.

Digital preservation and the integration of digital technology within the
heritage sector are also not solved problems. Digital technology and immersive
experiences technology are frequently declared to be `transforming' the idea of
the museum \cite{gedert_technology_2017, li_systematic_2024,
dutot_changing_2021}. We need to ask ourselves: have museums been transformed by
digital technologies? Or have digital technologies simply been wielded with an
analogue mentality? The `digital' in a museum should not be a substitute for the
physical artefact, but rather an extension of its reach
\cite{foster_defining_1998, parry_museums_2010}. It is worth keeping in mind
that `digitisation' of an object is also not a single act, but a perpetual
process \cite{rubio_work_2020, kelley_digital_2018}. Both digital and analogue
objects rely on physical storage mediums \cite{rubio_work_2020}, but the
financial and energy cost of preserving something digitally can often be orders
of magnitude greater than an `analogue' equivalent \cite{cieply_afterlife_2007}.

One advantage of digital technologies is the ability to provide a central point
of access to disparate and distributed collections, specifically the data of
those collections. The Musical Instrument Museums Online (MIMO) project, an
online catalogue that aggregates the data from collections of participating
musical instrument museums around the globe, is a perfect example of this
advantage. The key to the usability \cite{dolan_mimo_2017} of these systems lies
within their data, the collection and management of which requires
multidisciplinary skills. Museum professionals need adequate training to have an
understanding of digital data and its inherent problems of obsolescence and
silent corruption \cite{rubio_work_2020,schwarz_disk_2006,parry_museums_2010}.
Concerning the humanities, there is still a gap in digital skills and best
practices in data sustainability that needs to be closed
\cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. 

There is also the question of the digital heritage, such as software or data
sets, that are either utilised or created as part of the conservation process.
If musical instruments are to be preserved digitally, we need to be aware of how
the tools that are created for the process of conservation are themselves
conserved. Software has a reproducibility problem \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}
where it can be difficult to recreate the process or results after only
historically relatively short periods \cite{robert_reproducibility_2020}.

The digital components of this thesis have strived to follow the current best
practice guidelines. This section discusses the organisation and structure of
all digital components and the motivations behind this methodology.

\subsection{Open Methods}

To effectively tackle the problem of digital musical instrument conservation, we
require frameworks that address each of the components discussed above. 

Connected with the concept digital preservation is the movement of towards Open
Science \cite{unesco_open_2021}, a philosophy of academic research that
advocates for openly sharing not only the the results of research\footnote{
``e.g. original   scientific   research   results,   research  data,  software,
source  code,  source  materials,  workflows  and  protocols,  digital
representations  of  pictorial  and  graphical  materials   and   scholarly
multimedia   material'' \cite{unesco_open_2021}} but also the processes and
methodologies necessary to achieve those results. Digital technologies make a
concepts like Open Science feasible, but when applied to the digital assets, a
robust framework is required to make Open Science achievable. The FAIR
principles provide such framework, focussing on the ``Findability,
Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reuse [FAIR] of digital assets''
\cite{wilkinson_fair_2016}.

\begin{itemize}
\item Findability: Digital assets are described with rich metadata with a
persistent location
\item Accessibility: protocol is open, free, and universally implementable
\item Interoperability: the ability of data or tools from non-cooperating
resources to integrate or work together with minimal effort.
\item Reuse: digital assets are released with a clear and accessible data usage
license
\end{itemize}

For software to be FAIR, it must also be citable \cite{soito_citations_2016}, a
process which has historically lacked standardisation. Making software citable
involves compliance to guidelines not only by research software authors
\cite{smith_software_2016} but also by those citing that software
\cite{katz_recognizing_2021} and the journals cross-referencing those citations
\cite{stall_journal_2023}.

Open software should ideally be `living' with the potential for constant change,
but when used in research, this quality can be a problem for cite-ability and
reusability. Version control systems such as git provide a straightforward way
to track and tag versions of software. public, online, version-controlled
repositories like GitHub help software comply with FAIR principles, but there is
still a question of longevity \cite{smith_software_2016}. In addition to version
control platforms source should be uploaded to archive repositories, like the
Zenodo platform, which enable persistent referencing through a digital object
identifier (DOI) \cite{smith_software_2016,stall_journal_2023}.

Persistent referencing addresses on aspect longevity but another critical aspect
is the reproducibility of software \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}. Software is
should be executable no and in the future, a process which is hindered by
constantly developing programming languages in addition to all manner of
unforeseen obstacles, from hardware obsolescence to missing documentation
\cite{robert_reproducibility_2020}.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

While this discussion has focused on digital software, digitally designed
physical hardware presents additional challenges. Within the field of digital
musical instrument (DMI) design, long-term reusability remains an on-going
challenge \cite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}. Designing and fabricating hardware
requires more than just source code. Design of electronics hardware typically
incorporates Computer Aided Design (CAD) and Computer Aided Manufacture (CAM)
steps, necessitating the inclusion of not just schematics but also the bill of
materials, and documentation on assembly and operation all within a  well
structure archived repository \cite{calegario_documentation_2021}. CAD files and
surrounding support files also provide another point of failure with respect to
preservation. For example, in the course of this thesis the popular Electronic
Design Automation (EDA) software tool EAGLE was announced to be discontinued
\cite{autodesk_eagle_2024} for the July 2026. A conscious decision was made to
continue using the EAGLE format for the PCB design. Much in the same way as the
discontinuation of Python 2 may be beneficial for archival purposes as
``advanced programming language that is guaranteed not to evolve anymore''
\cite{rougier_loupe_2020}, so to does the discontinuation of the EAGLE means
that its XML file standard is now calcified and is unable changes. Instead, the
EAGLE files can be used in conjunction with other EDA software such as the open
source KiCAD \cite{kicad_eagle_2024}.

\textcite{perkel_challenge_2020} provides a checklist aggregated from the
ReScience C jounral's ten-year challenge initiative which challenges researchers
to replicate results from their software that is older than ten years. Key
recommendations in the list include version control and archiving which is in
keeping with this guidance for best practice \cite{smith_software_2016,
stall_journal_2023}.

Finally, all digital assets are available under the GLPv3 licence under
recommendation to provide as open permission as possible
\cite{stall_journal_2023}. The GPLv3 licence allows for the copy, distribution
and modify the source files and that any modifications to that digital source
must also be made available under the same licence \cite{gplv3}. Source code
repositories for this thesis were designed to conform to the current software
sustainability institute guidelines and underwent assessment from the Software
Sustainability Institute at the University of Edinburgh \cite{ssi_online_2025}.


\subsection{Case Study: Aurora}

During the course of this PhD was the passing of professor Angelo Farina who, as
well being prolific academic within the field of acoustics, was also the author
of a number of free software packages for acoustics analysis. One example of
Farina's software is Aurora, a free software suite for acoustical analysis.
Auroa provides an apposite case for the importance of software sustainability
and preservation, not only for its enduring value but also because of its
long-standing role in digital cultural heritage projects spanning decades
\cite{fausti_acoustic_2000,frey_acoustical_2013,almagropastor_assessing_2021}.

Aurora was created in 1995 \cite{farina_auralization_1995} as an extension to
the then Cool Edit software \cite{tronchin_acoustics_1997} -- which would
eventually become Adobe Audition \cite{farina_listening_2004} -- and was
initially distributed alongside Farina's Ramsete software for 3D simulation of
building acoustics \cite{farina_aurora_1995} from
\url{http://aurora.ramsete.com/Aurora} and \url{http://aurora-plugins.com/} as
shareware \cite{farina_registration_2007}. 

Aurora became prominently used in research after functionality was added that
simplified the process of sine sweep room impulse response measurement
\cite{farina_aurora_2000}. In 2009 the source code for Aurora would later be
ported by Simone Campanini to the free open source audio editor Audacity
\cite{farina_new_2009}. This port marked the first time Aurora was fully free to
use and ended the ``russian roulette'' approach to shareware that had been taken
with the Adobe Audition version \cite{farina_registration_2007}.

Aurora has been widely cited in research papers since its remains a part of
toolkit for the acoustics research community
\cite{fontana_highly_2024,popp_speech_2024,denti_pan_2024}. Although Aurora is
free software, its source code has not historically been open; however it was
still in active development by Farina as late as 2023
\cite{bevilacqua_new_2023}. Tracking Aurora’s use in historical research is
complicated by the lack of a software citation standard. Inferring the version
of Aurora used from publication dates is imprecise, and given the significant
development it has undergone, it is difficult to assess how changes may have
influenced previous research results. These factors motivated a small software
conservation project, undertaken in collaboration with Campanini and Farina,
which involved uploading the Audacity variant of Aurora’s source code to a
public version control repository, released under a GPLv3 license with an
assigned DOI \cite{farina_aurora_2025} in accordance with current best practice
guidlines \cite{stall_journal_2023, hong_how_2019}

Since Audacity is an open source project, additional software can be embedded
directly into it utilising Audacity's experimental `module' system, a
little-used and under-documented feature.
\footnote{\url{https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/modules.html}} One
requirement for modules are that they be co-compiled with the Audacity source
code in order to function.\footnote{``Modules should be built on the same date
as Audacity, otherwise the version string check will prevent the module
loading'' \url{https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/modules.html}}

The module approach was the one taken for Aurora port to Audacity
\cite{farina_new_2009}. A key advantage is the ability to distribute a single
working audio editor package that intergrates the Aurora suite. However, this
also risks the module’s source code becoming entangled with Audacity’s own
codebase — a situation that unfortunately occurred with Aurora.

Where this all has implications for software sustainability is when the host
software, Audacity, undergoes major development which -- after its announced
partnership in with Muse Group in 2021 \cite{prokoudine_ultimate_2021} -- is
likely to take place. In this scenario breaking changes -- updates to source
code that cause other dependant code or software to fail -- are inevitable.
Consequently, Aurora’s availability and functionality has cbecome precarious.

The repository for the Aurora binaries, hosted from
\url{https://www.angelofarina.it/Aurora/download}, will inevitably become
obsolete\footnote{At time of writing Farina's University of Parma site,
\url{http://pcfarina.eng.unipr.it}, is down though it has been mirrored at
\url{https://www.angelofarina.it}}. Thus, source code remains one of the few
reliable records to Aurora’s functionality. Although the source code for
Aurora's Audacity port is available, the original Adobe Audition version source
is not public. The current repository represents only a snapshot of the source
code's complete history.

Aurora is not unique in this predicament, the YIN source code by Alain de
Cheveigne \cite{cheveigne_yin_2002} and it faces strikingly similar challenges
to Aurora. YIN is also a widely used and cited software in acoustics. YIN's
source code was originally available from a commonly cited link at IRCAM website
\url{http://www.ircam.fr/pcm/cheveign/sw/yin.zip}, but this is no longer a
valid. YIN has recently been cited as available from de Cheveigne's ``informal''
page at Laboratoire des Systèmes Perceptifs (LSR)
\url{http://audition.ens.fr/adc/sw/yin.zip}
\cite{alain_cancellation_2023}.\footnote{YIN as it is available from the the LSR
page \textit{still} references the IRCAM url as the repository} Though the
original article describes the algorithm, citation to these zip archives are not
uncommon in research \cite{banhalmi_fundamental_2005,staudacher_fast_2016} and
when the software, not the algorithm in the paper, is a constituent part of that
research then it is important that a record of it is kept.

Aurora and YIN illustrate broader issues in the sustainability and preservation
of research software. Regardless of being free, open source, or widely used,
software remains vulnerable to decay. Research institutions could play a more
proactive role by hosting repositories and assigning DOIs to software produced
from their research outputs.

Documentation is vital for understanding source code structure and functionality
\cite{perkel_challenge_2020}.  Even basic steps such as adding a software
licence can improve the developer community engagement with software
\cite{colazo_licence_2009}.

The work presented in this thesis builds on lessons learned from software like
Aurora and YIN. These experiences have informed the methodology for project
structure, documentation and the broader digital output of this research.

\subsection{Chapter 2: Interface}

This chapter covers the creation of a model harpsichord interface that was
augmented with an optical electronic sensor system to allow for interaction with
digital software instruments. Incorporates the design, prototyping and
fabrication stages as well as the final deployment as part of an exhibition at
Museo San Colombano, Bologna.

Technological advances have transformed how museums document, present and
interpret their collections. Immersive experiences are realised through tools
such as 3D printing and virtual reality
\cite{allard_use-of-hand-held_2005,wachowiak_3d-scanning_2009,music_3d-printed_2024,kuzminsky_three-dimensional_2012,schaich_from_2007}.
These technologies form a kind of experiential authenticity, enabling encounters
that evoke the past's sensory, emotional, and intellectual essence
\cite{trant_when_1999}. Achieving authenticity requires museums to navigate the
delicate balance between preservation and meaningful engagement
\cite{pine_museums_2007}—a challenge that is particularly evident in the case of
historical musical instrument collections \cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}. Musical
instruments present an interesting problem for cultural heritage as they are
both an aesthetic physical object, but also a tool with an inherent tactile
experience \cite{charalampos-saitis_musical_2018}. However, musical instruments
inevitably decay until they reach a point where they are too fragile for
continued interaction \cite{paradiso_to-save_2019}. Museums face a constant
tension between accessibility and preservation, restricting how visitors can
interact with collections \cite{templeton_museum_2018, mcalpine_sampling_2014}.
What can be done to preserve not just the object, but the tactile experiences of
playing a musical instrument? The chapter discusses the motivations, design and
creation of a replica harpsichord keyboard with a typical early Italian layout,
electronically augmented for an interactive exhibition. Thinking beyond the
``red velvet cord''\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014, masu_o-in-nime_2023}, the
replica keyboard aims to provide an opportunity to enhance visitor interaction
with cultural heritage while safeguarding delicate artefacts. Rather than
prioritising technical novelty, the project demonstrates how digital
interventions can support meaningful interaction with a shared musical heritage,
ensuring its continued relevance in contemporary museum contexts. An exhibition
was held at Museo San Colombano, Bologna, which aimed to enhance the visitor
experience by providing an interface to digitised versions of instruments within
the collection by leveraging the propensity humans have to being influenced by
visual components when making musical judgements \cite{fritz_player_2012,
fritz_soloist_2014, fritz_listener_2017, tsay_sight_2013}. The exhibition
attempted to balance the competing demands of historical authenticity, public
accessibility, and preservation while situating itself within broader
discussions on the role of technology in museums. Discussed are the keyboard's
design principles, technical implementation, and implications, emphasising its
contribution to public engagement and the long-term preservation of musical
heritage.
