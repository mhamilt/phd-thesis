\chapter{Future Directions}\label{ch:future}

The two systems presented in the preceding chapters already function as viable tools in their current forms, yet neither represents an endpoint. The thesis as a whole should be read less as a finished artefact and more as a platform from which further iterations can evolve. Each prototype—whether the haptic keyboard interface or the \textsc{magpie} software—reveals new questions that extend beyond the practical scope of this work but remain central to its aims.

Some of these developments are already underway or have been tested in parallel with writing. In contrast, others remain prospective because of constraints in time, resources, or the need to preserve core methodological principles such as openness and reproducibility. This chapter, therefore, outlines a forward trajectory rather than a wish list: identifying where refinement is technically feasible, where further exploration is warranted, and where expansion may introduce trade-offs that require careful ethical or methodological justification.

The discussion is organised by project. Section~\ref{sec:future-interaction} focuses on the keyboard interface, detailing improvements to fabrication, sensing, and visitor interaction while maintaining its role within a conservation framework. Section~\ref{sec:future-magpie} then turns to \textsc{magpie}, considering its continuing development as an open-source research environment and the broader community practices that will shape its evolution. Together, these sections frame Chapter~\ref{ch:future} as a roadmap for sustainable growth—technical, conceptual, and collaborative—beyond the immediate boundaries of this thesis.

\subsection{Integrated Jack Fabrication}\label{sec:cad-cam-jacks}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{img/laser-gradients-1-row.jpg}
    \caption[Test gradients using a laser cutter on MDF]{Test gradients of various heights using a laser cutter on MDF with decreasing laser power from left to right. The range of the gradient was too compressed to be differentiated by the optical sensor. On high power settings, scorching tends to bleed around the raster, thus affecting the surrounding material and area of the gradient.}
    \label{fig:laser-speckle}
\end{figure}

Reflective gradient stickers were originally adopted as a fast, inexpensive, and reproducible way to enable optical sensing across multiple jacks. That choice made prototyping feasible, but it also introduced a maintenance loop that has grown rather than diminished with refinement. Dimensional variability in the jack bodies (typically 4.35\,mm across the short edge), humidity-driven expansion and contraction, and the finite lifespan of adhesive layers~\cite{antalis_coala_2025} all contribute to drift and repeated recalibration. What initially functioned as a practical shortcut has therefore become a persistent source of labour. The question is no longer how to improve the sticker, but how to remove it from the sensing chain altogether.

A more sustainable approach treats the reflective surface not as an applied layer but as a property of the jack itself. This shifts attention from the process of correction to the process of fabrication: if the relevant geometry and reflectance are built into the component, there is less to realign, replace, or reattach. At the same time, any change in fabrication method must remain compatible with the open, small-scale production ethos that underpins the interface as a conservation tool. Techniques must therefore rely on accessible materials, common CAM workflows, and file formats that can circulate outside specialist workshops.

One early path was to adapt the existing wooden jacks through laser etching. This had the appeal of retaining the original material while introducing a textured surface that could modulate reflected light. However, tests such as those shown in Figure~\ref{fig:laser-speckle} exposed the limitations of that approach. Low-power passes produce insufficient contrast for the optical sensor, while higher-power settings burn and bleed into the surrounding area, flattening any gradient. Although the method is not ruled out entirely—improvements in sensor-side sensitivity (see Section~\ref{sec:tia}) could make it viable—the reliability and repeatability required at scale cannot be guaranteed with current hardware.

A more decisive shift came from reframing the problem as one of fabrication rather than surface treatment. Instead of modifying a finished jack to make it senseable, the geometry of sensing can be designed into the part from the outset. Figures~\ref{fig:jack-model}--\ref{fig:jack-cut-out-3d-print} show the progression of experiments that take this approach. In each case, the jack body incorporates a sloped or recessed ``shutter'' feature that passes through the sensor’s field of view. Reflectance then becomes a function of motion through space rather than the condition of an applied layer.

Two fabrication routes were tested in parallel: CNC cutting in hardwood and additive manufacturing in PLA. CNC work promises material familiarity and structural rigidity, but introduces its own friction points. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cnc-jacks}, tear-out occurs along the narrow slots needed for tongues and quills, and post-planing is required to achieve consistent thickness across a set. More significantly, the equipment and skill requirements place it at odds with the accessibility and reproducibility that underpin the interface as a conservation-led system.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{img/cnc-jacks.jpeg}
    \caption[CNC Harpsichord Jacks]{Jacks created plywood and a CNC router. Though the jacks could be milled to a precise thickness, the cutting process was prone to tear outs in the material. As such, the CNC approach was subsequently abandoned in favour of experiments in laser cutting.}
    \label{fig:cnc-jacks}
\end{figure}

PLA-based printing, by contrast, tolerates complex geometry, supports colour and dimensional iteration, and scales quickly. It does not remove labour entirely: tongues and quills still require fitting, and small dimensional variances shift attention from sticker placement to jack-height alignment. Yet this is labour that serves a single fabrication stage rather than a recurring cycle of reapplication and recalibration. The material choice also reflects the function of these jacks within the broader conservation framework. They are not introduced into historic instruments, nor intended to replicate their material authenticity. Their purpose is to externalise and preserve action behaviour in a form that can be installed, tested, and iterated without risk to the original mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:jack-cut-out-3d-print} shows one such prototype (see also Section~\ref{sec:prototype}). Constraints around quilling remain—whether in PLA, Delrin, or other substitutes~\cite{bcn3d_pla_2019,dupont_delrin_2017,barclay_preservation_2005}—but these affect assembly technique rather than the sensing strategy itself. As the electronics evolve (Section~\ref{sec:tia}) and a stable workflow for jack fabrication emerges, the need for stickers should recede further, with the printed shutter serving as the primary surface for modulation.


\subsection{Modular Sensor Architecture}\label{sec:future-pcb-layout}

As the sensing system matured, the physical arrangement of the electronics emerged as a constraint in its own right. The original vertical sensor boards were designed to minimise footprint and sit close to the jack body, but their orientation made them mechanically fragile and difficult to install in the confined geometries of harpsichord actions. Narrow clearances between registers leave little room for board-edge components, and misalignment at installation has repeatedly resulted in damage or the need for ad hoc spacing interventions. These issues do not reflect a flaw in the sensing principle, but in how the circuitry is embedded within the mechanism.

A more robust approach separates the assembly into two distinct elements: a \emph{sensor module} that maintains fixed pitch and optical alignment, and a \emph{signal board} that handles routing, power conditioning, and analogue front ends. This modular structure localises mechanical risk to the sensor element while allowing the rest of the electronics to be placed where space and airflow are more permissive. Figure~\ref{fig:schmidt-sensor-board} illustrates one such module from \textcite{schmidt_sparksichord_2025}, where the sensing surface and connector remain compact and serviceable.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{img/schmidt_sparksichord_2025fig-13.png}
    \caption[Example sensor module]{Example sensor module, Figure 1.3 of
        \textcite{schmidt_sparksichord_2025}.}
    \label{fig:schmidt-sensor-board}
\end{figure}

Spatial constraints become especially acute in multi-register instruments. Figure~\ref{fig:3-registers} shows how stacked or closely spaced registers leave insufficient clearance for a long vertical board, regardless of pitch. In these cases, smaller per-key mini-modules provide an alternative that respects the geometry of the mechanism while retaining the same sensing principle. Where even that proves impractical, a hybrid strategy can be applied: two outer sensor rows capture motion directly, and values for the inner registers are interpolated during calibration. This trades installation complexity for computational load, but in a way that isolates mechanical risks rather than multiplying them.

Because LEDs mounted in this position are less visible during calibration, a small opaque diffuser can be added to preserve visual feedback without affecting exhibition conditions (see Figure~\ref{fig:led-diffuser}).

\subsection{Latency and Jitter}\label{sec:latency}

Responsiveness in the keyboard interface is already adequate for both performance and exhibition use, yet further refinement depends less on absolute speed than on timing consistency. In this context, \emph{latency} denotes the delay from pluck to MIDI transmission, while \emph{jitter} refers to its variability. Measurements of around 2\,ms latency and 1\,ms jitter (Section~\ref{sec:latency}) demonstrate that the system performs well in average conditions, but musical perception and haptic coupling benefit most from predictable timing behaviour rather than marginal speed improvements. Two factors dominate this predictability: the speed of the analogue front end and the determinism of the firmware scheduler. The development pathway therefore proceeds in three stages: first, improve the precision of sensor signal measurement (Section~\ref{sec:tia}); then constrain the timing sources and interrupt routines in firmware; finally, evaluate microcontroller platforms that can support these constraints while remaining open and reproducible.

\subsubsection{Sensor Signal Measurement}\label{sec:tia}

The current voltage-divider circuit (Figure~\ref{fig:volt-div}) is intentionally simple, inexpensive, and resilient to component changes, aligning with the thesis' open-methodology principle. However, the phototransistor’s parasitic capacitance imposes a low-pass effect~\cite{falco_understanding_2023}, slightly smoothing rapid transitions and introducing small, variable delays. When precision of onset is crucial—such as when synchronising gesture with sound playback—this behaviour becomes a measurable limit rather than a theoretical one.

     \caption[Demonstration of light diffusion applied to the RGB LEDs]{Demonstration of light diffusion applied to the RGB LEDs. Right: example of a bare LED and an LED with a diffuser attached. Left: CAD model of the light diffuser.}
    \label{fig:led-diffuser}
\end{figure}

A transimpedance amplifier (TIA) addresses this limitation by converting sensor current directly into voltage, bypassing the slow pull-up resistor and improving bandwidth (Figure~\ref{fig:tia-basic}). Stability is maintained through a feedback capacitor (Figure~\ref{fig:tia-cf})~\cite[p.~233]{horowitz_art_2015}, while biasing the collector--emitter junction further increases sensitivity and speed~\cite{falco_understanding_2023,pardue_near_2013}. To prevent the output from approaching supply rails, a differential configuration biases the inverting input (Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff}), following \textcite{mcpherson_portable_2013}, with resistor ratios tuned for optimal common-mode rejection~\cite[p.~227]{horowitz_art_2015}. The resulting circuit family, summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-evolution}, trades a small increase in complexity for a substantial gain in temporal precision.

Implementing the TIA stage nudges the design toward surface-mount components and denser layouts. This transition reduces prototyping convenience but remains compatible with low-cost PCB services. To maintain accessibility, TIAs should reside on the shared controller or signal board—close to power rails—rather than on each key module. Variations in resistor tolerance and sensor response may be managed through best-average networks~\cite{mcpherson_portable_2013} or limited trimming. Where speed is less critical, the simpler voltage-divider circuit remains a valid and maintainable fallback.

The QRD1114, compared with the QRE1113, offers higher on-state current and faster rise times (Table~\ref{tab:qre-vs-qrd}), although it introduces greater asymmetry and cross-talk. When paired with a tuned TIA (Figure~\ref{fig:general-tia}), these parameters yield more consistent transitions and may restore the viability of laser-etched patterns discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cad-cam-jacks}. As always, any such substitution must balance enhanced speed with reproducibility and sourcing longevity.

\subsubsection{Microcontroller}

With the analogue stage stabilised, attention shifts to the temporal precision of firmware. The current implementation~\cite{hamilton_firmware_2025} prioritises portability across Arduino-supported boards, ensuring long-term reproducibility but limiting access to high-resolution hardware timers. Deterministic scheduling can be introduced by employing a dedicated timer on the Nano~33~BLE (nRF52), using either \texttt{NRF\_TIMERx}~\cite{nordic_timer_2023,nordic_timer_2025} or \texttt{mbed::Ticker} for periodic sensing and MIDI dispatch, provided interrupt routines remain minimal~\cite{klingensmith_using_2019}. This approach may increase baseline latency slightly but significantly reduces variance, which has greater perceptual impact~\cite{jack_effect_2016}. An alternative is to migrate to an ESP32-S3 (e.g., Nano~ESP32) using ESP-IDF for audio-synchronous tasks. Such a move enhances timing flexibility but introduces new dependencies and the need to port drivers (e.g., I\textsuperscript{2}C FRAM). Any platform-specific adaptation must therefore be fully documented and archived to preserve the project’s reproducibility and open-access integrity.


\subsection{Exhibition Enhancements}

Exhibition deployment should couple the interface more closely to its acoustic and tactile context, within constraints of headphone use and gallery sound policy. The following enhancements align with perceptual evidence while keeping the chain open and reproducible.

\subsubsection{Convolution}

A lightweight convolution stage matches the rendered instrument to the venue’s impulse response at the listening point, reducing the disconnect between headphones and space. Open-back headphones preserve some room cues; optional IMU head-tracking via an Arduino Nano 33 BLE~\cite{franvcek_performance_2023}\footnote{an example of which can be found at
    \url{https://github.com/ysoldak/HeadTracker}} adds stabilised binauralisation when appropriate.

\subsubsection{Vibration}

Low-frequency, low-level tactile feedback increases immersion and reported audio quality in seated contexts~\cite{merchel_vibratory_2009,merchel_functional_2018}. For headphone-based exhibits, vibration signals should follow the noise-reduction practices in~\cite{merchel_functional_2018} (lower cut-offs, octave shifting where needed), acknowledging the different acuity of touch~\cite{summers_single_2000}. For keyboard use, subtle keybed or bench actuation provides whole-body cues without compromising museum noise policies. Findings in~\cite{young_musical_2018} suggest kinaesthetic factors dominate, but vibrotactile additions remain beneficial.

\subsubsection{Encouraging Engagement}

Visitor behaviour in the oratory suggests that some passers-by do not recognise the interface as “meant to be touched,” a learned museum norm~\cite{howes_introduction_2014,classen_touch_2005}. Signage helps but may be insufficient or ambiguous~\cite{khalifa_visitors_2025}. A gentle visual affordance can disambiguate: repurpose the existing RGB LEDs (Figures~\ref{fig:mark1-leds} and \ref{fig:mark-2}) for a slow, warm “breathing” pattern during idle. This differentiates the interface from adjacent historical instruments without compromising the aesthetic integration; the calibration indicators then serve a secondary role as an engagement cue.

\subsection{Application}

Beyond conservation and exhibition, the interface serves as a research probe and a platform for new performance practices.

\subsubsection{As Research Probe}

The system enables controlled studies on the role of kinaesthetic and vibrotactile feedback, and on the influence of visual aesthetics, analogous to studies using Yamaha AvantGrand and Disklavier pianos~\cite{fontana_perception_2018,charalampos_role_2018}. Because registers can be disengaged mechanically while still emitting MIDI, experiments can independently vary pluck occurrence and timing (e.g., staggering effects), with Mark~2 intended for this role. The modular PCB approach in Section~\ref{sec:future-pcb-layout} supports deployment on differing jack pitches and on multi-register instruments, enabling comparison across the augmented interface, an acoustic instrument, and traditional MIDI systems~\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}. Such work tests whether faithfully reproducing the mechanical action and kinaesthetic forces suffices for “tactile realism” before pursuing more elaborate electromechanical emulation~\cite{mcalpine_sampling_2014,gillespie_virtual_1994,lozada_novel_2007,timmermans_multibody-based_2020}.

\subsubsection{Old Interfaces, New Expression}

While the conservation use case motivates the design, the same sensing opens expressive controls unavailable to historical instruments. With continuous displacement streams before and after pluck, the firmware can map post-onset gestures to synthesis parameters, analogous to MIDI aftertouch~\cite{wiffen_prophet_1983,jungleib_complete_1983} but tailored to the harpsichord action. Additional pressure sensing at the keybed or jack rail~\cite{hamilton_two_2025} augments this space. In this way, the interface both preserves kinaesthetic information inherent in historical performance and enables contemporary expressions rooted in that tactile grammar, addressing long-standing debates about post-onset control in keyboard instruments~\cite{macritchie_using_2015,woolley_musical_2017}.


\section{MAGPIE}\label{sec:future-magpie}

Improvements to \textsc{magpie} can be split between those of functionality and
those of interface. The core functions of \textsc{magpie} would already provide
support for number of research use cases, but further development is needed to
enable it as a powerful open source tool for musical acoustics. Similarly, there
is functionality that exists but a efficient interface is needed in order to
full utilise it. \textsc{magpie} seeks to turn traditionally physics based
problems into a problems of engineering and interface is key in order to
facilitate that.

Development within \textsc{magpie} can be focussed within two directions. One in
functionality and the other in portability. The current iteration is a capable
illustrative tool for educational purposes and some specific material
measurement scenarios. The application for tone wood still needs to be developed
further. Similarly, the tool is available now in the open source language
Python, but the current interface relies on the Jupyter notebook system, which
comes with its own technical and portability challenges. One key area for
expansion is to translate the source code to a compiled language such as
\textsc{C}~/~\textsc{C++} and Rust. Such a transition provides more granular
control of compiler optimisations, but more interestingly opens potential for
deployment.

The primary bottleneck for development is the eigensolver stage. In
M\textsc{atlab} this achieved with \texttt{eigs} and in python the SciPy linear
algebra library's \texttt{scipy.sparse.linalg.eigs}. In both cases, these
eigensolvers are an interface to the ARPACK library \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998}.
For M\textsc{atlab} there is analysis of the target matrix to which the user is
not privvy. M\textsc{atlab} its own sparse library on top of ARPACK
\cite{gilbert_sparse_1992} which obfuscates the process further.
M\textsc{atlab}'s \texttt{eigs} function is not one supported by the software's
code generation systems and the function still requires the M\textsc{atlab}
runtime if it is to be interfaced with externally. This has implications for
portability issues, with development being constrained to those with paid access
to the M\textsc{atlab} environment.

In the case of \textsc{C++}, the relevant technologies available for interfacing
with ARPACK library. First is the open source Eigen library for C++%
\footnote{\url{https://gitlab.com/libeigen/eigen}} and the Spectra library%
\footnote{\url{https://spectralib.org}} for eigenvalue problems. Spectra
consists of a re-write of ARPACK solvers in C++ allowing for direct native
compilation.

\subsection{Functionality}

What is covered here is development of the core functionality as it is apart of
the \textsc{magpie} framework. Such development means the rewriting and current
\textsc{magpie} function suite, as well as the addition of further functions. As
an open source project, the state of the project is constantly fluid. At time of
writing the latest release of \citetitle{magpie_matlab_2025}, which consists of
the functionality as discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis}.

\subsubsection{Orthotropic Case}

The grid spacing ($h$) for \textcite{magpie_matlab_2025} is currently shared in
both the x and y directions. This has the major implication of the model being
most accurate for integer ratio plate dimensions. Currently the system is
optimum  where the ratio  of plate dimensions $L_x$ and $L_y$ is equal to the
ratio of the number of grid points in each dimension $N_x$ and $N_y$. The
alternative approach is to choose a grid spacing and truncate $\frac{L_x}{h}$
and $\frac{L_y}{h}$. Shared grid spacing has the effect of the system losing
accuracy outside of these very tight constraints.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{img/plot-grid-error.pdf}
    \caption[Grid space error]{Plot illustrating analytic fifth mode of a plate compared to an isotropic model with shared an independent grid spacing in the x and y dimensions. Shared spacing exhibits saw convergence while the independent spacing converges much more smoothly.}
    \label{fig:grid-error}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:grid-error} illustrates the error that arises from such an
approach. The reason for the behaviour of the shared grid spacing plot is that
effective plat dimensions are altered. This is only feasible for small $N_x$ and
$N_y$, since the total grid size $N_{xy}$ increases with the $NxNy$ the system
grows exponentially larger. For example, the plate in
Figure~\ref{fig:grid-error} is of dimensions $L_x$ = 100.1 cm and $L_y$ = 81.1cm
a grid size of  $N_x$ = 1001 points and $N_y$ = 811 points would be needed to
cancel out the error. The number of bytes for the sparse biharmonic scales
$N_{xy}$ by a factor of 104, with 13 diagonals and double precision. Additional
overhead is also required for the eigensolver stage which, with benchmarking,
adds a further factor of 50. A shared grid spacing would require approximately
4.2 gigabytes of RAM before it would match the accuracy of the independent $h_x,
    h_y$ grid spacing. By contrast, the final grid size to the rightmost of
Figure~\ref{fig:grid-error} comes at a cost of \~118 megabytes of RAM for the
sparse biharmonic and eigensolver. Where this is relevant is deployment in
resource constrains settings such as part of an embedded system, where RAM and
processing requirements come at a financial cost of implementation or in the
context of a web browser where overall performance will be much slower compared
to a native implementation.

Features such as independent grid spacing, which is implemented as part of an
orthotropic plate model, have been merged into the the M\textsc{atlab} library
repository of \textsc{magpie}, which  were based on the orthotropic and lumped
mass model as described in \textcite{ducceschi_method_2024}. The source is not
yet in a format which would be distributable. The same principles in exploring
the isotropic case can be applied to the orthotropic case, magpie would be
capable of applying the same process to the  elastic constants of a wooden
plate, two elastic moduli $E_x$ and $E_y$ and  a shear modulus $G_{xy}$.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{img/koster_keyboard_1994_p49.png}
    \caption[Soundboard bracing of a seventeenth-century Couchet harpsichord]{Soundboard bracing of a seventeenth-century Couchet harpsichord
        from \textcite[p.~49]{koster_keyboard_1994}.}
    \label{fig:bracing}
\end{figure}

% \textcite[p.~93]{koster_keyboard_1994}.} \caption{Soundboard bracing of an
% eighteenth-century Kirkman harpsichord from \textcite[p.~% 99]{koster_keyboard_1994}.}


The latest developments to \textsc{magpie} have implemented a lumped mass model.
The addition of lumped masses to the system allows for modelling of the wooden
braces that provide strength to musical instrument soundboards
(Figure~\ref{fig:bracing}). Furthermore, lumped masses should also provide a
means to compensating for the effects of components such a as accelerometers or
exciters which are attached to plate as part of experimental measurement, such
as the accelerometer in \textcite{duran_experimentally_2023}. Such a set of
features are not best explored through a code interface, but instead can best be
leveraged with graphical user interfaces.

\subsection{Graphical User Interfaces}

A graphical user interfaces provide a means to change multiple parameters
quickly in a way that is more intuitive for the end user. In addition to
intuitive access to a libraries functions, there is also interactions that arise
from constraints \cite{wang_artful_2018}.%
\footnote{``Principle 1.11 Design is constraints which give rise to interactions.''}
\textsc{magpie} as a software library naturally provides a programmatic
interface in each of its implementations. Complex, algorithmic iteration over
parameters that allow for simulations to be run over long periods without
intervention. Providing the user with a huge parameter space is only as powerful
as the ability to explore it. There are commonly subtle transition points where
small changes in any one parameter will have a dramatic affect on mode shapes or
frequencies. When the user is constrained, however, to the interesting areas of
the parameter space then the system becomes much easier to explore. Likewise,
the small adjustments in parameters are isolated to a single mouse interaction
which would be otherwise difficult or unwieldy to change and explore if it were
attempted to be achieved through the command-line interface or re-compilation of
software.

An example of the functionality that emerges from a GUI would be in the
presentation of computational results versus those of a real experiment. One
could iterate over a range of values, but there is no intuitive way to start
exploring subranges within the larger parameter space; interrupting exploration
in one space to tangentially explore another.

Alternatively as a view that would provide feedback on the percentage error of
each mode like as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:error-bars}. Serving the dual
function of fine tuning results but also as practical understanding and
exploring how each parameter impacts the simulation.

The user should be given the flexibility to change slider limits based on the
sub-parameter space they are trying to explore. Resolution, or step size, of a
graphical slider is limited by the resolution of on which it is displayed.
Minute movements are not a pleasant solution to the problem of sub-parameter
space exploration. Instead, the user could be allowed to edit the limits
directly giving a method to move from a macro level exploration of a parameter
zoom into a sub-range of interest (Figure~\ref{fig:slider-subrange})

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:jupyter}, an interface for the python version
of \textsc{magpie} \cite{magpie_python_2025} has already been created using the
Jupyter notebooks development environment. The Jupyter interface
\cite{magpie_jupyter_2025} is quite limited, and allows for only those designs
which can easily implemented using the IPywidgets. Functionality such as
adjustable range constraints, though technically possible, are more easily
implemented.  Similarly, M\textsc{atlab}'s App Designer%
\footnote{\url{https://uk.mathworks.com/products/matlab/app-designer.html}}
would provide the same mode switching for end users. The App Designer is
constrained to only those elements made available.

        experimental results. The $R_{0y}$ and $K_{0y}$ parameters provide an
        example of an editable a slider sub-range for finer parameter control. Both

M\textsc{atlab} also has deployment constraints as \texttt{eigs} function is not
one that is supported by the software's code generation systems and the function
still requires the M\textsc{atlab} runtime if it is to be interfaced with
externally. Such constraints have implications on the accessibility and
maintainability, with practical development being constrained to only those who
have paid for access to the M\textsc{atlab} environment. That is not to say that
the M\textsc{atlab} and Jupyter GUIs are redundant, on the contrary, they
facilitate quick parameter exploration that could subsequently be automated
directly through the library. Neither Jupyter nor M\textsc{atlab}'s App Designer
are permissive to all design approaches, but with the implementation of the
library in C++ a number of possibilities become available. This section will
discuss some potential deployments for a \textsc{magpie} GUI, the technical
requirements, and what benefits there are to gained form such development.

\subsubsection{C++ Interface}

Graphical user interfaces are typically platform specific, Cocoa for macOS,
win32 for windows and either GTK or QT for Linux operating systems, each of
which require platform specific implementation. The JUCE framework therefore
provides an appealing solution to target all 3 core operating systems
simultaneously, with its cross-platform native wrappings for audio libraries and
OpenGL for graphics card rendering. The more graphics orientated, but still open
source, \texttt{bgfx}%
\footnote{\url{https://github.com/bkaradzic/bgfx}} may also be more appropriate
given its support of multiple 3D graphics rendering libraries. If the ultimate
goal is provide a tool that is easily accessible and cross-platform,
developments within web technology may provide the best solution.

\subsubsection{Web Technology}\label{sec:wasm}

Porting \textsc{magpie} to a compiled language like \textsc{C++} open up a
number of exciting possibilities. Similarity in results to that of
M\textsc{atlab} give an indication of a viable plate analysis tool that is not
constrained to only those system on which M\textsc{atlab} is supported. Of
particular  note is the possibility of deploying \textsc{magpie} as an entirely
web based application. Traditional software has typically required some form of
installation process, where binaries are compiled for a specific machine
architecture. Web technologies do away with part of that necessity by delivering
software directly through a browser. One such technology is Web Assembly (WASM)
\url{https://webassembly.org}, which allows for optimised binary code to be
executed by a virtual machine in the browser. This is achieved through the
emscripten,%
\footnote{\url{https://emscripten.org}} an open source compiler that enables
compilation of \textsc{C}~/~\textsc{C++} code into WASM. Assembly code capable
of more performative execution than the equivalent JavaScript
\cite{reiser_accelerate_2017}. When combined with the native \textsc{C++} source
of Eigen and Spectra, the emscripten compiler can potentially be used to
distribute and execute \textsc{magpie} entirely in the browser.%
\footnote{Such an application using the Eigen library has already been
    demonstrated as feasible\\ \url{https://observablehq.com/@rreusser/eigen}} To
make a web tool like \textsc{magpie} would traditionally require server
architecture in order to execute ARPACK or to host a M\textsc{atlab} runtime. In
a server / client arrangement the bottlenecks on performance are then tied to
the slowest of the network and the computation. Creating a \textsc{magpie}
server would also not be without cost and would also be limited by concurrent
usage.

The server and client can be on the same machine, which is exactly the
architecture for the Jupyter interface (Section~\ref{sec:jupyter}). The extra
technical requirements for installation of a local server reintroduces platform
specific behaviour and limitations, which the browser client is meant to avoid.
Computational performance in this scenario is also affected compared to an
equivalent system using natively compiled binaries. The alternative for
\textsc{magpie} to execute entirely on the client-side would be an
implementation of a sparse matrix library and eigensolver entirely within
JavaScript, the result of which would be severely limited in performance
\cite{lion_investigating_2022}. \textsc{magpie} would likely see much more
utility if development is pursued for an entirely native or entirely web client
approach.

The usage of WASM would allow for compilation for both approaches from a single,
shared codebase. It is worth noting that the above web technologies are not
supported equally across all browsers and their versions.%
\footnote{\url{https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/WebAssembly\#browser_compatibility}}
Such limitation of highlight an importance of both open sourcing and version
control as multiple systems and deployments can more easily be supported.

WASM addresses the porting of the core \textsc{magpie} to the web browser, but
there still remains a question o of interface. The Jupyter version of
\textsc{magpie} (Section~\ref{sec:jupyter}) demonstrates one potential
interface, but this is limited by the communication happening between IPython
the renderer for the browser. Breaking away from the constraint of Jupyter
allows for direct usage of further browser technology, notably the WebGL and
WebGPU \cite{chickerur_webgl_2024} which allow fast 3D drawing within the
browser. Figure~\ref{fig:magpie-p5js} shows a prototype example of the first
four eigenshapes of a plate above their corresponding `chladni pattern'
projected shadows.%
\footnote{\url{https://mhamilt.github.io/eigenvis/}} The plates in
Figure~\ref{fig:magpie-p5js} are drawn using the \texttt{p5.js} library which
has an interface to WebGL.%
\footnote{\url{https://p5js.org/reference/p5/WEBGL/}}

Each plate in comprised of approximately 1.5k grid points, for a total 12k grid
points across four displacement and four projected shadows. The interface render
at approximately 11 frames per second (FPS). The interface is an early proof of
concept, using eigenvectors from the \textsc{c++} version of \textsc{magpie}
output as JavaScript arrays. Although the plates are rendered at a low FPS, this
would likely be improved by implementing displacement and shading as part of
vertex and fragment shaders rather than direct drawing using \texttt{p5.js}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{img/magpie-p5js.png}
    \caption[Prototype interface using WebGL]{Prototype interface using WebGL via \texttt{p5.js} to render plate
        modes.}
    \label{fig:magpie-p5js}
\end{figure}

M\textsc{atlab} has functionality to compile its code to WASM
\cite{mcvittie_generatejavascriptusingmatlabcoder_2021}, however some
functionality such as that in M\textsc{atlab}'s sparse libraries is not
currently open to code generation and compilation to a binary outside of the
M\textsc{atlab} runtime%
\footnote{(list of functions supported:
    \url{https://uk.mathworks.com/help/referencelist.html?type=function&capability=codegen&listtype=alpha}}
and it is unlikely to be in the near future.

A key development for \textsc{magpie} is the addition of an orthotropic model so
match more closely that of the quarter-sawn wood as is used for string
instrument soundboards. Even further, the addition of braces and lumped masses
to the internal system to mimic that of the structural bracing found on physical
instruments \cite{ducceschi_open_2024}.

Presenting the tool kit in an open source well-structure manner should hopefully
encourage usage, collaboration and development as the system matures.

\section{Closing Remarks}

This thesis has intended to illustrate the importance of an open methodology as
well as demonstrate two use cases for its importance within digital research
When applies to digital conservation, the intention is to show that we must take
the same approach to conservation of cultural heritage as we do to the
methodologies applied to carry it out. Two projects were presented, an interface
for conserving the instructional value of musical instrument heritage artefacts
and a plate analysis software toolkit. Both projects look to tackle polar ends
of the heritage conservation spectrum, the act conservation and the act of
engagement with that which is conserved. There is an ``as above so below''
symmetry to digital cultural heritage, where both the heritage and the digital
tools require conservation. With musical instrument heritage there is the added
value of interaction and instruction which are not simply conserved by creating
a digital facsimile of that heritage. Instead we must look at alternatives for
conserving experience alongside the raw data of dimensions, materials and
construction.

% Both digital and analogue objects rely on physical storage
% mediums\nocite{Rubio2020}, but the financial and energy cost of preserving
% something digitally can often be orders of magnitude greater than an
% `analogue' equivalent\nocite{Cieply2007}.

The open methodology described here has tried to demonstrate the need for
careful separation of components into digital repositories in order that each
component can be reusable and iterated upon without affecting others. The
keyboard interface is separated into physical mechanical, digital software, and
the boundary between both in the form of electronics hardware designed in
software. Software itself has been separated into components of data, processing
and interface. Each digital component is stored in a version control repository,
whose functions are many. The version control repository is a tool that
simultaneously allows for access to older versions of source code, ensuring the
ability to access older versions of software, but it can also serve as a record
of how ideas are developed.

Through online git services, the version control repository can also act as the
means of software being open source. Git repositories are decentralised and a
combination of local and cloud service storage provides some degree of parity
and preservation, but one this is coincidental and not deliberate. Services such
as GitHub are owned and operated by commercial entities ad should not be
depended upon to provide an resolvable DOI and digital archive. Relying on web
archive projects to keep a record of these repositories is neither deliberate
nor reliable \cite{calano_github_2025}. That responsibility should instead lie
with institutes. Academic institutes do not defer responsibility of storing and
distributing academic research literature from the Microsoft library and the
same should also be true for research software.

The Zenodo archive operated from CERN provides integration with GitHub, reducing
the impedance to archiving digital software and providing a DOI. The DOI
functions is a similar manner to the ISBN and ISSN, identifying a digital
artefact without ambiguity. Enabling software citation through a DOI stresses
software's importance, permitting attribution, while facilitating access and
version specificity \cite{soito_citations_2016}. Research to rely on an external
service, like Zenodo, is perhaps still an abdication of responsibility.
Institutes have support for digital archives such as the Edinburgh Research
Archive%
\footnote{\url{https://era.ed.ac.uk/}} at the University of Edinburgh and
Sistema Biblioteca Atteneo%
\footnote{\url{https://sba.unibo.it/en/almadl/almadl-services/assigning-a-doi}}
at the Università di Bologna, but these services are still tailored toward
traditional publications and datasets rather than software repositories. Both of
these institutes already host their own online git services.%
\footnote{UoE ECDF \url{git.ecdf.ed.ac.uk} and multiple departmental GitLab
    services at Unibo.} The ideal scenario would be where these services are simply
connected together allowing institutes to take responsibility of both version
control and it's archiving.

Academic research is capable of long term usage of software, ARPACK from 1990
\cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998} (discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis}) serves as
one example. Aurora (Section~\ref{sec:aurora}) from just five years later is now
at risk of no longer being useable without intervention and development.
Research software access and permanence should not be a coin toss weighted by
the extent of its adoption. Aurora demonstrated this by being both widely
adopted and at risk of obsolescence. Instead, a more deliberate methodology is
needed for research software to be accessible and reproducible as is the
expectation with other aspects of academic research.

The praxis of such a open methodology does not come without cost labour and
time. Researcher are likely already learning a new skill with respect to
programming. With the addition of repository organisation, drafting
documentation, learning a version control system and archiving development time
can be inflated as a researcher orientates themself with these new tools.

Adopting an open methodology as standard practice lessened these costs as
support networks are formed. It is important to remember that here are no
shortcuts in good research and anything worth doing is worth doing well, but
acknowledgment of this cost raises an important issue. With respect to the
humanities, there is still a gap in  digital skills and best practices in data
sustainability that needs to be closed \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. Academic
institutes will also need to take a proactive approach with digital skills
literacy if those in the humanities research are to follow best practice with
the respect to digital research.

We do not only have to think of the impact of software sustainability if digital
research, but also what the implication is for digital cultural heritage. When
applied to cultural heritage, it is paramount that we approach digital
conservation through a critical lens, otherwise we risk active harm to the
heritage at stake
\cite{rubio_work_2020,schwarz_disk_2006,parry_museums_2010,cieply_afterlife_2007}.
Whatever approach is taken to digital conservation, it should be carried out
deliberately, with the consequences of conservation decisions being intentional,
not accidental. \textcite{barclay_preservation_2005} and
\textcite{desilvey_curated_2017} proclaim the same thing We should not
``pickle''\cite{desilvey_curated_2017} the past as that is what we are inclined
to do, neither should we idly transition musical instruments across states of
currency, conservation, and restoration while neglecting the consequences of
those decisions. Better yet, if there is neglect should consequences be damned
let it be explicit \cite[p.~75-76]{barclay_preservation_2005}. Similarly for the
digital tools we employ in the process of that conservation. If these digital
tools are disposable, then there should be intentionality in the ephemerality.
If these tools should be enduring, then steps need to be taken to make sure they
endure.
