\section{Interaction}\label{sec:future-interaction}

Developments to the the keyboard interface can be split between further refinement in both its execution, improvements when deployed within an exhibition context and further application beyond the exhibition at San Colombano.
Refinements to the interface include the adoption of CAD/CAM processes during the mechanical interface construction process as well as major and minor improvements to the executions of the optical sensing system.

\subsection{CAD/CAM processes}\label{sec:cad-cam-jacks}

A major limitation across all interfaces discussed in Chapter~\ref{} is the gradient stickers used for the sensor. Although the stickers are printed and cut by machines, allowing for large quantities to be made, they still need to be applied by hand. In the application process it not uncommon for the gradient stickers to be too wide. The short edge of the jacks were roughly similar, at around 4.35 mm, but they were made by manually resulting in variation across the full set. The jacks and the jack-slide are wooden and such prone to expansion and contraction with changes in humidity. This was the case for when the Mark 1 (Sections~\ref{sec:mark-1}) was moved from the laboratory, where many jacks that were freely moving became constricted in their slots. This meant that the gradient stickers either needed trimmed or replaced entirely, with reapplication also necessitating re-calibration. Inspecting and adjusting 98 jacks was a time consuming task and one ripe for optimisations.
The adhesive for the stickers was hi-tack meaning that they were unlikely to fall off. The lifespan of the adhesive is finite, rated to around four years for outdoor use \cite{antalis_coala_2025}. Factoring in that the jacks are moving, creating friction with the inside of the jack slot, will likely mean the lifespan of the sticker adhesive is shorter.

Preferably, the surface for the optical sensors would be integral to the construction of the jack. Given the small area of a 4.35mm short edge an ~8mm travel, creating a surface of varying reflectance by hand is not feasible. The process would therefore need to be automated or computer aided.
Two immediate options present themselves: the modification of the current jacks or CAD/CAM fabrication of the jacks entirely, the latter of which has become much more accessible in recent years. CAD/CAM in this context includes 3D printing, computer numerical control routing, and laser cutting, which requires either 3D modelling or vector graphics software. 
For 3D modelling, AutoDesk's Fusion 360\footnote{\url{https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/personal}} has a free tier available for personal use and was used for the modelling throughout the project. Vector graphics for laser cutting can be achieved either through open source projects like InkScape,\footnote{\url{https://inkscape.org}}. Given the very simple but large quantity of shapes involved many of the laser cutting vector files were produced through the Processing\footnote{\url{https://processing.org}} development environment. 
Though they may not be always used a final design, CAD/CAM prototypes ease access to fabrication process and permit much quicker iterations on designs.

Research into both modification of the jacks or their CAD/CAM manufacture were undertaken, but ultimately a satisfactory result compared to the gradient sticker approach was not found. Both approaches showed promise but were ultimately too unrefined to be applied to the Mark 1.

For modification, a laser cutting approach was tested. The hypothesis was for a speckle pattern to be etched into the side of the jack to alter reflectance. A number of materials were tested including MDF, plywood and hardwood. It was found that the available laser cutters were not able to etch a pattern with enough  differentiation between light and dark points. Both a rasterised and vector approach were attempted and it was found that the marks left by the laser cutter were too faint (Figure~\ref{fig:laser-speckle}. When power was increased, scorching of the material bled into the surrounding area, ruining the gradient from high to low reflectance. 
It is possible that the optical sensor could be wired to an amplifier circuit and tuned to a smaller, more sensitive range, but pursuing this approach would require a major redesign of the electronics system.

For CAD/CAM fabrication two methods were tested, a computer numerical control (CNC) router and 3D printing both of which could share the same 3D model (Figure~\ref{fig:jack-model}). The CNC router approach was the first taken as the intention was to pair it with the laser cutter to etch a pattern, which would hopefully offset defect rate of either process by being able to manufacture at a greater volume with far less manual labour. Once it was found the laser cutter approach was not viable, a second design was needed. Rather than reflectance from a speckle pattern, the concept returned to the distance application of the optical sensors.

Taking inspiration from the rolling shutter of a camera,
section of the jack would be cut out (Figure~\ref{}. As the cut out section passed the optical sensor, a larger amount of light would be reflected. The baffles of the sensor would help restrict the field of view limit the light bleed between sensors. CNC routing of the jack bodies turned out to be time consuming for only a small number. The available router model and CNC bits were also prone to breaking , particularly around the damper slots, where there as commonly tear out. Finally, the materials for the jacks are typically hardwood such as pear, service or beech tree. Available MDF and plywood materials were far less dense than what would ideally be used in the final version and defect rate was unlikely to improve without extensive testing experimentation with router bits. The wood for the jack bodies would need to be planed first then likely adjusted by hand for width after the CNC process. Given these limitations the CNC, pursuing that option did not seem viable. The design pivoted instead to a 3D printing approach using PLA as had been used for the brackets and baffles of the sensor board. The 3D print of single jack body was also only 12 minutes, meaning it could be scaled.

Test using this system were promising but there were a number of limitations. The tongues and plectra still needed fitted to all jacks, which would either need to be done after the printing or as part of the print. 
Comparing PLA \cite{bcn3d_pla_2019} to Delrin \cite{dupont_delrin_2017}, a common modern substitute for quill plectra \cite{barclay_preservation_2005}, suggested that the PLA would be too brittle and difficult to rework for adjusting the voicing of the jack. For the first implementation at scale, the risk was determined to not be worth the additional time cost of adjusting gradient stickers which, in the context of the full install process, was relatively small by comparison.

\subsection{Latency and Jitter}

Though sensor system is working effectively in the measurement of jack displacement, there is still room for improvement with respect to latency. Latency in this case is the time between a string being plucked and the corresponding MIDI signal being sent. As covered in Section~\ref{sec:latency}, the latency of the current system averages at 2 milliseconds with 1 millisecond of jitter. The latency is bottlenecked by two points in the system: the sensor signal measurement and then in the microcontroller itself.
Limitation in the sensor signal measurement is affected the inherent parasitic capacitance that is present on all transistors. The microcontroller is limited by the response time of the phototransistors. Even if the response rate is improved the microcontroller, or its firmware, could still contribute to suboptimal latency. As such, in terms of latency, the sensor signal measurement should be addressed first. Besides latency the microcontroller is the sole contributor to jitter. Irregular timing in the central firmware loop results in deviation in time. This section will cover the methods for reducing jitter, but the consequences that impact the rest of design as a result.

\subsubsection{Sensor Signal Measurement}\label{sec:tia}

Currently the signal of the sensors are in voltage divider configuration using a pull-up resistor and a signal on the sensor board is selected through the use of a multiplexer. The voltage divider works as current is induced through the phototransistor, a voltage is then present across the pull-up resistor. This voltage can be measured against transistor current by creating a signal path between the pull-up resistor and the phototransitor collector that is then connected to an analogue-to-digital converter. 
The motivations behind this configuration were manifold, but each other related back to the principles of accessibility and reproducibility within FAIR framework.
A single resistor is a cheap and simplistic method for measuring the current through the transistor as voltage with a low technical barrier to implement it.
It is also more reproducible as the resistor is a fundamental component of electronics.
The specific model and manufacturer of the resistor may become obsolete, but the very idea of   a resistor at the value of 100 k$\Omega$s will not.
The same cannot be said for the integrated circuits (ICs) as used to package operational amplifiers, resistor networks, programmable memory \&c. which may ``here yesterday, gone today'',  becoming obsolete or unavailable for all manner of reasons \cite[p. 273]{horowitz_art_2015}.
There are problems to which only these more complex ICs can provide a solution.
The phototransistor has an inherent parasitic capacitance, which manifests as a low pass filter \cite{falco_understanding_2023} on the output signal and thus slower response time.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER Basic TIA circuit using a photodiode \cite[Figure 4.22]{horowitz_art_2015}.}
    \label{fig:tia-basic}
\end{figure}


Instead of the pull-up resistor formation a number of transimpedance amplifier (TIA) circuits can be implemented. 
The most basic TIA for phototransistors can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-basic}, where the output voltage $V_{\text{out}}$ is the current flowing from the non-inverting input of the op-amp through the (in this case) photodiode $I_{C}$, scaled by the feedback resistor $R_{f}$.

$$
V{out} = I_{C}R_{div}
$$


As noted in \textcite{horowitz_art_2015}, this circuit should be assumed to include a capacitor in parallel to the feedback resistor. The phototransistor will begin conducting with a lower light presence, if their is a bias voltage present across the collector and emitter ($V_{CE}$). An example of such a circuit can be see in \textcite{pardue_near_2013} (Figure~\ref{fig:vb-tia}(b)).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER (a) pull-up resistor configuration of the phototransistor (b) TIA circuit with voltage offset $V_b$. From \cite[Figure 3]{pardue_near_2013}.}
    \label{fig:tia-bias}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:tia-bias} there is a bias voltage $V_{b}$ which increases the voltage between the phototransistor collector and emitter, which in turn should improve the response time of the phototransistor \cite{falco_understanding_2023}. 

$$
V_{out} = V_{b} + I_{C}R_{1}
$$

Rail-to-rail op-amps are those that permits the voltage output to be up to the limits of the supply voltage, where op-amps typically limit before this point. In the case of the biased TIA above, there is a risk that the op-amp will be saturated unless it is rail-to-rail. To bypass this limitation, the output voltage can be biased down by introducing a voltage at the inverting input, which is the configuration in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff} \cite{mcpherson_portable_2013}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER TIA difference amplifier from \textcite[Figure 3]{mcpherson_portable_2013}.}
    \label{fig:tia-diff}
\end{figure}


Voltage output $V_{\text{out}}$ in this circuit becomes:

$$
V_{out} = V_{ref} + I_{C}R_{5} + \frac{R_5}{R_4}(3.3 - V_{\text{ref}})
$$

With the addition of $R_4$ to bias the output voltage, the op-amp is now in a differential amplifier configuration. 
Typically for a difference amplifier the ratio of resistor for each channel should be equal for optimal common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) \cite{olson_optimizing_2023}. 
Using the convention of Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff}, the rule for would be $\frac{R_5}{R_4} = \frac{R_2}{R_3}$. 
With the addition of a current source in the form of the phototransistor, optimum CMRR is less definite, with $\frac{R_5}{R_4} \approx \frac{R_2}{R_3}$. Given variation in all components, this becomes more of an engineer exercise,  with best noise reduction found through trial and error. Typically a difference amplifier could employ a quad network of resistors \cite[p. 227]{horowitz_art_2015}. The asymmetric biasing of Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff} values means that the four unique, high-tolerance resistor, values are needed. 

Variation across the optical sensors means that the feedback (or load) resistor 
\footnote{$R_5$ in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff}, $R_1$ in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-bias}}
may need adjustment across all sensors as was the case in \textcite{pardue_near_2013}.\footnote{``Early on it was noticed that different sensors of the same make yielded significantly different current response even when in the same configurations. With the QRE1113 sensors, we have used load resistors ranging between 12k$\Omega$ to 47k$\Omega$ to achieve similar range.''}
If multiple optical sensors are to share the same TIA, such as in \textcite{mcpherson_portable_2013}, then the values will always be a best average. A bespoke circuit for each sensor is feasible, but highly impractical. Given the sufficient operation of the current pull-up configuration, the improvement in response time would be sufficient without too much concern about additional noise.
Since op-amps can come in single, dual and quad packages \cite{ti_opa4377_2011}, it may be better use of space allow easier routing if the TIA circuits are located on the controller board close to the power supply, rather than the newly suggested signal boards. 

The decision to use TIA is not a trivial one and will have an impact on the requirements for the overall project.
The design principles of the keyboard (Section~\ref{sec:design-constraints}) taken in hand with the FAIR principles of the open methodology (Section~\ref{sec:open-methods}) informed the design of the electronics system. 
The intention was to demonstrate the creation of an interface using economic electronics, a low technical knowledge barrier without a dependence on specialist facilities.
Components for electronic system could be sourced and assembled using a modestly outfitted university maker space and small scale PCB manufacturing services such as JLCPCB or PCBWay.
\footnote{Although the current design is \textit{just} about practical with prototyping board, the assembly of it would not be enviable.}
Components such as op-amps are not commonly found in THT packaging, but rather in surface-mount device (SMD) format (Figure~\ref{fig:tht-vs-smd}).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER The QRE1113 optical sensor in both THT and SMD formats with a XX for scale.}
    \label{fig:tht-vs-smd}
\end{figure}


THT parts are more permissible to the prototyping process, allowing for ```spur of the moment'' \cite[p. 268]{horowitz_art_2015} circuit wiring. By contrast, SMD components are soldered directly to `pads' on the surface of a PCB. SMD are much smaller compared to their THT equivalent, saving on space and are typically much more energy efficient with reduced inductances \cite[p. 268]{horowitz_art_2015}. As a result, SMD components are much less permissive to the prototyping process compared to THT.
With SMD parts comes a technological burden, requiring access to equipment such as re-flow ovens and hot air rework station.
Soldering these components is possible with hand soldering tools, but the process becomes much less practical.

Alternatively, the burden can shifted to the PCB manufacturing services to detriment of cost, lead times and the ability to experiment.
Ten PCBs can potentially be wired ten different ways, but this can only be done if one has the facilities to achieve it.
The circuits for the Mark 1 and Mark 2 keyboards represent the crossing point where the THT-over-SMD principle applied to the design will need to be compromised in order for improvements to be enacted.

A move to entirely SMD components would be expected to reduce the footprint of the PCB by half \cite{prasad_surface_1997} allowing the space to be used for a refined op-amp design \cite{mcpherson_portable_2013} or the ability to install in more constrained spaces or flush mounted to surface as one edge of the PCB could be cleared of protruding components.

The sensor system would benefit from single piece mounting bracket which would allow for much more simplified installation. Given the open source nature of the project the advantages come from the ability to modify the design for a bespoke application.

\subsubsection{Optical Sensor}

Switching to another variety of optical sensor, such as the On Semiconductor QRD1114 \cite{onsemiconductor_qrd1114_2024} may yield better results. 
The optical characteristics of the QRD1114 \cite{onsemiconductor_qrd1114_2024} and the QRE1113 \cite{onsemiconductor_qre1113_2024} differ enough that resistor values of the TIA circuit in \textcite{mcpherson_portable_2013} will need to change. For clarity, a more generalised phototransitor TIA circuit is given in Figure~\ref{fig:general-tia}:

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER Generalised TIA circuit for a optoisolator such as the optical sensors QRE1113 and QRD1114.}
    \label{fig:general-tia}
\end{figure}

To restate the parameters and constraints of the generalised phototransitor TIA circuit: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item $R_{in}$ will dictate the impedance seem by the phototransistor $Q$. 
    \item $R_{f}$ will dictate scaling of change in collector current $I_{C}$ to the change output voltage $V_{\text{out}}$ such that $\Delta V_{\text{out}} = \Delta I_C R_f$  \cite{mcpherson_portable_2013}.
    \item CMRR is adjusted in the ratios of all four resistor, with an approximate rule of $\frac{R_f}{R_{in}} \approx \frac{R_1}{R_2}$
    \item $V_{\text{ref}}$ the resulting reference voltage of the voltage divider from $R_1$ and $R_2$ will apply result in voltage bias across the collector and emitter $V_{CE} \approx V_{\text{ref}}$
    \item to avoid the need for a rail-to-rail op-amp, the inverting input is biased by $V_{CC}$ resulting in a $V_{out}$ of:
\end{itemize}

$$
V_{out} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{cc} + I_{C}R_{f} + \frac{R_f}{R_{in}}V_{cc}(\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} - 1)
$$

Table~\ref{tab:qre-vs-qrd} shows the key electrical characteristics that differ between the QRE1113 and QRD1114.
The QRD1114 has a faster rise time and greater sensitivity \cite{onsemiconductor_qrd1114_2024} when compared to the QRE1113 \cite{onsemiconductor_qre1113_2024} and could be used as a drop-in replacement, with the remainder of the design being unchanged. While the QRD1114 has a faster rise time its asymmetric rise and fall times and the higher fall time suggests a greater presence parasitic capacitance. The effect can be mitigated by the usage of a smaller pull-up resistor ($R_1$ in Figure~\ref{fig:simple-schematic}) or smaller value $R_{in}$ in Figure~\ref{fig:general-tia}, the substitution of which would necessitate new choice of values for the remaining three resistors.
With careful choice of TIA circuit, the QRD1114 may provide the sensitivity required to reintroduce the laser-cut speckle pattern as suggested in Section~\ref{sec:cad-cam-jacks}.

\subsubsection{Microcontroller}

If the bottleneck of sensor response time is addressed, the second area for improvement is within the analogue-to-digital conversion of the signal. The firmware for the interface \cite{hamilton_firmware_2025} was designed to be portable to most microcontrollers that use the Arduino integrated development environment (IDE), in particular the Nano family of microcontrollers.
\footnote{It should be all, but there are always unforeseen exceptions.} 
The motivation behind this decision was two-fold, to permit experiments with multiple microcontrollers without the need for rewriting the source code and to further the accessibility of the project under the FAIR principles. 
The Arduino IDE is open source, but it has a track record within electronics education and as such presents a lower technical skill barrier compared to other microcontroller development platforms such as mbed, Platform.io or ESP-IDF.

The constraint of using platform independent libraries restricted the usage of high-precision timers within the firmware.
For the Mark 1, the central programme loop executes as quickly as possible, the result of which is jitter around the base latency for generating MIDI data. 
To counter act jitter a high-precision, typically hardware, timer would need to be used. The timer either sets a flag to trigger, or executes directly, the routine for reading sensor data and sending MIDI data.

Timer interrupts and high precision timer logic are chipset and microcontroller specific. 
Programming the hardware timer Atmel's ATMega family of microcontrollers \cite{gammon_interrupts_2012} is very different from Noridic's nRF52 microcontrollers \cite{wolff_realtime_2019}, such as is present on the Nano 33 BLE.
Commitment to a hardware timer interface or API immediately restricts the types microcontrollers which can be used. Timer code is also not easily ported without sacrificing functionality or adding unnecessary complexity.
Like the case of the TIA in Section~\ref{sec:tia}, improving the jitter will require a compromise the design and methodology principles of project. Once again it is compromise in accessibility and reproducibility. Not all microarchitectures are equal and neither will they be readily available long into the future. 

Restricting the microcontroller architecture comes at the risk of obsolescence. This is another reason why documentation and archiving of firmware is important as it can serve as a record of functionality.
The function of the sensor system at this point is fixed and archived. 
Refining the operation of the firmware through architecture specific code makes it much more likely that it would need to be rewritten for another architecture in the future.
The nRF52 of the Nano 33 BLE is proven to be more than capable of the task been a part of successful implementation in the Mark 1 keyboard with sub-optimal firmware.

Continuing to use the Nano 33 BLE, the firmware would be best improved through use of either Nordic's
\texttt{NRF\_TIMERx} API \cite{nordic_timer_2023, nordic_timer_2025} or the \texttt{mbed\:\:Ticker} API \cite{mbed_ticker_2023}. As a hardware timer, the \texttt{NRF\_TIMERx}  would provide the greatest temporal resolution and lowest jitter, but manual register setup would prove a much lower eas of use compared to the software \textsc{c++} and object orientated \texttt{mbed\:\:Ticker} API. 
Best practice dictates that the timer interrupt should be as short as possible.
\footnote{``Conventional wisdom among real-time programmers is that ISRs
should be as short as possible: clear the interrupt, maybe transfer a
few bytes of data, and exit.'' \cite{klingensmith_using_2019}}
By following this rule and to avoiding missed cycles, the latency using a timer may be slightly longer than without. As shown in \textcite{jack_effect_2016}, the critical factor is jitter so long as the baseline latency is short enough.

Another approach would be to use a different microcontroller entirely. 
A test was carried out using an Arduino Nano ESP32 which is a NORA-W106, a module containing an ESP32-S3. 
The benefit of this microcontroller is that it would allow the microcontroller to be swapped and the rest of the PCB designs would be unaffected.

Some research was carried during the course of the project using an ESP32 programmed with the Espressif development framework ESP-IDF.
The same logic as the original firmware was programmed and the latency was reduced to below 10 ms. ESP-IDF allows access to functionality of the ESP32 including regular processing of an internal audio buffer that is not exposed in the Arduino IDE. 
It is possible that the 10 ms latency could be improved upon with appropriate optimisation, but there would be a cost in the form of development time. 
Consideration would need to be made on the time to port existing source code for the I2C FRAM chip or fall back on a simpler storage option.

\subsection{Exhibition Enhancements}

There are site-specific possibilities for refining the manner in which the keyboard is deployed as part of exhibition. These improvements arise from spatial observations during the visit from Una Europa as well as holistic improvements from recent haptic research. These improvements address the very specific form the exhibition took where visitors listened to audio over headphones and that audio was generated by a sample library. The exact same approaches covered here may not be possible if the audio source from a loudspeaker or from a different audio synthesis engine altogether.

\subsubsection{Convolution}

The current audio engine is restrictive to the user experience as their is disconnect between the acoustics of the space and the those applied to the sampled harpsichord signal. An easy improvement would be the addition of a signal processing stage which convolves the audio engine output with an impulse response of the exhibition space at the point of audition. Coupled with open-back headphones, this would ideally prevent the user from being isolated from the space.
This could be further augmented through the use of IMU enabled binaural head-tracking, which can also be achieved through open source electronics such as the Arduino Nano 33 BLE \cite{franvcek_performance_2023}.\footnote{an example of which can be found at \url{https://github.com/ysoldak/HeadTracker}}

\subsubsection{Vibration}

To augment the user experience and immersion, low-pass filtered audio signal from the audio synthesis engine could be fed back to the user in the form of local vibrations. Research carried out by \textcite{merchel_vibratory_2009} and in \textcite{merchel_functional_2018}, looked at the addition of a shaker to a seat to simulate low vibrations as would felt during a concert. Audio from a 5.1 surround concert was summed and low-passed before being routed to an electrodynamical shaker in order to generates whole body vibration for participants. The studies showed an overall preference for the presence of seat vibrations among participants who also reported improved quality of the audio reproduction. Given the diffusion of audio in exhibition over headphones, adherence to noise reduction process in \textcite{merchel_functional_2018} would be recommended. Where headphones are used, a lower cut-off was used on the audio signal as well as octave shifting or reducing the vibration signal to its fundamental component. Since our touch is less refined than our hearing, there is greater scope for shifting the frequency content of an audio signal if it is to be experienced as vibration \cite{summers_single_2000}.
Though the \textcite{merchel_functional_2018} related to concert audio, the same principles should apply to the experience of playing instrument. 
\textcite{young_musical_2018} findings suggest that kinaesthetic feedback is the dominant haptic component with the most influence the performance experience, but that vibrotactile feedback does not have a negligible affect. The same principle applied, but to the key bed of the interface may provide another step towards immersion and is the grounds for further development and research.

\subsubsection{Encouraging Engagement}

An area of improvement that is far outside the scope of this thesis, but still worth covering, is the very nature of visitor interaction within the museum context.
Spatial observations of visitors during the workshop highlighted that a proportion of visitors walked past the Mark 1 without interacting with it.
There is the obvious point that visitors will naturally have different experience with same activity \cite{dorph_visitor_2019} and this can be partly put down to which model of visitor they fall into \cite{templeton_museum_2018}. There are also holistic factors to consider which may impact the level of engagement with the exhibit.

The Mark 1 currently sits within the oratory of the museum, surrounded by period instruments to which visitors can only be spectators. The ``hands-off'' model of museum curatorship is one that is typical of the twentieth-century museums, but this was not always the case \cite{howes_introduction_2014}. By contrast, the museums of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries were very much regarded as ``hands-on'' sites \cite{howes_introduction_2014}, where visitors had permission to touch the exhibits \cite{classen_touch_2005}.
The idea that we should look, but should not touch, in the museum context is very much learned
and reinforced by the environment.

By striving for comparable aesthetics to the instruments around, there perhaps arises ambiguity. Every exhibit in the museum is assumed to not be touched. Signage may also be a source of confusion \cite{khalifa_visitors_2025} as it encourages visitors to play the Mark 1, the one exception within the museum, but that may not be enough to overcome the ingrained idea of the museum as a ``site of pure spectatorship'' \cite{howes_introduction_2014}. One might instead hesitate and think twice that perhaps the signage providing permission is misplaced, the risk-benefit analysis of which might leave the visitor inclined to `leave well alone'.

A proposed means to overcome this behaviour is the usage of light. 
As part of an exhibition, light influences and encourages us where to look. 
Light grabs our intention and differentiates objects within the space.  
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mark1-leds} and \ref{fig:mark-2}, both keyboards contain an LED lighting system that is used as a visual aid during the calibration. Once calibration is complete, however, the LEDs lay completely dormant and unused. 
Since the aesthetic are similar to the surrounding historic instruments, the exhibition keyboard requires some method to differentiate it form the other, marking it out as different. 
The RGB LEDs may provide a means to achieve this and would give them a second purpose as part of the exhibition.
The diagnostic lighting of Figure~\ref{fig:mark1-leds} and \ref{fig:mark-2} is perhaps not the best aesthetic choice for the museum context.
More fitting would be a slow oscillating (breathing) warm white LED display which would activate after some period of dormancy. 
Such a display may be enough to catch the eye of visitors and proactively encourage interaction.

\subsection{Application}

As well as a means of engagement and conservation of instructional value within musical cultural heritage the keyboard interfaces presented in this thesis are not limited to just this application. This section briefly discusses further application and areas for research of which the augmented interface, or a future version, may have in other field of research.

\subsubsection{As Research Probe}

One of the richest avenues for further research using the interface is as part of a larger study that explores interface haptic feedback as well as the potency of visual aesthetics to player experience. In the same manner as the Yamaha AvantGrande\footnote{\url{https://uk.yamaha.com/en/musical-instruments/pianos/products/avantgrand/}} and Disklavier\footnote{\url{https://uk.yamaha.com/en/musical-instruments/pianos/products/disklavier/}} pianos have been used in perceptual studies of the effect of haptics on piano \cite{fontana_perception_2018, charalampos_role_2018}, so too could the interface and sensor system presented here be applied to harpsichord players.

The ability for the jacks to be disengaged and prevent plucking the string, but still capable of triggering a MIDI message is ripe for exploration. 
The ability to disengage the registers independent of each other permits research into the effects of staggering on player performance.
Involvement is such a studied is one of the intended uses of the Mark 2 interface, which will hopefully serve as a research probe to explore the unique characteristics of the harpsichord's haptic and and its impact on performance and quality perception.

The sensor system is also modular and can be tailored to another harpsichord with differing jack pitch spacing. The implementation of a PCB layout as described in section~\ref{sec:future-pcb-layout} should allow for deployment on those instruments with more than two registers.
Fitting the sensor system to full scale harpsichord should allow comparison between the augmented interface, an acoustic instrument and a traditional MIDI \textcite{mcalpine_sampling_2014}.

Such a study would go towards determining if the mechanical recreation, with kinaesthetic force feedback, is sufficient as part of a digital musical instrument before pursuing elaborate electromechanical emulations as suggested by \textcite{mcalpine_sampling_2014} and prototyped in a similar fashion to \textcite{gillespie_virtual_1994,lozada_novel_2007,timmermans_multibody-based_2020}. 
Begging the question, does recreating the harpsichord action at a smaller scale achieve tactile realism? The answer to which question would provide grounds for further work on a exclusively actuated keyboard. 

\subsubsection{Old Interfaces, New Expression}

Much has been discussed already about musical cultural heritage and the purpose of interface for providing a means for instructional engagement.
That is not to say that the purpose of such interfaces can and should be constrained to only this scope.
In Section~\ref{} we mentioned the research by \textcite{oboe_mikey_2006} which briefly hint at limitation in expressive for the harpsichord and more broadly keyboard instruments as a whole. 
The ability to affect the note after it has been played has long been a debate among piano, harpsichord and keyboard players alike \cite{}. With traditional acoustic keyboard instrument, this is just not possible. Further, the manner in which an organ key is pressed has been claimed to affect the transients of the notes, but this is has also been shown to be a false \cite{woolley_musical_2017}.

On the other hand, an augmented interface with sensors tracking key velocity and pressure is capable of providing these modes of expression.
This is part of the intention with the Mark 2 keyboard, which by its open and documented construction allows for wealth of modification possibilities within its firmware.
For traditional MIDI keyboards, aftertouch is not a new concept, beginning to be used in 1983 in the Prophet T8 \cite{wiffen_prophet_1983} and a part of the MIDI standard authored in the same year \cite{jungleib_complete_1983}.
The triggering system for MIDI notes works on passing thresholds in the optical sensor signal, but there is still a stream of data waiting.
The open source nature of firmware means that the end user is not locked out of parameter "mapping" process \cite{hunt_importance_2002}, but instead they can directly change what the available data can control.
The sensor data can be used to track velocity and jack displacement both before and after plucks. 

MIDI note on and note off messages give a natural point of entry for such data.
The addition or pressure sensors in the key bed, or on the jack rail would provide  an additional vector of musical expression \cite{hamilton_two_2025}.
In combination with the haptic nature of the interface, such developments to the firmware would give a means to return these transient controls \cite{woolley_musical_2017} back to the performer.

The interface provides a method for retaining kinaesthetic information that would be present in original music played on the harpsichord. 
The interface can equally, and hopefully, be used as conduit for engaging the traditional and the modern, harnessing the kinaesthetics of an old interface, but for new expressions in modern music.

\section{MAGPIE}\label{sec:future-magpie}

Improvements to \textsc{magpie} can be split between those of functionality and those of interface. The core functions of \textsc{magpie} would already provide support for number of research use cases, but further development is needed to enable it as a powerful open source tool for musical acoustics. Similarly, there is functionality that exists but a efficient interface is needed in order to full utilise it. \textsc{magpie} seeks to turn traditionally physics based problems into a problems of engineering and interface is key in order to facilitate that.

Development within \textsc{magpie} can be focussed within two directions. One in functionality and the other in portability. The current iteration is a capable illustrative tool for educational purposes and some specific material measurement scenarios. The application for tone wood still needs to be developed further. Similarly, the tool is available now in the open source language Python, but the current interface relies on the Jupyter notebook system, which comes with its own technical and portability challenges.

Key area for expansion is to translate the source code to a compiled language such as \textsc{C}, \textsc{C++}, and Rust. Such a transition provides more granular control of compiler optimisations, but more interestingly opens potential for deployment. 

The primary bottleneck for development is the eigensolver stage. In M\textsc{atlab} this achieved with \texttt{eigs} and in python the SciPy linear algebra library's \texttt{scipy.sparse.linalg.eigs}. In both cases, these eigensolvers are an interface to the ARPACK library \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998}. For M\textsc{atlab} there is analysis of the target matrix to which the user is not privvy. 
M\textsc{atlab} its own sparse library on top of ARPACK \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992} which obfuscates the process further.
M\textsc{atlab}'s \texttt{eigs} function is not one supported by the software's code generation systems and the function still requires the M\textsc{atlab} runtime if it is to be interfaced with externally. This has implications for portability issues, with development being constrained to those with paid access to the M\textsc{atlab} environment.

ARPACK itself is open source \url{https://github.com/opencollab/arpack-ng} and requires The Basic Linear Algebra Subroutines (BLAS) and Linear Algebra PACKage (LAPACK) \url{http://www.netlib.org/blas/} and \url{http://www.netlib.org/lapack/}

In the case of \textsc{C++}, the relevant technologies available for interfacing with ARPACK library. First is the open source Eigen library for C++ \url{https://gitlab.com/libeigen/eigen} and the Spectra library \url{https://spectralib.org} for eigenvalue problems. Spectra consists of a re-write of ARPACK solvers in C++ allowing for direct native compilation. 


\subsection{Functionality}

What is covered here is development of the core functionality as it is apart of the \textsc{magpie} framework. Such development means the rewriting and current \textsc{magpie} function suite, as well as the addition of further functions. As an open source project, the state of the project is constantly fluid. At time of writing the latest release of the M\textsc{atlab} magpie version is 0.0.2 \cite{magpie_matlab_2025}, which consists of the functionality as discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis}.

\subsubsection{Orthotropic Case}

The grid spacing ($h$) for \textcite{magpie_matlab_2025} is currently shared in both the x and y directions. This has the major implication of the model being most accurate for integer ratio plate dimensions. 
Currently the system is optimum  where the ratio  of plate dimensions $L_x$ and $L_y$ is equal to the ratio of the number of grid points in each dimension $N_x$ and $N_y$. The alternative approach is to choose a grid spacing and truncate $\frac{L_x}{h}$ and $\frac{L_y}{h}$. Shared grid spacing has the effect of the system losing accuracy outside of these very tight constraints. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER Error in isotropic results arising from shared grid spacing in x and y dimensions.}
    \label{fig:grid-error}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:grid-error} illustrates the error that arises from such an approach. The reason for the behaviour of the shared grid spacing plot is that effective plat dimensions are altered. 
This is only feasible for small $N_x$ and $N_y$, since the total grid size $N_{xy}$ increases with the $NxNy$ the system grows exponentially larger. 
For example, the plate in Figure~\ref{fig:grid-error} is of dimensions $L_x$ = 100.1 cm and $L_y$ = 81.1cm a grid size of  $N_x$ = 1001 points and $N_y$ = 811 points would be needed to cancel out the error.
The number of bytes for the sparse biharmonic scales $N_{xy}$ by a factor of 104, with 13 diagonals and double precision. Additional overhead is also required for the eigensolver stage which, with benchmarking, adds a further factor of 50. 
A shared grid spacing would require approximately 4.2 gigabytes of RAM before it would match the accuracy of the independent $h_x, h_y$ grid spacing. By contrast, the final grid size to the rightmost of Figure~\ref{fig:grid-error} comes at a cost of \~118 megabytes of RAM for the sparse biharmonic and eigensolver. Where this is relevant is deployment in resource constrains settings such as part of an embedded system, where RAM and processing requirements come at a financial cost of implementation or in the context of a web browser where overall performance will be much slower compared to a native implementation.

Features such as independent grid spacing, which is implemented as part of an orthotropic plate model, have been merged into the the M\textsc{atlab} library repository of \textsc{magpie}, which  were based on the orthotropic and lumped mass model as described in \textcite{ducceschi_method_2024}. The source is not yet in a format which would be distributable. The same principles in exploring the isotropic case can be applied to the orthotropic case, magpie would be capable of applying the same process to the  elastic constants of a wooden plate, two elastic moduli $E_x$ and $E_y$ and  a shear modulus $G_{xy}$. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER Bracing of applied to the soundboard of a harpsichord.}
    \label{fig:bracing}
\end{figure}


The latest developments have implemented a lumped mass model. The addition of lumped masses to the system allows for modelling of the wooden braces that provide strength to musical instrument soundboards (Figure~\ref{fig:bracing}). Furthermore, lumped masses should also provide a means to compensating for the effects of components such a as accelerometers or exciters which are attached to plate as part of experimental measurement, such as the accelerometer in \textcite{duran_experimentally_2023}. Such a set of features are not best explored through a code interface, but instead can best be leveraged with graphical user interfaces.

\subsection{GUIs}

A graphical user interfaces provide a means to change multiple parameters quickly in a way that is more intuitive for the end user. In addition to intuitive access to a libraries functions, there is also interactions that arise from constraints \cite{wang_artful_2018}.\footnote{``Principle 1.11 Design is constraints which give rise to interactions.''}
\textsc{magpie} as a software library naturally provides a programmatic interface in each of its implementations. Complex, algorithmic iteration over parameters that allow for simulations to be run over long periods without intervention. Providing the user with a huge parameter space is only as powerful as the ability to explore it. There are commonly subtle transition points where small changes in any one parameter will have a dramatic affect on mode shapes or frequencies. 
When the user is constrained, however, to the interesting areas of the parameter space then the system becomes much easier to explore. Likewise, the small adjustments in parameters are isolated to a single mouse interaction which would be otherwise difficult or unwieldy to change and explore if it were attempted to be achieved through the command-line interface or re-compilation of software.

An example of the functionality that emerges from a GUI would be in the presentation of computational results versus those of a real experiment.

On could iterate over a range of values, but there is no intuitive way to start exploring subranges within the larger parameter space; interrupting exploration in one space to tangentially explore another.

The user should be given the flexibility to change slider limits based on the sub-parameter space they are trying to explore. Resolution, or step size, of a graphical slider is limited by the resolution of on which it is displayed. Minute movements are not a pleasant solution to the problem of sub-parameter space exploration. Instead, the user could be allowed to edit the limits directly giving a method to move from a macro level exploration of a parameter zoom into a sub-range of interest (Figure~\ref{fig:slider-subrange}) 

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:jupyter}, an interface for the python version of \textsc{magpie} \cite{magpie_python_2025} has already been created using the Jupyter notebooks development environment.
The Jupyter interface \cite{magpie_jupyter_2025} is quite limited, and allows for only those designs which can easily implemented using the IPywidgets. Functionality such as adjustable range constraints, though technically possible, are more easily implemented.  Similarly, M\textsc{atlab}'s App Designer
\footnote{\url{}}
would provide the same mode switching for end users. The App Designer is constrained to only those elements made available.

M\textsc{atlab} also has deployment constraints as \texttt{eigs} function is not one that is supported by the software's code generation systems and the function still requires the M\textsc{atlab} runtime if it is to be interfaced with externally. Such constraints have implications on the accessibility and maintainability, with practical development being constrained to only those who have paid for access to the M\textsc{atlab} environment.

Neither Jupyter nor M\textsc{atlab}'s App Designer are permissive to all design approaches, but with the implementation of the library in C++ a number of possibilities become available. This section will discuss some potential deployments for a \textsc{magpie} GUI, the technical requirements, and what benefits there are to gained form such development.

\subsubsection{C++ Interface}

Graphical user interfaces are typically platform specific, Cocoa for macOS, win32 for windows and either GTK or QT for Linux operating systems, each of which require platform specific implementation. The JUCE framework therefore provides an appealing solution to target all 3 core operating systems simultaneously, with its cross-platform native wrappings for audio libraries and OpenGL for graphics card rendering. The more graphics orientated, but still open source, bgfx\footnote{\url{https://github.com/bkaradzic/bgfx}} may also be more appropriate given its support of multiple 3D graphics rendering libraries.

If the ultimate goal is provide a tool that is easily accessible and cross-platform, developments within web technology may provide the best solution.

\subsubsection{Web Technology}

Porting \textsc{magpie} to a compiled language like \textsc{C++} open up a number of exciting possibilities. 
Similarity in results to that of M\textsc{atlab} give an indication of a viable plate analysis tool that is not constrained to only those system on which M\textsc{atlab} is supported. Of particular  note is the possibility of deploying \textsc{magpie} as an entirely web based application. Traditional software has typically required some form of installation process, where binaries are compiled for a specific machine architecture. Web technologies do away with part of that necessity by delivering software directly through a browser.
One such technology is Web Assembly (WASM) \url{https://webassembly.org}, which allows for optimised binary code to be executed by a virtual machine in the browser. This is achieved through the emscripten,\footnote{\url{https://emscripten.org}} an open source compiler that enables compilation of \textsc{C} / \textsc{C++} code into WASM. Assembly code capable of more performative execution than the equivalent JavaScript 
\cite{reiser_accelerate_2017}. When combined with the native \textsc{C++} source of Eigen and Spectra, the emscripten compiler can potentially be used to distribute and execute \textsc{magpie} entirely in the browser.
\footnote{Such an application using the Eigen library has already been demonstrated as feasible \url{https://observablehq.com/@rreusser/eigen}}. 
To make a web tool like \textsc{magpie} would traditionally require server architecture in order to execute ARPACK or to host a M\textsc{atlab} runtime. 
In a server / client arrangement the bottlenecks on performance are then tied to the slowest of the network and the computation.
Creating a \textsc{magpie} server would also not be without cost and would also be limited by concurrent usage.

The server and client can be on the same machine, which is exactly the architecture for the Jupyter interface (Section~\ref{sec:magpie-jupyter}).
The extra technical requirements for installation of a local server reintroduces platform specific behaviour and limitations, which the browser client is meant to avoid.
Computational performance in this scenario is also affected compared to an equivalent system using natively compiled binaries.
The alternative for \textsc{magpie} to execute entirely on the client-side would be an implementation of a sparse matrix library and eigensolver entirely within JavaScript, the result of which would be severely limited in performance \cite{lion_investigating_2022}.
\textsc{magpie} would likely see much more utility if development is pursued for an entirely native or entirely web client approach.

The usage of WASM would allow for compilation for both approaches from a single, shared codebase.
It is worth noting that the above web technologies are not supported equally across all browsers and their versions.\footnote{\url{https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/WebAssembly\#browser_compatibility}}
Such limitation of highlight an importance of both open sourcing and version control as multiple systems and deployments can more easily be supported.

WASM addresses the porting of the core \textsc{magpie} to the web browser, but there still remains a question o of interface.
The Jupyter version of \textsc{magpie} (Section~\ref{sec:magpie-jupyter}) demonstrates one potential interface, but this is limited by the communication happening between IPython the renderer for the browser. Breaking away from the constraint of Jupyter allows for direct usage of further browser technology, notably the WebGL and WebGPU \cite{chickerur_webgl_2024} which allow fast 3D drawing within the browser. Figure~\ref{fig:magpie-p5js} shows a prototype example of the first four eigenshapes of a plate above their corresponding `chladni pattern' projected shadows.\footnote{\url{https://mhamilt.github.io/eigenvis/}} 
The plates in Figure~\ref{fig:magpie-p5js} are drawn using the \texttt{p5.js} library which has an interface to WebGL.
\footnote{\url{https://p5js.org/reference/p5/WEBGL/}}

Each plate in comprised of approximately 1.5k grid points, for a total 12k grid points across four displacement and four projected shadows. The interface render at approximately 11 frames per second (FPS). The interface is an early proof of concept, using eigenvectors from the \textsc{c++} version of \textsc{magpie} output as JavaScript arrays. Although the plates are rendered at a low FPS, this would likely be improved by implementing displacement and shading as part of vertex and fragment shaders rather than direct drawing using \texttt{p5.js}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{img/magpie-p5js.png}
    \caption{Prototype interface using WebGL via \texttt{p5.js} to render plate modes.}
    \label{fig:magpie-p5js}
\end{figure}


M\textsc{atlab} has functionality to compile its code to WASM (\url{https://www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/69973-generatejavascriptusingmatlabcoder}). Some functionality such as that in M\textsc{atlab}'s sparse libraries is not currently open to code generation and compilation to a binary outside of the M\textsc{atlab} runtime (list of functions supported: \url{https://uk.mathworks.com/help/referencelist.html?type=function&capability=codegen&listtype=alpha}) and it is unlikely to be in the near future.


A key development for \textsc{magpie} is the addition of an orthotropic model so match more closely that of the quarter-sawn wood as is used for string instrument soundboards. Even further, the addition of braces and lumped masses to the internal system to mimic that of the structural bracing found on physical instruments \cite{ducceschi_open_2024}. 

Presenting the tool kit in an open source well-structure manner should hopefully encourage usage, collaboration and development as the system matures.

\section{Closing Remarks}

This thesis has intended to illustrate the importance of an open methodology as well as demonstrate two use cases for its importance within digital research
When applies to digital conservation, the intention is to show that we must take the same approach to conservation of cultural heritage as we do to the methodologies applied to carry it out. 
Two projects were presented, an interface for conserving the instructional value of musical instrument heritage artefacts and a plate analysis software toolkit. Both projects look to tackle polar ends of the heritage conservation spectrum, the act conservation and the act of engagement with that which is conserved.
There is an ``as above so below'' symmetry to digital cultural heritage, where both the heritage and the digital tools require conservation.
With musical instrument heritage there is the added value of interaction and instruction which are not simply conserved by creating a digital facsimile of that heritage. Instead we must look at alternatives for conserving experience alongside the raw data of dimensions, materials and construction. 


% Both digital and analogue objects rely on physical storage mediums\nocite{Rubio2020}, but the financial and energy cost of preserving something digitally can often be orders of magnitude greater than an `analogue' equivalent\nocite{Cieply2007}.

The open methodology described here has tried to demonstrate the need for careful separation of components into digital repositories in order that each component can be reusable and iterated upon without affecting others.
The keyboard interface is separated into physical mechanical, digital software, and the boundary between both in the form of electronics hardware designed in software. Software itself has been separated into components of data, processing and interface.
Each digital component is stored in a version control repository, whose functions are many. The version control repository is a tool that simultaneously allows for access to older versions of source code, ensuring the ability to access older versions of software, but it can also serve as a record of how ideas are developed. 

Through online git services, the version control repository can also act as the means of software being open source. 
Git repositories are decentralised and a combination of local and cloud service storage provides some degree of parity and preservation, but one this is coincidental and not deliberate.
Services such as GitHub are owned and operated by commercial entities ad should not be depended upon to provide an resolvable DOI and digital archive. 
Relying on web archive projects to keep a record of these repositories is neither deliberate nor reliable \cite{calano_github_2025}.
That responsibility should instead lie with institutes.
Academic institutes do not defer responsibility of storing and distributing academic research literature from the Microsoft library and the same should also be true for research software.

The Zenodo archive operated from CERN provides integration with GitHub, reducing the impedance to archiving digital software and providing a DOI. 
The DOI functions is a similar manner to the ISBN and ISSN, identifying a digital artefact without ambiguity. Enabling software citation through a DOI stresses software's importance, permitting attribution, while facilitating access and version specificity \cite{soito_citations_2016}.
Research to rely on an external service, like Zenodo, is perhaps still an abdication of responsibility. 
Institutes have support for digital archives such as the Edinburgh Research Archive
\footnote{\url{https://era.ed.ac.uk/}}
at the University of Edinburgh and Sistema Biblioteca Atteneo

Academic research is capable of long term usage of software, ARPACK from 1990 \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998} (discussed in Chapter~\ref{}) serves as one example. Aurora (Section~\ref{sec:aurora}) from just five years later is now at risk of no longer being useable without intervention and development. Research software access and permanence should not be a coin toss weighted by the extent of its adoption. Aurora demonstrated this by being both widely adopted and at risk of obsolescence. Instead, a more deliberate methodology is needed for research software to be accessible and reproducible as is the expectation with other aspects of academic research. 

The praxis of such a open methodology does not come without cost labour and time. Researcher are likely already learning a new skill with respect to programming. With the addition of repository organisation, drafting documentation, learning a version control system and archiving development time can be inflated as a researcher orientates themself with these new tools. 

Adopting an open methodology as standard practice lessened these costs as support networks are formed.
It is important to remember that here are no shortcuts in good research and anything worth doing is worth doing well, but acknowledgment of this cost raises an important issue. 
With respect to the humanities, there is still a gap in  digital skills and best practices in data sustainability that needs to be closed \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. Academic institutes will also need to take a proactive approach with digital skills literacy if those in the humanities research are to follow best practice with the respect to digital research.

We do not only have to think of the impact of software sustainability if digital research, but also what the implication is for digital cultural heritage.
When applied to cultural heritage, it is paramount that we approach digital conservation through a critical lens, otherwise we risk active harm to the heritage at stake \cite{rubio_work_2020,schwarz_disk_2006,parry_museums_2010,cieply_afterlife_2007}.
Whatever approach is taken to digital conservation, it should be carried out deliberately, with the consequences of conservation decisions being intentional, not accidental. 
\textcite{barclay_preservation_2005} and \textcite{desilvey_curated_2017} proclaim the same thing We should not ``pickle''\cite{desilvey_curated_2017} the past as that is what we are inclined to do, neither should we idly transition musical instruments across states of currency, conservation, and restoration while neglecting the consequences of those decisions. Better yet, if there is neglect should consequences be damned let it be explicit \cite[p. 75-76]{barclay_preservation_2005}.
Similarly for the digital tools we employ in the process of that conservation. If these digital tools are disposable, then there should be intentionality in the ephemerality. If these tools should be enduring, then steps need to be taken to make sure they endure.
