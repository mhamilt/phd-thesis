\subsubsection{Mechanical Interface}

\paragraph{CAD/CAM processes}
Easing acces to fabrication and permitting iteration on designs

A major limitation across all interfaces discussed in Chapter~\ref{} is the gradient stickers used for the sensor. Although the stickers are printed and cut by machines, allowing for large quantitites to be made, they still need to be applied by hand. In the application process it not uncommon for the graident stickers to be too wide. The short edge of the jacks were roughly similar, at around 4.35 mm, but they were made by manually resulting in variation across the full set. The jacks and the jack-slide are wooden and such prone to expansion and contraction with changes in humidity. This was the case for when the Mark 1 (Sections~\ref{sec:mark-1}) was moved from the laboratory, where many jacks that were freely moving became constricted in their slots. This meant that the gradient stickers either needed trimmed or replaced entirely, with reapplication also necessitating re-calibration. Inspecting and adjusting 98 jacks was a time consuming task and one ripe for optimisations.
The adhesive for the stickers was hi-tack meaning that they were unlikely to fall off. The lifespan of the adhesive is finite, rated to around four years for outdoor use \cite{antalis_coala_2025}. Factoring in that the jacks are moving, creating friction with the inside of the jack slot, will likely mean the lifespan of the sticker adhesive is shorter.

Preferably, the surface for the optical sensors would be intergal to the construction of the jack. Given the small area of a 4.35mm short edge an ~8mm travel, creating a surface of varying reflectance by hand is not feasible. The process would therefore need to be automated or computer aided.
Two immediate options present themselves: the modification of the current jacks or CAD/CAM fabrication of the jacks entirely.

Research into both approaches were undertaken but ultimately a satisfactory result compared to the gradient sticker approach was not found. Both approaches showed promise but were ultimately too unrefined to be applied to the Mark 1.

For modification, a laser cutting approach was tested. The hypothesis was for a speckle pattern to be etched into the side of the jack to alter reflectance. A number of materials were tested including mdf, plywood and hardwood. It was found that the available laser cutters were not able to etch a pattern with enough  differentiation between light and dark points. Both a rasterised and vector approach were attempted and it was found that the marks left by the laser cutter were too faint (Figure~\ref{fig:laser-speckle}. When power was increased, scorching of the material bled into the surrounding area, ruining the gradient from high to low reflectance. 
It is possible that the optical sensor could be wired to an amplifier circuit and tuned to a smaller, more sensitive range, but pursuing this approach would require a major redesign of the electronics system.

Once it was found the laser cutter approach was not viable, a second design was needed. Rather than reflectance from a speckle pattern, the concept returned to the distance application of the optical sensors.

\subsubsection{Sensor Signal Measurement}\label{sec:tia}

Currently the signal of the sensors are in voltage divider configuration using a pull-up resistor and a signal on the sensor board is selected through the use of a multiplexer. The voltage divider works as current is induced through the phototransistor, a voltage is therefore present across the pull-up resistor which can be measured against ground by tapping out a signal between the pull-up resistor and the phototransitor collector. The transistor has an inherent parasitic capacitance, which manifests as a low pass filter \cite{falco_understanding_2023} on the output signal and thus slower response time.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER Basic TIA circuit using a photodiode \cite[Figure 4.22]{horowitz_art_2015}.}
    \label{fig:tia-basic}
\end{figure}


Instead of the pull-up resistor formation a number of transimpedance amplifier (TIA) circuits can be implemented. 
The most basic TIA for phototransistors can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-basic}, where the output voltage $V_{\text{out}}$ is the current flowing from the non-inverting input of the op-amp through the (in this case) photodiode $I_{C}$, scaled by the feedback resistor $R_{f}$.

$$
V{out} = I_{C}R_{div}
$$

As noted in \textcite{horowitz_art_2015}, this circuit should be assumed to include a capacitor in parallel to the feedback resistor, an example of which can be see in \textcite{pardue_near_2013} (Figure~\ref{vb-tia}(b)).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER (a) pull-up resistor configuration of the phototransistor (b) TIA circuit with voltage offset $V_b$. From \cite[Figure 3]{pardue_near_2013}.}
    \label{fig:tia-bias}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{tia-bias} there is a bias voltage $V_{b}$ which increases the voltage between the phototransistor collector and emitter, which in turn should improve the response time of the phototransistor \cite{falco_understanding_2023}. 

$$
V_{out} = V_{b} + I_{C}R_{1}
$$

Rail-to-rail op-amps are those that permits the voltage output to be up to the limits of the supply voltage, where op-amps typically limit before this point. In the case of the biased TIA above, there is a risk that the op-amp will be saturated unless it is rail-to-rail. To bypass this limitation, the output voltage can be biased down by introducing a voltage at the inverting input, which is the configuration in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff} \cite{mcpherson_portable_2013}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER TIA difference amplifier from \textcite[Figure 3]{mcpherson_portable_2013}.}
    \label{fig:tia-diff}
\end{figure}


Voltage output $V_{\text{out}}$ is given as

$$
V_{out} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{cc} + I_{C}R_{f} + \frac{R_f}{R_{in}}V_{cc}(\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} - 1)
$$

With the addition of $R_4$ to bias the output voltage, the op-amp is now in a differential amplifier configuration. 
Typically for a difference amplifier the ratio of resistor for each channel should be equal for optimal common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) \cite{olson_optimizing_2023}. 
Using the convention of Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff}, the rule for would be $\frac{R_5}{R_4} = \frac{R_2}{R_3}$. 
With the addition of a current source in the form of the phototransistor, optimum CMRR is less definite, with $\frac{R_5}{R_4} \approx \frac{R_2}{R_3}$. Given variation in all components, this becomes more of an engineer exercise,  with best noise reduction found through trial and error. Typically a difference amplifier could employ a quad network of resistors \cite[p. 227]{horowitz_art_2015}. The asymmetric biasing of Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff} values means that the four unique, high-tolerance resistor, values are needed. 
Variation across the optical sensors means that the feedback (or load) resistor 
\footnote{$R_5$ in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-diff}, $R_1$ in Figure~\ref{fig:tia-bias}}
may need adjustment across all sensors as was the case in \textcite{pardue_near_2013}.\footnote{``Early on it was noticed that different sensors of the same make yielded significantly different current response even when in the same configurations. With the QRE1113 sensors, we have used load resistors ranging between 12kΩ to 47kΩ to achieve similar range.''}
If multiple optical sensors are to share the same TIA, such as in \textcite{mcpherson_portable_2013}, then the values will always be a best average. A bespoke circuit for each sensor is feasible, but highly impractical. Given the sufficient operation of the current pull-up configuration, the improvement in response time would be sufficient without too much concern about additional noise.
Since op-amps can come in single, dual and quad packages \cite{ti_opa4377_2011}, it may be better use of space allow easier routing if the TIA circuits are located on the controller board close to the power supply, rather than the newly siggested signal boards. 
The decision to use TIA is not a trivial one and will have an impact on the requirements for the overall project.
The design principles of the keyboard (Section~\ref{sec:design-constraints}) taken in hand with the FAIR principles of the open methodology (Section~\ref{sec:open-methods}) informed the design of the electronics system. 
The intention was to demonstrate the creation of an interface using economic electronics, a low technical knowledge barrier without a dependence on specialist facilities.
Components for electronic system could be sourced and assembled using a modestly outfitted university maker space and small scale PCB manufacturing services such as JLCPCB or PCBWay.
\footnote{Although the current design is \textit{just} about practical with prototyping board, the assembly of it would not be enviable.}
Components such as op-amps are not commonly found in THT packaging, but rather in surface-mount device (SMD) format (Figure~\ref{fig:tht-vs-smd}).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{example-image}
    \caption{PLACEHOLDER The QRE1113 optical sensor in both THT and SMD formats with a XX for scale.}
    \label{fig:tht-vs-smd}
\end{figure}


THT parts are more permissible to the prototyping process, allowing for ```spur of the moment'' \cite[p. 268]{horowitz_art_2015} circuit wiring. By contrast, SMD components are soldered directly to `pads' on the surface of a PCB. SMD are much smaller compared to their THT equivalent, saving on space and are typically much more energy efficient with reduced inductances \cite[p. 268]{horowitz_art_2015}. As a result, SMD components are much less permissive to the prototyping process compared to THT.
With SMD parts comes a technological burden, requiring access to equipment such as re-flow ovens and hot air rework station.
Soldering these components is possible with hand soldering tools, but the process becomes much less practical.
Alternatively, the burden can shifted to the PCB manufacturing services to detriment of cost, lead times and the ability to experiment.
Ten PCBs can potentially be wired ten different ways, but this can only be done if one has the facilities to achieve it.
The circuits for the Mark 1 and Mark 2 keyboards represent the crossing point where the THT-over-SMD principle applied to the design will need to be compromised in order for improvements to be enacted.

A move to entirely SMD components would be expected to reduce the footprint of the PCB by half \cite{prasad_surface_1997} allowing the space to be used for a refined op-amp design \cite{mcpherson_portable_2013} or the ability to install in more constrained spaces or flush mounted to surface as one edge of the PCB could be cleared of potruding components.

The sensor system would benefit from single piece mounting bracket which would allow for much more simplified installation. Given the open source nature of the project the advantages come from the ability to modify the design for a bespoke application.

\subsubsection{Microcontroller}

If the bottleneck of sensor response time is addressed, the second area for improvement is within the analogue-to-digital conversion of the signal. The firmware for the interface \cite{hamilton_firmware_2025} was designed to be portable to most microcontrollers that use the Arduino integrated development environment (IDE), in particular the Nano family of microcontrollers.
\footnote{It should be all, but there are always unforeseen exceptions.} 
The motivation behind this decision was two-fold, to permit experiments with multiple microcontrollers without the need for rewriting the source code and to further the accessibility of the project under the FAIR principles. 
The Arduino IDE is open source, but it has a track record within electronics education and as such presents a lower technical skill barrier compared to other microcontroller development platforms such as mbed, Platform.io or ESP-IDF.
The constraint of using platform independent libraries restricted the usage of high-precision timers within the firmware.
For the Mark 1, the central programme loop executes as quickly as possible, the result of which is jitter around the base latency for generating MIDI data. 
To counter act jitter a high-precision, typically hardware, timer would need to be used. The timer either sets a flag to trigger, or executes directly, the routine for reading sensor data and sending MIDI data.
Timer interrupts and high precision timer logic are chipset and microcontroller specific. 
Programming the hardware timer Atmels ATMega family of microcontrollers \cite{gammon_interrupts_2012} is very different from Noridic's nRF52 microcontrollers \cite{wolff_realtime_2019}, such as is present on the Nano 33 BLE.
Commitment to a hardware timer interface or API immediately restricts the types microcontrollers which can be used. Timer code is also not easily ported without sacrificing functionality or adding unnecessary complexity.
Like the case of the TIA in Section~\ref{sec:tia}, improving the jitter will require a compromise the design and methodology principles of project. Once again it is compromise in accessibility and reproducibility. Not all microarchitectures are equal and neither will they be readily available long into the future. 
Restricting the microcontroller architecture comes at the risk of obsolescence. This is another reason why documentation and archiving of firmware is important as it can serve as a record of functionality.
The function of the sensor system at this point is fixed and archived. 
Refining the operation of the firmware through architecture specific code makes it much more likely that it would need to be rewritten for another architecture in the future.
The nRF52 of the Nano 33 BLE is proven to be more than capable of the task been a part of successful implementation in the Mark 1 keyboard with sub-optimal firmware.
Continuing to use the Nano 33 BLE, the firmware would be best improved through use of either Nordic's
\texttt{NRF_TIMERx} API \cite{nordic_timer_2023,nordic_timer_2025} or the \texttt{mbed::Ticker} API \cite{mbed_ticker_2023}. As a hardware timer, the \texttt{NRF_TIMERx}  would provide the greatest temporal resolution and lowest jitter, but manual register setu would prove a much lower eas of use compared to the software \textsc{c++} and object orientated \texttt{mbed::Ticker} API. 
Best practice dictates that the timer interrupt should be as short as possible.
\footnote{``Conventional wisdom among real-time programmers is that ISRs
should be as short as possible: clear the interrupt, maybe transfer a
few bytes of data, and exit.'' \cite{klingensmith_using_2019}}
By following this rule and to avoiding missed cycles, the latency using a timer may be slightly longer than without. As shown in \textcite{jack_effect_2016}, the critical factor is jitter so long as the baseline latency is short enough.

Another approach would be to use a different microcontroller entirely. 
A test was carried out using an Arduino Nano ESP32 which is a NORA-W106, a module containing an ESP32-S3. 
The benefit of this microcontroller is that it would allow the microcontroller to be swapped and the rest of the PCB designs would be unaffected.
Some research was carried during the course of the project using an ESP32 programmed with the Espressif development framework ESP-IDF.
The same logic as the original firmware was programmed and the latency was reduced to below 10 ms. ESP-IDF allows access to functionality of the ESP32 including regular processing of an internal audio buffer that is not exposed in the Arduino IDE. 
It is possible that the 10 ms latency could be improved upon with appropriate optimisation, but there would be a cost in the form of development time. 
Consideration would need to be made on the time to port existing source code for the I2C FRAM chip or fall back on a simpler storage option.

\section{MAGPIE}

Improvements to \textsc{magpie} can be split between those of functionality and those of interface. The core functions of \textsc{magpie} would already provide support for number of research use cases, but further development is needed to enable it as a powerful open source tool for musical acoustics. Similarly, there is functionality that exists but a efficient interface is needed in order to full utilise it. \textsc{magpie} seeks to turn traditionally physics based problems into a problems of engineering and interface is key in order to facilitate that.

Development within \textsc{MAGPIE} can be focussed within two directions. One in functionality and the other in portability. The current iteration is a capable illustrative tool for educational purposes and some specific material measurement scenarios. The application for tone wood still needs to be developed further. Similarly, the tool is available now in the open source language Python, but the current interface relies on the Jupyter notebook system, which comes with its own technical and portability challenges.

Key area for expansion is to translate the source code to a compiled language such as \textsc{C}, \textsc{C++}, and Rust. Such a transition provides more granular control of compiler optimisations, but more interestingly opens potential for deployment. 

The primary bottleneck for development is the eigensolver stage. In MATLAB this achieved with \texttt{eigs} and in python the SciPy linear algebra library's \texttt{scipy.sparse.linalg.eigs}. In both cases, these eigensolvers are an interface to the ARPACK library \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998}. For MATLAB there is analysis of the target matrix to which the user is not privvy. 
MATLAB its own sparse library on top of ARPACK \cite{gilbert_sparse_1992} which obfuscates the process further.
MATLAB's \texttt{eigs} function is not one supported by the software's code generation systems and the function still requires the MATLAB runtime if it is to be interfaced with externally. This has implications for portability issues, with development being constrained to those with paid access to the MATLAB environment.

ARPACK itself is open source \url{https://github.com/opencollab/arpack-ng} and requires The Basic Linear Algebra Subroutines (BLAS) and Linear Algebra PACKage (LAPACK) \url{http://www.netlib.org/blas/} and \url{http://www.netlib.org/lapack/}

In the case of \textsc{C++}, the relevant technologies available for interfacing with ARPACK library. First is the open source Eigen library for C++ \url{https://gitlab.com/libeigen/eigen} and the Spectra library \url{https://spectralib.org} for eigenvalue problems. Spectra consists of a re-write of ARPACK solvers in C++ allowing for direct native compilation.

\subsubsection{Web Technology}

Porting \textsc{magpie} to a compiled language like \textsc{C++} open up a number of exciting possibliities. 
Similarity in results to that of \textsc{matlab} give an indication of a viable plate analysis tool that is not constrained to only those system on which \textsc{matlab} is supported. Of particular  note is the possibility of deploying \textsc{magpie} as an entirely web based application. Traditional software has typically required some form of installation process, where binaries are compiled for a specific machine architecture. Web technologies do away with part of that necessity by delivering software directly through a browser.
One such technology is Web Assembly (WASM) \url{https://webassembly.org}, which allows for optimised binary code to be executed by a virtual machine in the browser. This is achieved through the emscripten,\footnote{\url{https://emscripten.org}} an open source compiler that enables compilation of \textsc{C} / \textsc{C++} code into WASM. Assembly code capable of more performative execution than the equivalent JavaScript 
\cite{reiser_accelerate_2017}. When combined with the native \textsc{C++} source of Eigen and Spectra, the emscripten compiler can potentially be used to distribute and execute \textsc{magpie} entirely in the browser.
\footnote{Such an application using the Eigen library has already been demonstrated as feasible \url{https://observablehq.com/@rreusser/eigen}}. 
To make a web tool like \textsc{magpie} would traditionally require server architecture in order to execute ARPACK or to host a MATLAB runtime. 
In a server / client arrangement the bottlenecks on performance are then tied to the slowest of the network and the computation.
Creating a \textsc{magpie} server would also not be without cost and would also be limited by concurrent usage.
The server and client can be on the same machine, which is exactly the architecture for the Jupyter interface (Section~\ref{sec:magpie-jupyter}).
The extra technical requirements for installation of a local server reintroduces platform specific behaviour and limitations, which the browser client is meant to avoid.
Computational performance in this scenario is also affected compared to an equivalent system using natively compiled binaries.
The alternative for \textsc{magpie} to execute entirely on the client-side would be an implementation of a sparse matrix library and eigensolver entirely within JavaScript, the result of which would be severely limited in performance \cite{lion_investigating_2022}.
\textsc{magpie} would likely see much more utility if development is pursued for an entirely native or entirely web client approach.
The usage of WASM would allow for compilation for both approaches from a single, shared codebase.
It is worth noting that the above web technologies are not supported equally across all browsers and their versions.\footnote{\url{https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/WebAssembly\#browser_compatibility}}
Such limitation of highlight an importance of both open sourcing and version control as multiple systems and deployments can more easily be supported.

WASM addresses the porting of the core \textsc{magpie} to the web browser, but there still remains a question o of interface.
The Jupyter version of \textsc{magpie} (Section~\ref{sec:magpie-jupyter}) demonstrates one potential interface, but this is limited by the communication happening between IPython the renderer for the browser. Breaking away from the constraint of Jupyter allows for direct usage of further browser technology, notably the WebGL and WebGPU \cite{chickerur_webgl_2024} which allow fast 3D drawing within the browser. Figure~\ref{fig:magpie-p5js} shows a prototype example of the first four eigenshapes of a plate above their corresponding `chladni pattern' projected shadows.\footnote{\url{https://mhamilt.github.io/eigenvis/}} 
The plates in Figure~\ref{fig:magpie-p5js} are drawn using the \texttt{p5.js} library which has an interface to WebGL.
\footnote{\url{https://p5js.org/reference/p5/WEBGL/}}
Each plate in comprised of approximately 1.5k grid points, for a total 12k grid points across four displacement and four projected shadows. The interface render at approximately 11 frames per second (FPS). The interface is an early proof of concept, using eigenvectors from the \textsc{c++} version of \textsc{magpie} output as JavaScript arrays. Although the plates are rendered at a low FPS, this would likely be improved by implementing displacement and shading as part of vertex and fragment shaders rather than direct drawing using \texttt{p5.js}.

\paragraph{ITEMM OpenLab / Existing Tools}

\textsc{magpie} is by no means the first tool to provide the functionality described here and neither is it the only ``free'' tool available to do so. The Institut Technologique Européen des Métiers de la Musique (ITEMM) provides a suite of tools for luthiers via its online service known as ITEMM Open Lab\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com}}. ITEMM OpenLab provides resources for thos in the field of musical instrument making, hosting datasets of materials, 3D models and CAD files for instrument parts and tools. Most relevent, is that ITEMM OpenLab also provides a suit of tools for calculating wood properties intended to provide luthiers accesss to toools and processes used in the ITEMM Laboratories.

Available tools include those for calculating, resonance frequencies of soundboards and easels, string simulation strings under tension, and wood properties. The \textit{Rigidité de plaques} tool provides a means calculating the elastic constants of wood, .\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/calculateur-mecanique/rigidite-de-plaques-1/}} using the methodology laid out by \textcite{mcintyre_measuring_1988}. 
The open availability of the tools at ITEMM OpenLab are adminrable, but there are some inherent restrictions.
The \textit{Rigidité de plaques}, like the other calculators on OpenLab, is free to use however the tools themselves are accessible only after registering for the service and authenticating. 
The ``free as in freedom'' distinction made by \textcite{stallman_why_2007} would not classify \textit{Rigidité de plaques} as a FLOSS tool. 
The source is ``open source'' in the sense that, given it is software delivered via a web browser, the source code is downloaded onto the user's computer, but it is not easily identified nor is it documented.
ITEMM OpenLab meets a technical definition of free open source software, but sadly one where the free and open are lowercase.
One can, after time, identify the relevant files,
\footnote{calculations take pace in \url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/wp-content/plugins/ITEMM-OpenLab-tools-main/js/p_w_script.js} and an \texttt{olt} utility class \url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/wp-content/plugins/ITEMM-OpenLab-tools-main/js/olt_class.js} used across calculators, both of which available without authentication.} 
but they are missing documentation, attribution and an open source licence. 
The legal notice\footnote{\url{https://www.openlab-itemm.com/mentions-legals/}} on ITEMM OpenLab covers the 3D objects, but this does not naturally extend to the software \cite{cc_can_2025}. Instead, the software calculators should have an explicit open source licence attached to them so that there terms of use are clearer. From ITEMM OpenLab's desciprtive text and legal notice it is clear that attribution and accessibility are core principles, both of which would be better served by a clear citation system \cite{soito_citations_2016}, especially if these tools are to be used for further use.
The 'open first' design methodology of \textsc{magpie} was in part to avoid issues in lack of clarity for reuse.
\textcite{stallman_floss_2021} differentiates between free open source software (FOSS) and free / libre open source  software (FLOSS). Such a distinction is to make clear the meaning of `free' given its ambiguity of free as in ``free speech'' or as ``free beer'' \cite{stallman_why_2007}. FLOSS is a distinction of open source software where ``free'' carries both meanings.
Web tools for plate analysis may be extant, but there is still a gap for a truly free and open tool where the `free' and `open' are not lowercase.

\section{Closing Remarks}


This thesis has intended to illustrate the importance of an open methodology as well as demonstrate two use cases for its importance within digital research
When applies to digital conservation, the intention is to show that we must take the same approach to conservation of cultural heritage as we do to the methodologies applied to carry it out. 
There is an ``as above so below'' symmetry to digital cultural heritage, where the both heritage and the digital tools require conservation.
With musical instrument heritage there is the added value of interaction and instruction which are not simply conserved by create a digital facsimile of the heritage. Instead we must look at alternatives for conserving experience alongside the raw data of dimensions, materials and construction. 

The open methodology described here has tried to demonstrate the need for careful separation of components into digital repositories in order that each component can be reusable and iterated upon without affecting others.
The keyboard interface is separated into physical mechanical, digital software, and the boundary between both in the form of electronics hardware designed in software. Software itself has been separated into components of data, processing and interface.
Each digital component is stored in a version control repository, whose functions are many. The version control repository is a tool that simultaneously allows for access to older versions of source code, ensuring the ability to access older versions of software, but it can also serve as a record of how ideas are developed. 
% Rafael Bombelli (1526–1572) letters to Cardano are a cornerstone for complex mathematics. The Goldbach’s Conjecture comes from letters between Euler and Christian Goldbach. These are not published works, but rather correspondence was typically ephemeral. For the digital age, ideas and concepts ar not necessarily worked out on paper, but rather in code. In a digital form, these records are much more precarious, requiring technology to render them and with great ease all record can be eradicated entirely. The version control repository goes some way to keeping a record of code in the same manner that historic diaries and correspondance have offered insight into published work.
Through online git services, the version control repository can also act as the means of software being open source. 
Git repositories are decentralised and a combination of local and cloud service storage provides some degree of parity and preservation, but one this is coincidental and not deliberate.
Services such as GitHub are owned and operated by commercial entities ad should not be depended upon to provide an resolvable DOI and digital archive. 
Relying on web archive projects to keep a record of these repositories is neither deliberate nor reliable \cite{calano_github_2025}.
That responsibility should instead lie with institutes.
Academic institutes do not defer responsibility of storing and distributing academic research literature from the Microsoft library and the same should also be true for research software.

The Zenodo archive operated from CERN provides integration with GitHub, reducing the impedance to archiving digital software and providing a DOI. 

The DOI functions is a similar manner to the ISBN and ISSN, identifying a digital artefact without ambiguity. Enabling software citation through a DOI stresses software's importance, permitting attribution, while facilitating access and version specificity \cite{soito_citations_2016}.
Research to rely on an external service, like Zenodo, is perhaps still an abdication of responsibility. 
Institutes have support for digital archives such as the Edinburgh Research Archive
\footnote{\url{https://era.ed.ac.uk/}}
at the University of Edinburgh and Sistema Biblioteca Atteneo
\footnote{\url{https://sba.unibo.it/en/almadl/almadl-services/assigning-a-doi}}
at the Università di Bologna, but these services are still tailored toward traditional publications and datasets rather than software repositories.
Both of these institutes already host their own online git services.\footnote{UoE ECDF \url{git.ecdf.ed.ac.uk} and multiple departmental GitLab services at Unibo.} 
The ideal scenario would be where these services are simply connected together allowing institutes to take responsibility of both version control and it's archiving.
Academic research is capable of long term usage of software, ARPACK from 1990 \cite{lehoucq_arpack_1998} (discussed in Chapter~\ref{}) serves as one example. Aurora (Section~\ref{sec:aurora}) from just five years later is now at risk of no longer being useable without intervention and development. Research software access and permanence should not be a coin toss weighted by the extent of its adoption. Aurora demonstrated this by being both widely adopted and at risk of obsolescence. Instead, a more deliberate methodology is needed for research software to be accessible and reproducible as is the expectation with other aspects of academic research. 

The praxis of such a open methodology does not come without cost labour and time. Researcher are likely already learning a new skill with respect to programming. With the addition of repository organisation, drafting documentation, learning a version control system and archiving development time can be inflated as a researcher orientates themself with these new tools. 
Adopting an open methodology as standard practice lessened these costs as support networks are formed.
It is important to remember that here are no shortcuts in good research and anything worth doing is worth doing well, but acknowledgment of this cost raises an important issue. 
With respect to the humanities, there is still a gap in  digital skills and best practices in data sustainability that needs to be closed \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. Academic institutes will also need to take a proactive approach with digital skills literacy if those in the humanities research are to follow best practice with the respect to digital research.
